# Chapter 18 — Uxies II

*Draft 2, new. Second of four Uxies chapters. Third person present. The two
years she stopped going, and what it cost. Sits after Chapter 17 — Fridan has
just failed to be believed by his sister; she has spent forty-two years failing
to be believed by hers.*

---

Malde comes on the fourth day with a folder.

That is not like her. Malde arrives with food, or with news, or with one of the children and an apology about the child; she has never in eleven years arrived with a document, and Uxies sees it under her arm from the top of the stairs and knows the shape of the evening before her sister has got her coat off.

"Don't," says Malde. "Don't do the face."

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the entire face."

She puts the folder on the table between the two cups and does not open it.

---

What is in it is a place.

Not a clinic — Malde is not stupid and would not survive suggesting a clinic, and they both know exactly how that conversation went in the year Uxies was twenty-three. It is a residential programme on the coast, eleven days, described in the folder as *reintegrative* and in Malde's account as "a break, that's all, a break where people talk to you."

Uxies reads two pages of it while the tea goes cold.

The photographs are of a low building with a lot of glass, and in every one of them there are groups. Groups eating. Groups walking. A group in a circle of chairs with their hands moving, mid-argument, which on Earth 2 is the picture you use to sell contentment.

"They put you in a room with people," she says.

"That's rather the idea."

"For eleven days."

"Ux—"

"There is not one photograph in this document," Uxies says, "of a person on their own."

Malde is quiet for a second. Then she says, with the particular flat honesty that is the reason Uxies loves her: "No. There wouldn't be. Because that's the thing they're treating."

---

Here is what happened when Uxies was nineteen, which neither of them has said out loud in thirty-two years and which is sitting in the room with them now, occupying most of it.

She stopped going to the yard.

Not because anyone forbade it. Nobody has ever forbidden it; it is not the kind of family and not the kind of world. She stopped because at nineteen she came home one evening and found her sister sitting on the floor of the hall crying, in the abandoned, undignified way that people cry when they have been waiting a long time to do it — twenty-one years old, hands over her face, saying *I don't know what to do about you, I don't know what to do* — and Uxies stood in the doorway and understood that the walking had a cost and that she was not the one paying it.

So she stopped. She was good about it. She did not stop dramatically or make a performance of the stopping; she simply did not go, and when the fourth day came she stayed in and let Malde talk, and when the sixth came she found something else to do.

It lasted two years and one month.

---

The first month is nothing. She is restless and she sleeps badly and she puts it down to restlessness and bad sleep.

The third month she stops being able to work.

That is the word she uses for it, then and now, and it is the wrong word: she does not stop being able to *work*, she stops being able to do the thing she is for. She sits in the calibration hall in front of an instrument that is out by a fraction, and she waits for it to tell her, the way it has told her since she was twenty-two — and there is nothing. Not a wrong answer. An absence where the answer arrives. She sits with one device for six hours and hands it on unfinished, and the man she hands it to is kind about it, which is worse, and she goes into a stairwell and puts her forehead on the cold wall.

By the eighth month she is being examined. They find, in order: nothing, nothing, a vitamin deficiency that is corrected without effect, and finally a diagnosis which is not a diagnosis but a category, and which translates roughly as *the tiredness of the unsociable* — a recognised condition on Earth 2, treated with company.

She is prescribed company.

She takes it. That is the part she will not forgive herself for, though she has never been able to explain to anybody what the offence was. She goes to the gatherings. She sits in the circles of chairs. She argues, badly, on cue, because arguing is what health looks like there, and every session she gets a little better at the imitation and a little further from the middle of herself.

In the twentieth month she cannot lift her arm above her shoulder without deciding to.

In the twenty-second she stops speaking for four days. Not from despair — she is very clear about this afterwards, with the two physicians who ask. There is simply nothing that requires saying, and the effort of finding something is greater than the return, and she lies in a room in the noise of a city of eleven million with her eyes open and waits for it to be a different day.

Malde comes on the fourth day of that, and sits on the end of the bed, and does not talk.

That is the thing Uxies has never been able to hold against her. In the worst week of her life her sister did the one correct thing anybody has ever done for her, instinctively, without being told, and has never once known that she did it.

---

On the eleventh day of the twenty-fifth month, Uxies gets up and walks to the yard.

She does not decide to. She has thought about it a great deal since and the honest account is that she is on the freight road before she has any theory about where she is going, and that some part of her that has been very patient for two years has simply taken the controls.

It takes forty minutes. Her legs are not what they were.

She goes under the west span, along the road, through the gap in the fence — and the last forty metres happen, the thinning, the sound coming down and down and then being gone —

and she stands in the middle of a flat open place with stacks in it and a fast sky, and something in the exact centre of her, which has been folded up small for two years and one month, opens out.

She is not aware of crying until it is well underway.

She goes home. She sleeps eleven hours. She goes back to the hall in the morning and calibrates four instruments before midday and the fourth one tells her where it is lying inside ninety seconds, and she sits with her hands flat on the bench for a while afterwards.

Nobody connects the two things.

That is the whole of it, and it is the sentence she has been carrying for thirty-two years: she recovered in a fortnight, completely, from a two-year illness with a diagnosis and a prescription and eleven months of company — and not one person, including her, including every physician who saw her, ever put it beside the walking.

Because the walking was the symptom. Everyone knew that. It had been established for years.

---

"I did the eleven days," she says.

Malde looks up. "What?"

"Your folder. The programme, the circles of chairs. I did it. It was called something else then and it was nine months, not eleven days, and I did all of it." Uxies closes the folder and puts her hand flat on the cover. "And at the end of it I could not lift my arm without deciding to."

"That wasn't — Ux, that was the *illness*—"

"Yes," says Uxies. "That's what everyone said."

The city argues outside the window. Somewhere below, two people are being right at each other with real enthusiasm, and a third has joined in.

"I'm not doing it again," Uxies says. "I want to say that once, tonight, properly, so we don't have to do the years of it. I'm not going. I'm not going to a coast. I'm going to keep walking out there, and I'm going to keep not being able to tell you why, and you are going to keep worrying, and that is — " she turns her cup a quarter turn on the table, which is Malde's own gesture and neither of them notices " — I think that is just the arrangement. I think that's what we've got."

Malde is crying. She does it the way she does everything, without apology and at full volume, and Uxies moves round the table and sits beside her and puts an arm across her shoulders, which is not a thing she is good at and which she does anyway.

"I hate it," Malde says, into her hands.

"I know."

"You're not — " she pulls a breath " — you're not broken, I've never once thought you were broken, I want you to know that I have never—"

"Malde," says Uxies. "You've thought it every day for thirty-two years. It's all right. So have I."

---

Later, at the door, Malde stops with her coat half on.

"Can I ask you one thing? And you can tell me to get lost."

"Go on."

"What's it *like*. Out there. Not why — I've stopped asking why." She is not looking at Uxies; she is looking at the coat sleeve. "Just what it's like. In the yard. When you're standing in it."

Uxies thinks about it for a long time.

She has never been asked this. In forty-two years, across two physicians, one programme, a partner, and a sister who has cried about it twice, nobody has asked her what it is *like*. They have asked what it is for, and what it does, and whether she can stop, and how she would feel if she could not go — but not one of them has asked what happens in there.

"It's like listening," she says at last.

"To what?"

"That's the bit I don't have." She shrugs, slightly. "There's no sound in it. I'm not hearing anything, I want to be clear, I'm not — it isn't voices. It's the *shape* of listening. Leaning towards something with your whole self and never once finding out what."

Malde puts her coat on properly.

"For forty-two years," she says.

"Yes."

"And it never answers."

"No," says Uxies. "It never answers."

She says it without any particular weight, because it is simply the condition of her life and has been since she was nine, and there is no version of the sentence that carries what it costs.

Malde nods, and does up the last button, and goes out into the argument.
