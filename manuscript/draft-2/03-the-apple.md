# Chapter 3 — The apple

*Draft 2. Second of two from Draft 1's Chapter 1. Roadmap 2. First person
present. Ends Act 1's first movement — the run is stopped and he walks out.*

---

I search the second floor for what feels like an hour and might be four minutes.

There is no clock anywhere in that building that agrees with any other. I check three, early on, in different rooms, and after the third I stop checking, because the discrepancy is not small and interesting — it is large and it does not resolve, and I have already understood that a man who spends the night trying to establish what time it is will find nothing else.

The rooms give onto each other in ways that do not reconcile.

I want to be precise about this, because it is the first thing about that building that is *wrong* in a way I cannot file under wealth or theatre.

I go through a study into a bedroom. From the bedroom, a door into a bathroom — small, black and white tile, no window. From the bathroom there is a second door, which I take, and it puts me back into the study.

Not a study. *The* study. The same one: the same green shade on the lamp, the same two books left out on the desk, the same position of the chair I moved.

But I have come in through the wrong wall. The door I originally entered by is on my left, closed, where it should be. The door I have just come through is behind me, in the wall opposite the window, and when I went out of that study four minutes earlier there was no door in that wall. There was a bookcase, and I had looked behind it.

I stand in the middle of the room and work through it twice, patiently, the way you check an arithmetic error.

Then I stop, because I have noticed something more important than the impossibility, which is that I am not frightened by it. I am *irritated* by it. I have registered a room that does not obey the rules of rooms, and my response has been the response of a man whose route has been closed for roadworks.

I accept it and I go on searching. Arguing with it would cost time I do not have.

That, in the end, is the whole of what they did to me. Not one moment of it involved making me believe something impossible. It involved making the impossible not worth the argument.

---

So I search.

I open drawers. There are things in the drawers — candles, a corkscrew, three keys on a loop, a folded card table cloth — and none of it is what I am looking for, and I know that before my hand has finished moving.

I put my arm down the back of a radiator and find dust and a dead moth.

I lift the lid of a window seat and find a stack of folded curtains, and I put my hand all the way down the side of them, right to the bottom, and there is nothing, and I hold that position for a second longer than I need to because the position is the same one I will be in when I find it, and some part of me has started rehearsing.

The thing I am looking for is small. I know that. I do not know what it looks like, and this ought to be an insurmountable problem and is not one at all, because I also know that I will recognise it.

That is simply part of the arrangement. It arrived with the rest of it, in the warm room, along with my own name.

---

Twice, people pass the doorway while I am working, and both times they slow down.

The second one stops.

She is younger than most of them — thirty-five, forty — with her hair pinned up and her coat open at last, and she stands in the doorway and watches me go through the curtains, and when I look up she does not pretend she was not looking.

"You're very thorough," she says.

"Is that against the rules?"

She considers this seriously, which is worse than if she had laughed.

"No," she says. "It's just unusual. Most people look where they've been told to look."

I am kneeling on a window seat with my arm in a stack of curtains, and there is a version of that night in which I ask the obvious question, and I do not know what that version looks like because I have never been able to imagine it.

What I say is: "And where's that?"

Her expression changes very slightly. Something goes out of it.

"Ah," she says.

Just that. And then, after a moment, in a completely different tone — lighter, kinder, the tone you use on somebody in a hospital corridor: "You want to be careful how thorough you are. It gets noticed."

"By who?"

"Mm," she says, and goes.

I stay where I am, holding a curtain, and I do not follow her and I do not call after her.

*Told by whom* is a question I do not ask, because asking it out loud would be admitting I have not been told anything — and some instinct much older than this building says that admitting that in this building would end my run.

I have thought about that instinct a great deal. It was not mine.

---

The clue, when it comes, is not a clue in any way I could defend afterwards.

I am in a room on the third floor with a long table in it. Dark wood, unpolished, the length of a small hall. Nine chairs down one side and eight down the other, which I count twice, and the missing chair is not anywhere in the room.

On the table there is a bowl.

In the bowl there are three apples, and one of them is on its side.

That is all it is. That is the entire event. Three apples in a bowl and one of them lying down, and the instant I see it something in my chest goes *there* — not a thought, not a deduction, a physical occurrence, like a hand closing.

I stand in the doorway and feel my pulse in my throat.

---

It is not the apple.

I check. I go to the table and I check the bowl and I check under the bowl and I lift all three and put my hand into the bottom of it and feel the cold waxy skins, and there is nothing there but fruit. I turn the apple upright and nothing happens. I put it back on its side and nothing happens.

The apple means nothing. I would say that under oath.

But the *feeling* means something, and the feeling is a bearing.

I understand that standing at the table with an apple in my hand: the certainty has narrowed. All night it has been a general condition, a weather I was walking around inside. Now it has an edge. It is stronger when I face one way than when I face another — the way a pain narrows when you press near it.

I put the apple down and I turn slowly on the spot in an empty room with a bowl of fruit, like a man looking for a signal.

North wall.

Or through it.

There is a dust-shadow on the table, I notice — a clean ring in the film of dust about the size of a saucer, a little way from the bowl, where something round has stood for a long time and been moved.

I do not write that down, afterwards. It does not occur to me to. It comes back to me eleven weeks later, in a meeting, and I have to put my hand flat on a desk.

---

I am reaching for the handle of the door in the north wall when the lights change.

They do not go out. They go *down* — all of them, everywhere in the building at once, by perhaps a fifth. Enough that the room dims and the window stops being a window and becomes a mirror, and I see myself standing in the middle of it with my hand out.

Then a voice, from nowhere in particular, pleasant and unhurried, the way a station tells you a platform has changed:

"The run at this location is now concluded. Thank you."

That is all. No name. No apology. Nothing that could be argued with.

I stand with my hand on the door.

*Now?* — and I hear how it would sound before I have finished thinking it. *Now, when I have it, when it is through this wall, when I am four feet away?*

Because I know they are already walking. I have not heard a door and I have not heard a voice, and I know, with the flat certainty of everything else in that building, that thirty people in coats have turned and started for the stairs without breaking stride — because the announcement told them something it did not tell me, and because they have all heard that voice before.

I open the door anyway.

There is a landing behind it, and a tall window, and a stairwell going down.

There is no object. There is no bowl and no table and no north wall.

And there is no feeling.

The bearing is gone. Not faded — gone, cleanly, the way a light goes when a switch is thrown, and the absence of it is so total that for a moment I cannot remember what having it was like.

Behind me somebody says, kindly, "You'll want to come down. They don't wait."

---

They gather us in the entrance hall.

The doors stand open onto the night and the cold comes in over the threshold in a slow sheet, and the people in coats are grateful for their coats at last. Nobody is talking much. Two women near the door are comparing something on a phone with the particular boredom of people at the end of an evening.

Cars are arriving outside. Not taxis. Long, dark, patient cars, arriving in an order that nobody is directing, each one stopping at the foot of the steps for exactly as long as it takes.

The very old woman goes first. Two people help her down and she does not look back at the building.

The man with the ring finds me while I am standing at the edge of it.

He looks almost sorry.

"You were close," he says.

"How would you know that?"

"Because they stopped it." He says it the way you would explain rain. "They don't stop a run for the ones who are lost."

I turn to look at him properly, and for the first time all night I want something from one of these people badly enough to ask for it straight.

"What happens now?"

"Now?" He seems surprised by the question. "Now you go home and you sleep it off, and in a while somebody gets in touch, and you say yes or you say no." He shrugs, and it is a small movement inside a large coat. "Most people say yes. That's the whole of it, really. Nobody's ever had to make anybody do anything."

He steps down onto the gravel. A door is opened for him by nobody I can see.

"Bad luck," he says, without turning round, and he is gone.

---

I stand in the doorway of a building I could not draw, at an hour I could not name, in a city I have not once looked at properly.

And for the first time all night the certainty is not silent.

It has moved. It has gone *ahead* of me — out through the open doors and down the steps and off to the left along a street I have never seen — and it pulls, the way a hand pulls when you are still deciding to walk.

There is another building.

I do not know where. I do not know how far, or what is in it, or why a thing that was four feet away behind a wall on the third floor is now somewhere out in a city at whatever hour this is.

I know I am going.

I go down the steps into the cold, and the cars go on arriving behind me, and I do not get into any of them.

Nobody stops me.
