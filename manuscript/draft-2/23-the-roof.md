# Chapter 23 — The roof

*Draft 2. Second of two from Draft 1's Chapter 9. Roadmap 20–21. Third person
past. Zalian's dialogue is deliberately not expanded — she has one line here
and it is the only one she gets.*

---

"I don't know your name," he said. "I've never known it. I'm going to say this anyway, because you can hear it, and I've been told you can, and I've spent four months finding out that's true."

The wind took it. Nothing came back.

"In August something used me. It came in while I was asleep and it built a place for me and walked me through it, and it did that because there is a piece of something in me that isn't from you." He was steady. He was surprised at how steady. "And the thing I picked up at the end of it wasn't a prize. It was a machine, and it was pointed at *you*."

Below him a car door closed. Somebody was going to work.

"They aren't after me. They have never been after me. They're getting something out of you, a night at a time, out of people who wake up with nothing — and you can't see it, because the only place you look properly is the one place they worked out how to stand."

He took a breath and it went most of the way down.

"There have been others. Hundreds. There's a woman in Halverton with a note in her own handwriting she's kept for nineteen years and she doesn't know why—"

And she was there.

---

There was no arrival.

He would say that afterwards to the only person who ever asked him properly, and he would say it badly, because the language available is all built for things that come from somewhere. She was not present, and then she was present, at the same size as everything — the way weight is present in a bridge.

Warm.

That was the first thing, still, after four months and everything he had learned and the two columns in the notebook. Not kind. Warm the way a room is warm: not doing it at him, simply its condition.

Something in his chest broke open at it with an eagerness so total and so undignified that he was ashamed of it before it had finished happening. Four months of talking to kitchens. Four months of *please*. And one second of the actual thing, and every part of him went toward it like a dog at a gate.

He had one second of it.

Then she looked at him.

---

It was not the same as being spoken to, and he had no preparation for it whatsoever.

It was not attention in the human sense — an eye turned, a face lifted, the small social weight of being regarded. It was more like being a page in good light.

He was open. Not violated; nothing was forced and nothing rummaged. Simply *legible* — all at once, all of him, without sequence. The boy on the bicycle and the fortnight in November and the flat grey shortage that food did not touch. Four stitches on a cuff. A cushion plumped before he arrived. The lie he told on a doorstep in Halverton and the way he had delivered it with his hands out of his pockets.

And the two columns. Both of them. Read together, without emphasis, neither weighted above the other.

He stood on a roof in the wind with his hands on the parapet and let it happen, because there is no mechanism in a human body for doing anything else — and because, and this is the part he never told anybody, for about two seconds it was the best thing that had ever happened to him.

To be entirely known. To have every single thing about him held in something's regard at once, including the worst of it, and not to have to *say* any of it. No account to give. No case to build. No twenty-two pages and a woman across a kitchen table asking him to please just talk to her.

For two seconds he was not alone in the way he had been alone since the twenty-sixth of August.

Then the reading arrived at the place in him that was small, and old, and quiet.

And she stopped.

---

He felt the stop. It had a texture.

It was the sensation of a very large thing arriving at data it has no compartment for — not shock, nothing so animal as shock. A halt. The way a wheel halts on a stone: not damaged, not deciding, simply no longer able to continue the motion it was making.

And then something else came through and past him, and it was the nearest thing to an emotion he ever detected in her.

He spent years failing to name it. He tried *dismay*, which was too small, and *revulsion*, which was wrong and which he had wanted for a while because it was survivable. He settled in the end for the closest available word, which was **grief**, and he knew it was wrong the whole time, and he went on using it because there was nothing else.

The wind came across the roof.

She said: **"You are not the one I was speaking to."**

That was all.

---

He got out one syllable — not a word; the beginning of *wait* — and she was gone.

And the going was not like the silence.

The silence had been absence. A room with nobody in it, which is a thing that can end; you can stand in an empty room and shout and the shouting is still the kind of act that a person in the next room might hear. That is what the four months had been. He had thought of them as the worst thing that could happen and they had been, all along, a door standing open in a house where nobody was home.

This was the door being closed.

He felt it close. He felt the specific, deliberate, unhurried *shutting* of something that had been open his entire life without his ever knowing it was there — and the roof he was standing on afterwards was a different roof, in a world he had never actually been in before and would now be in permanently.

---

He stayed up there until it got light.

At some point he shouted. He was aware of doing it and made no attempt to stop, and what he shouted was not dignified and was mostly her, or the absence where a name for her should have been. There was one whole ugly minute of *I was trying to tell you*, which he could still hear four years later and which he never wrote down.

And the wind took all of it, and it went nowhere, and he understood that it went nowhere in a completely new way — because for four months his words had been going somewhere and being ignored, and now there was simply no channel for them to travel in at all.

He was shouting into physics.

He sat down eventually with his back against the fan housing.

The city came on. Lights. The first buses — the 14, he noted, running four minutes late out of the depot, which meant somebody had not fixed the thing he had flagged in July. The man in the kitchen opposite was gone. A plane went over very high with the sun on it before there was any sun on the ground.

---

And Fridan, who forgot nothing, went back over the last ten minutes with the flat total availability that had been installed in him in August.

And found the thing at the bottom of it.

She had come at the first sentence.

Not the third, where the substance was. Not at *they're getting something out of you*. At the first — at *I don't know your name, I've never known it* — she had already been there. The arriving had taken no time at all. Less than a breath. Faster than a phone is answered by somebody sitting beside it.

She had been right there.

For four months he had talked to kitchens and ceilings and the inside of a shower. He had gone up onto this same roof at five in the morning and said *please*. He had built a matrix, with conditions down the side. And every single word had landed, and she had been near enough to answer inside a second, and had not.

He put his head back against the housing and laughed once, and it came out wrong.

---

Anser came that night, and Fridan let it come.

**"You spoke."**

"Yes."

**"On a roof, I imagine."**

"Yes."

A pause. Not one of hers.

**"Then that's done,"** Anser said — and there was nothing at all in it. No reproach. No I-told-you. None of the small satisfactions a person is entitled to when they turn out to have been right. **"You'll want to sleep. Not tonight; you won't manage it. In a few days. Tell me when you're ready and we'll start on what's actually in front of you."**

Fridan lay in the dark with his mouth shut and waited.

He waited about eleven seconds. And then he understood what he was waiting for, and that it was not going to come.

Anser had not asked what she said.

Four months of a thing that answered every question the instant it was asked, that had never once been incurious, that had opened its very first conversation with *ask in whatever order you like* — and a god had appeared to a man on a roof and spoken to him for the only time in his life, and it had not asked what she said.

*File it,* Fridan thought, very carefully, in the part of him that could not be heard by anything. *Don't turn it over. Don't look at it. File it and go on.*

"Give me a week," he said aloud.

**"A week,"** Anser agreed.

---

He lay awake until the room went grey, working the problem, because working the problem was the only structure left standing.

She could hear what was spoken. That had been true on a footbridge in August and true on a roof that morning, and it was the only law of the universe he had ever verified personally.

She could not hear him now. Not the words, not the shouting, not anything — he had felt the mechanism close and he did not need it explained to him.

So there was a message that had to reach her. The only one that mattered. The one that would let her see the hole in the floor of her own creation.

And it had to be *said*, out loud, because that is the only kind of thing she gets.

And the one man alive who knew what it was had just had his mouth taken away.

He put it to himself in the plainest words he could find, which is how he put everything to himself from that morning onward:

*How do you say something to a god who has stopped being able to hear you?*
