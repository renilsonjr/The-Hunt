# Chapter 16 — Vera Lund

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 7. Roadmap 15. Third person past.*

---

The name came on the third night, and it came without being asked for, which Fridan understood much later was the whole technique.

**"Vera Lund,"** Anser said. **"Fourteen Cowl Street, Halverton. She ran nineteen years ago and she woke with nothing, which is what the process is supposed to produce. She is sixty-eight and she is well. You want to see one, and I would rather you saw one than spent a year deciding whether I invented them."**

"You're sending me to a stranger's house."

**"I'm telling you an address. You'll decide the rest on the train."**

He lay awake afterwards until it got light, doing the arithmetic on himself. Eleven days he had spent on roofs and in bathrooms saying *are you there* into the dark, and now something else had a name and gave him instructions and he was already looking up the trains.

He saw it clearly. He watched himself see it.

And then he did the thing that mattered, which was that he went and checked.

---

That is why he went. Not obedience — he has been accused of obedience about that Saturday and he has never accepted the charge.

*Vera Lund of fourteen Cowl Street, Halverton* was the first falsifiable thing anybody had said to him since the twenty-sixth of August.

Everything else he had been given was unfalsifiable by construction. A voice on a footbridge. A building that could not be found because it was not in a city that had a name. A memory nobody could inspect. Even the man in the green jacket had dissolved into a bus-network control that came back negative and proved nothing.

But a woman at an address either exists or does not.

He found her in eleven minutes. The electoral roll had a V. Lund at that address, and had had one for thirty-one years. There was a school newsletter from a decade earlier, digitised badly, thanking *Mrs V. Lund, school secretary*, on her retirement, for three decades of service and for knowing where everything was. There was an obituary notice for a Roy Lund of Cowl Street, Halverton, four years old.

He sat at the kitchen table at two in the morning and looked at a low-resolution photograph of a woman holding a bunch of flowers in a school hall.

It was the first time since August that anything anybody had told him had turned out to be checkable, and it had checked.

He understood even then that this was exactly what it would feel like if he were being managed. He went anyway, and he would go again.

---

Halverton took two hours and eleven minutes and was a town of the kind that has one of everything.

Cowl Street ran up from the church. Number fourteen was in a terrace of six, red door, a small square of garden with a rose in it cut back hard for the winter and a bay tree in a pot that had not been watered lately.

He had prepared a lie on the train and he was ashamed of how good it was.

He told her he was compiling accounts of unusual sleep for a project on the long-term recall of dreams; that she had come up through a survey she would have filled in years ago and forgotten about; that it was entirely voluntary and he could leave at any point.

Every part of it was designed to be checkable and none of it was true. He had even built in the detail about the forgotten survey, because a stranger on a doorstep who cannot say how he got your name is a stranger you close the door on.

He delivered all of it standing on a step with his hands out of his pockets.

Vera Lund listened to the whole thing with her head tipped, and then said:

"You've come from the city on a Saturday for this?"

"Yes."

"Well then you'd better come in, because I've got a cake going stale."

---

The front room was warm and crowded in the way of a house where one person has lived for a long time.

Photographs on the sideboard, three generations of them, and one of a much younger Vera on a beach with her hair blown sideways, laughing at whoever was holding the camera. A chair with a depression in it that was clearly hers, angled at the window. And the other chair, which was clearly for guests, with a cushion on it that had been plumped some time before he arrived.

He noticed the cushion and had to look at the carpet for a second.

She talked while the kettle went, and it was not filler; it was the fluent, well-worn account of a life given by somebody who does not often get asked for it. She had been a school secretary for thirty-one years. She had liked it. She had known where everything was, which she said was ninety per cent of the job and the only part anybody ever thanked you for.

Her husband had died in the spring of a year she named without ceremony.

Her son was in the west and rang on Sundays. Her daughter was forty minutes away and rang less, which she reported as a fact of physics rather than a complaint.

"They're good," she said. "They're both good. It's just that nobody tells you the last bit is quiet."

---

She brought the tea in on a tray and put it down on the low table by the window.

On the table there was a case for reading glasses.

Brown leather, hard-shell, the seam split a little at one end. And on the front, near the clasp, the leather had gone pale and smooth in a patch about the size of a thumb.

Fridan looked at it for what he was fairly sure was too long.

Nineteen years ago somebody had modelled a woman's life closely enough to put that on a table in a furnished flat in a building that did not exist, in a city with no name, on the third floor, behind a door most people could not see.

He had stood over it. He had picked it up — the hinge had been stiff, and the case had been empty — and he had put it back down at a slight angle and thought, at the time, that the angle did not matter.

"That's an old thing," said Vera Lund, following his eyes. "It was my mother's."

"It's nice."

"It's not nice, it's split. But there we are."

She poured the tea.

---

He asked his questions in the order he had written them out, because they were plausible questions and because holding the paper gave his hands something to do.

Did she remember her dreams generally. Not much, and less than she used to.

Had she ever kept a dream diary. Good heavens, no.

Was there any period she would describe as unusual.

"Well," she said. "There's the fortnight."

And Fridan, with a cup halfway up, said, "The fortnight."

"Oh, it's nothing. It's a family joke — Roy used to bring it out at dinner and I'd want to go through the floor." She settled back, and she was enjoying herself, and the enjoyment was the worst thing in the room. "Nineteen years ago, thereabouts. Late summer. I slept like the *dead* for about two weeks. Went to bed at ten, woke at seven, out like a light, every single night. And I never slept like that. I was a four-times-a-night woman all my life, always was, still am."

"And after the fortnight?"

"Back to normal. Exactly as before." She laughed. "Roy said I'd been away. That was his joke, for years. He'd say, *she went away for a fortnight in the August and she's never told me where.*"

The clock on the sideboard went on with what it was doing.

"How did you feel," Fridan said. "During it. Rested?"

She thought about that properly, which he had not expected.

"No," she said. "That's the odd bit, now you say it. I was tired the whole fortnight. Tired the way you are after you've done something."

---

He asked one more question, and it was not on the paper, and it was the only one he actually needed.

"In that fortnight — or around it — did you ever have a feeling of being *sure* about something? Certain. About anything at all, for no reason you could give."

Vera Lund looked at him with mild amusement.

"Me? No, love." She patted the arm of her chair. "I have never in my whole life been sure of anything. Ask my daughter. It's my great failing — Roy used to order for me in restaurants."

"Never."

"Not once," she said, comfortably, and drank her tea.

And Fridan sat in a warm front room in Halverton and understood something that had not been in any of it.

She had gone into that building with nothing.

No pull. No bearing. No hand closing in the chest, no *there*, no compass — a sixty-eight-year-old school secretary who had never been certain of anything in her life had been walked into a building the size of a district and told to find a small object, and she had searched it for a fortnight, and she had got tired the way you are after you have done something, and she had got *further than anyone had ever got*.

By exhaustion. Floor by floor, room by room, in the dark, for two weeks, guessing.

That was the wear-path. That was what had worn it: not four hundred people who knew where to go, but four hundred people who did not, converging on the far end of a corridor because they had been everywhere else first.

---

"Only the note," she said.

"Sorry?"

"You asked if there was anything else about that time. There's the note."

She got up and went to the sideboard and opened the second drawer down without hesitating, which told him it had been moved from house to house and kept in the second drawer down for nineteen years.

It was a piece of paper about the size of a postcard, torn along one edge from a larger sheet, folded and unfolded enough times that the fold had gone soft.

"I found this on the kitchen table," she said, "on a Tuesday morning in that fortnight, in my own handwriting, and I have no more idea what it means than the man in the moon."

She handed it to him.

Two small marks side by side. And underneath, a little apart, a third.

Below that, in a hand that was recognisably hers from the note by the telephone and was also not quite hers — pressed too hard, the letters not joined where she joined them everywhere else — four words:

*it was on a shelf*

---

Fridan sat in a warm front room in Halverton with a piece of paper in his hand and understood that he was going to be sick, and that being sick would require an explanation, and so he was not sick.

"Do you know," said Vera comfortably, "I kept it because it frightened me. Isn't that silly? Not the words. Just — finding my own writing that I couldn't remember doing. You feel like there's someone else in the house."

"Yes," Fridan said.

"I asked Roy if he'd written it as a joke and he hadn't, and he wasn't a good enough liar to have got away with it." She took the paper back, refolded it along its own fold, put it in the drawer and closed it. "There. That's my unusual sleep. I hope it's some use to your project."

Then she looked up at him, and something in her face changed, and she said in a different voice:

"Are you all right, love? You've gone very white."

---

And here is the thing Fridan carried out of that house and never put down.

He had every piece. He could have told her what she had done, and where she had walked, and that she had been *good* at it — better than good, that she had got to a shelf, nineteen years ago, and had stood in a corridor two metres from a door she could not see and had written down four true words on a kitchen table the next morning without knowing why.

He could have given a sixty-eight-year-old woman in Halverton the missing fortnight of her life. He could have told her why her husband's joke had made her want to go through the floor for nineteen years, which was that some part of her had known it was not a joke.

He could not say one word of it.

Not because he did not trust her. Because the room was in the world, and the world was full of air, and anything he said aloud in it went straight to something that had been listening to him for three weeks and had not answered once, and he did not know what it would do with the knowledge, and neither did Anser, and *the honest position is that neither of us should find out by accident.*

So he said: "Long train. I should get back for the four-forty."

She saw him to the door. She said it had been nice to have somebody in the house, and that he was to tell his project she said hello.

She stood on the step in her cardigan with her arms folded against the cold, and she waved when he got to the end of Cowl Street.

He made himself turn round and wave back.
