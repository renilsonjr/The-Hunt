# Chapter 17 — The platform

*Draft 2. Second of two from Draft 1's Chapter 7. Roadmap 16. Third person past.
The threat is not violence. It is sleep.*

---

He was on the platform at Halverton for twenty minutes.

It was a two-platform station with a shelter on each side and a bridge between them, and there were four other people waiting, and the light had that particular late-afternoon quality of a place you are leaving and will not think about again.

He was not thinking about the man in Halverton, because there was no man in Halverton. He was thinking about a rose cut back hard for the winter, and about the fact that she had plumped a cushion.

The man came and stood a polite distance away — four feet, near enough to speak to, far enough to be nothing — and looked up the line the way everybody looks up the line.

After a while he said, without turning:

"That's a long way to come for an afternoon."

---

He was perhaps forty. Grey coat, good shoes, a canvas bag over one shoulder. His face had nothing in it at all.

Fridan looked at him and had, instantly and uselessly, every available detail: a small scar through one eyebrow, a wedding ring worn thin on the inside edge, a paperback in the outside pocket of the bag with a supermarket receipt used as a bookmark about two-thirds through.

"Do I know you?"

"No," the man said.

He said it without weight, and then he did something Fridan had not been prepared for by anything in the preceding month: he turned round properly, and faced him, and spoke to him like a person.

"I want to be straightforward with you," he said, "because I think you've had a few weeks of people not being. I'm not going to hurt you. I couldn't tell you how, honestly — it's not that sort of arrangement. I'm asking you to stop."

"Stop what."

"Going to houses." The man shifted the bag on his shoulder. "You've been to one. There'll be a temptation to go to more. It doesn't do them any good, and I'd like you to think about that part rather than the part about me."

---

Fridan stood on a platform in a town two hours from home and found that his mouth was dry.

"Does she know?"

"No."

"Will she?"

"No." The man looked genuinely bothered by the question, in the way of somebody who has been asked to confirm something distasteful about his own job. "That's rather the point of it. She had a nice afternoon. Somebody came about her dreams and she got the cake out."

"And if I'd told her?"

"Then she'd have had that instead," the man said. "For however long she's got. That's the bit I meant about it not doing them any good."

The rails ticked.

"Who do you work for?"

And the man said, "I don't know."

---

He said it plainly, without any of the weight it should have had, in the voice of a man being asked about his pension arrangements.

"I've never met anybody," he said. "It's an account. I get told a name and a town and I go, and I write down what I saw, and it pays extremely well, and it has been, up until fairly recently, the easiest money in the world."

"You've never asked."

"I asked at the start." He shrugged. "You get a very polite non-answer and a raise. After that you'd have to be a particular sort of person to keep going, and I'm not, and I've got two at university." He glanced sideways. "I've been doing this eleven years. Eleven years of writing down what time somebody got home. You're the first one that's ever looked back at me."

"The first."

"The first," said the man. "In eleven years. And I want to be clear that I'm not being dramatic — I mean that most of the people I write down don't have any reason to look at me, because they don't know anything's happening. You do. That's why we're talking. Somebody rang me up about you, which has never happened either."

---

"What happens to the ones who don't know?"

"Nothing," the man said. "Genuinely. That's not a comfort, it's just the fact. They live their lives. I file a note four times a year that says they're still at the same address and their bins go out on a Thursday."

"For how long?"

"For as long as I've been on them." A pause. "There's a woman on my list I've been writing down since before my eldest was born."

Fridan thought about a fifty-one-year file that he did not yet know existed, and did not think about it, because he had no way to.

"You're not the only one on this," the man went on. "I want to be honest about that too. Some of it isn't people. Some of it comes in through the sleeping, and that's the bit you want to be careful about, because you can't lock that."

"How do you know that?"

"Because it's in the terms," he said. "There's a paragraph. I've read it about four hundred times and I still couldn't tell you what it means, but it's there, and it's not about people like me."

---

The signal changed somewhere up the line.

Fridan said, "What happens if I don't stop?"

And the man thought about it.

He actually thought — Fridan watched him do it, and it took several seconds, and there was no menace anywhere in it, which was why it went in like a nail.

"Nothing happens," he said at last. "Nobody comes for you. That's not how it's done and I don't think anyone knows how to do it that way any more." He picked up his bag properly as the train came into sight at the far end of the platform. "You'll just get tired, is all. Everybody does. That's not a threat, it's — look, it's the shape of it. You'll get tired, and one night you'll sleep properly, the way you used to. And whatever it is you've got, you'll have it a bit less."

"And then?"

"And then a bit less than that."

He stepped back to let Fridan on first, which was somehow the most frightening thing that had happened all day.

"Get some rest," he said. "I mean that in the ordinary way."

---

Fridan rode two hours and eleven minutes back to Nor Yesey with his forehead against the glass and the fields going by in the dark.

He did not open the notebook. He did not write anything down for two days, which had not happened since August.

Because he had spent a month building a picture of what he was up against, and the picture had been an *adversary*: something with intent, something that could be outmanoeuvred, something you could run a control on with a bus timetable. He had been frightened of it in the way you are frightened of an opponent, which is a frightened that has a shape and a limit and something to push against.

And a man with two children at university had just explained, kindly, on a platform, that there was no opponent.

They did not need to find him. He was not hiding. They did not need to threaten him, or follow him, or take anything from him, or persuade him of anything at all.

They only had to wait for the one thing every living human being does, every night, without deciding to.

---

*You should rest*, the woman in the grey suit had said, in a warm flat furnished from a book he had creased on a train, at four in the morning, twice, without adjusting a single word.

He had thought, for a month, that the terrible thing about that night was what had been done to him while he slept.

Sitting on the 6:12 out of Halverton with his coat still on, he understood that the terrible thing was much simpler and that he had been carrying it since the moment he woke:

he was a man who was going to have to sleep, every night, for the rest of his life, in a house with a door he could not lock.

And that there were four hundred others, and that one of them was a woman in Halverton who had waved to him from a step, and that nobody was ever going to tell her.
