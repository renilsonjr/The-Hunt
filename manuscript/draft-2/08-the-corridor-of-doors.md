# Chapter 8 — The corridor of doors

*Draft 2. First of three from Draft 1's Chapter 4, which carried four roadmap
chapters at 490 words each. Roadmap 7. First person present.*

---

I am four steps up the flight to three when I understand what she said to me.

*Third floor's nice. Quiet.*

I stop with my hand on the banister. Because a woman in a grey suit who has spent the whole night not answering questions has just told me where to go, and she said it the way you say a thing you are allowed to say — lightly, in passing, on her way up — and there is exactly one arrangement in which that sentence is possible.

Either she was guessing.

She does not guess. I have watched her people guess all night: hands sliding along wallpaper, arms up to the shoulder in ceiling voids, a woman on a chair reaching for a bulb that was not there. They are bad at it in a specific way, the way you are bad at something you have been told to do without being told why.

Or she is being told the same things I am.

I stand on the stairs with the fire door settling above me and I say it out loud, because out loud is the only way anything gets heard in this building:

"Is she with you?"

The stairwell hums.

"Are you on their side?"

And the nothing that comes back is not the nothing of an empty room. I have had four hours of her tonight and I know the shape of her attention the way you know whether someone is still on the phone. She is there. She has heard the question and she is declining it, and the declining has a texture — patient, entirely without guilt, the way a person waits out a child's tantrum in a shop.

I climb the rest of the flight.

I am aware, distantly, the way you are aware of a stone in your shoe when you are late, that a reasonable man would go down instead of up. That the door onto the street has been unlocked all night and nobody has ever once stood between me and it.

I keep noticing that nothing stops me. I keep failing to draw the conclusion.

---

The third floor is apartments.

Not offices. Not the function rooms of the lower floors with their stacked chairs and their smell of polish. Apartments — a corridor of them, both sides, running the length of the building to a window at the far end that shows nothing but the dark and a smear of my own reflection.

Sixteen doors. I count them twice.

None of them has a number.

They are my door. Not similar to my door: the same door, the same brushed handle at the same height, the same faint scuff at the bottom edge where a foot has pushed it open sixteen times over, and standing at the top of the corridor looking down it I have the sensation — which never entirely leaves me afterwards, in any corridor, in any building, for the rest of my life — of looking into a mirror at a mirror.

The first one I try opens.

---

It is my apartment.

Short hall. Kitchen off it. Two sofas at right angles that nobody has sat on. Lamps burning at four in the morning for nobody, and the bedroom door ajar, and the corner of a made bed.

The bowl on the counter is empty.

On the low table by the window there is a pair of reading glasses in a case.

Brown leather, hard-shell. The seam is split a little at one end. And near the clasp the leather has gone pale and smooth in a patch about the size of a thumb, the way a thing goes when somebody has held it in the same grip several thousand times.

I stand over it for a long time.

They aren't mine. I have never owned a pair of glasses in my life. I pick the case up — it is light, and empty, and the hinge is stiff — and I put it back down at exactly the angle it was, and I go out, and I close the door, and I stand in the corridor with my hand still on the handle.

Then I open the next one.

---

Sofas. Lamps. Bowl.

On the table, a wristwatch with a cracked face. It has stopped at twenty past two. The strap is worn through in one hole and not in any of the others.

*Next.*

A child's drawing, folded twice, the folds gone soft and furry the way paper goes when it has been opened and refolded for years. A house, a sun in the corner with rays coming off it, four figures of descending size. It has been kept.

*Next.*

A paperback in a language I cannot read. Not one I recognise the shape of. Spine creased white in one place, hard, the way you crease a book when you force it flat one-handed.

I put that one down faster than the others.

*Next.*

A key on a ring with a green plastic tag, and nothing else on the ring, which means it is a spare, which means somebody gave it to somebody.

*Next.*

A hairbrush. There is hair in it.

*Next.*

Nothing on the table at all — and I stand in that one longer than any of the others, turning slowly, because I know by now what these rooms are and an empty table is worse than any object in the world. Then I see it on the arm of the sofa: a pair of glasses, different ones, cheap ones, folded, one arm slightly bent.

*Next.*

A train ticket.

---

I pick it up because it is paper and paper has writing on it.

It is a single, second class, from a station I have never heard of to another station I have never heard of, and it is dated the ninth of July.

Six weeks ago.

I stand in a furnished flat on the third floor of a building that is bigger inside than out, holding a train ticket that is six weeks old, and something in the arithmetic of the night comes apart in my hands.

I had been carrying an idea without noticing I was carrying it — that whatever this was, it was old. That the wear in things, the child's drawing gone soft at the folds, the stopped watch, all of it belonged to some long-ago period of the arrangement, and that I had walked into the ruins of something.

Six weeks.

There was a man or a woman in this building in July. They came up out of a lobby with somebody in a grey suit and were shown a flat and told to rest, and they had a train ticket in their pocket from a journey they had actually taken, and somebody put a copy of it on that table so the room would feel like theirs.

And then they went home. Or they didn't.

I put the ticket back down. My hands are not steady and I make a small mess of it, and I have to square it up on the table, twice, before I can leave.

---

I open eleven of the sixteen.

I do not open the last five, and the reason is not courage or the lack of it. It is that at door eleven I find a mug on the table, a plain one, with a chip out of the rim at the near edge, in the exact place a right-handed person's mouth would go — and I understand that I am about to spend the rest of the night going through the small kept objects of people I will never meet, one after another, like a man going along a row of graves reading names, and that there is a limit and I have reached it.

I come out and I shut the door and I put my back against the wall of the corridor and go down onto my heels with my hands over my head.

I stay there for a while. My legs have gone. I have been awake for — I do not know. There is no clock in this building that agrees with any other, and no window that shows anything but night, and I have been down nine floors and up three and along corridors that do not reconcile with the outside of the building I came into.

Sixteen doors. On one floor. Of one building.

*They have done this before.*

Not once. Not twice, not as a rehearsal, not as some earlier version of the thing that went wrong and taught them how to do it properly. There is a room down that corridor with a stopped watch laid out on a table by a window, in a flat furnished out of a stranger's life to make them comfortable while they were used, and there is another with a child's drawing in it, and another with a ticket from July.

Every one of them was walked in off a street they had never seen.

Every one of them was told they were early, and shown a kitchen, and told *you should rest.*

And every single one of them, I would bet my life on it, stood in a warm empty flat at four in the morning with their heart going, looking at some small object out of their own history, and thought: *how strange. How strange, that they know me.*

---

I get up eventually because the floor is cold.

And standing there in the corridor with my hand flat on the wallpaper, steadying myself, the last piece arrives — the one that had been waiting behind all the others for its turn, and that I would give a great deal now to have never worked out at all, because working it out is the reason I did not go down the stairs and out of the front door and into the night while I still could.

Sixteen rooms. Sixteen people, at least, on this floor alone. Hundreds, probably, across all the floors and all the years and all the buildings there must be.

*And not one of them found it.*

I know that the way I know everything in this place, completely and without being able to say when I started.

Because it is still here.

It is still in this building, four hundred people later, on a floor a woman in a grey suit told me to go to — and the certainty in my chest, the thing that has been pulling at me since the bowl of apples, the thing I have privately believed all night was the finest and strangest fact of my life, has just turned over and shown me its other face.

They did not bring me here to compete with those people.

They brought me here because those people failed.

I look down the corridor at the window with the dark in it and my own shape standing in the middle of it, sixteen doors away, very small.

And I say, out loud, to nobody, in a voice I do not entirely recognise:

"All right. Where is it."
