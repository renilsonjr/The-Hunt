# Chapter 13 — Eleven days

*Draft 2. Second of two from Draft 1's Chapter 5, taking Draft 1's Chapter 6
opening with it. Roadmap 12. Third person past.*

---

He started talking to her that afternoon.

It began reasonably. He was washing a cup, and he said, out loud, in the tone of a man testing a light switch, "Are you there?"

The tap ran. The building did its afternoon noises.

"I know you only get it when I say it," he said. "So I'm saying it."

Nothing.

He put the cup on the rack and dried his hands and stood with the towel in them for a while. Then he tried the words that had worked — exactly, in the order they had worked, because he had them, because he had everything:

"I don't know where to go."

It had taken her no time at all on the footbridge. **I know**, she had said, before he had finished being afraid.

The fridge cycled on.

---

Over the next four days he was methodical about it, because being methodical is what a frightened man does with his hands.

He built a matrix. He was not proud of this afterwards and he did not take it out of the notebook, but he built one: conditions down the side, attempts across the top, a column for the exact words used.

He tried in the dark, lying flat, because that was closest to the conditions of the night.

He tried in the shower with the water loud, because the rail had been loud in its way.

He tried at four in the morning, which was the hour of the second building. He set an alarm for it twice, and sat up in bed in the cold and spoke clearly into a room with the lamp off, and both times went back to sleep at about five out of sheer physical necessity and hated himself for it.

He tried the phrasings. He tried the plain question and the formal one; he tried it as a demand and then, on the third day, as a request. He tried saying her lines back to her — *look at the line*, *you are doing this correctly* — on the theory that a system might respond to its own vocabulary.

He went up onto the roof of his own building at five one morning and stood at the parapet in the wind, because she had first spoken to him on top of a building. He had a theory about that for about two days. The theory was wrong.

He said please. He was not proud of that either and he wrote it down anyway, because by then he had started keeping the record honest as a matter of discipline, on the grounds that a record you have edited is not evidence of anything.

---

On the fourth day he worked out that he had no name for her.

He was in the middle of an attempt when it stopped him — mid-sentence, standing in his own kitchen at eleven at night — because the sentence had needed a name in it and there was not one.

In an entire night she had not given him one. And he had not asked.

He sat down on the floor with his back against the cupboard and went through it. Every human being who has ever tried to reach anything of that kind has had at least a name to put in the sentence. That is the minimum equipment. People have had nothing else — no doctrine, no building, no instruction — and they have had a name, and the name is the thing that makes the speaking possible at all.

He had been calling her *she* since the footbridge on the strength of nothing but the shape of a voice.

He did not even have a pronoun she had agreed to.

That was the first day he understood, properly, in his body rather than as a proposition, that he had never known who he was talking to.

---

And she had never lied to him.

He kept arriving back at that from every direction, and it did not help. It made it worse.

She had said *that is your route*, and it had been. She had said *there is a gate; it is not locked tonight*, and it had not been. She had said *go over it at the post, not at the panel*, and the panel would have folded. She had said *they are expecting you*, and five people had been standing in a lobby at three in the morning, waiting, exactly as described.

Every single thing she had said had been true.

And laid end to end, every true thing had been an *instruction*.

She had told him where to go, and when to go over a fence, and which way to take a bridge, and that he was doing it correctly — and she had not, in one entire night, told him a single fact about the world he was standing in.

He had asked her what it looked like and she had said *you will know it*.

He had asked her if she would still be here after and she had said *take it*.

He got a paper notebook by then; the phone had started to feel like the wrong instrument. He wrote the two of them out, one under the other, and looked at them for a long time.

*A thing that cannot lie*, he wrote — and crossed it out, because he did not know that.

He wrote: *a thing that did not lie.*

Then he wrote: *and did not answer.*

---

There were other things and he catalogued them without knowing what he was cataloguing.

His arm was fine. There was nothing to see. But three or four times a day the hair went up along it, wrist to elbow, for no reason and in no cold, and settled again — a field under a passing shadow.

He stopped being interested in food and did not lose weight, and then began to.

And once, on the Thursday, sitting at the kitchen table with a pen in his hand and the notebook open, he became aware that the room was very quiet — and understood, after a moment, that the quiet was inside him, and that he had not taken a breath for some considerable time.

He did not know how long. That was the part he came back to.

He had not been *holding* his breath. Holding it is work; you feel the work, the pressure builds and the body starts making its case. He had simply stopped, the way you stop tapping a foot, with no more sense of lack than a man who has stopped tapping a foot.

He breathed in, deliberately, and his body accepted it politely, the way you accept a drink you did not want.

He wrote in the notebook: *ask a doctor.* He underlined it. He put a box around it.

He did not ask a doctor, then or ever, and when he tried later to reconstruct why, the honest answer was that some part of him had already decided it did not want a test whose result could be *there is nothing wrong with you*.

---

On the eleventh day he wrote down the last thing anybody said in that room.

He did it at the kitchen table, in the afternoon, with the parking structure going about its business outside the window. He wrote:

*When I picked it up, a woman's voice behind me said thank you.*

He looked at it.

Everything else in the document was something that had happened *to* him. That one line was the only sentence in seven pages in which somebody else got something.

They had walked him in off a street. They had put him in a flat furnished from a life they had modelled closely enough to get the crease in a book and to know that the table by the door should be empty. They had told him to rest, and let him not rest, and stood in corridors asking him what he was looking for.

And at the exact moment he had his hand in the middle of the only thing he had ever wanted, a woman had stood in a doorway and been *grateful*.

Not to him. He had gone over the two words perhaps two hundred times by then — the pitch of them, where the stress fell, the small rising note at the end. She had not been thanking him.

She had said it the way a person says it into a phone at the end of a call that has gone well.

---

By then he had not slept properly for eleven days.

It was not insomnia. Insomnia is a thing that happens to a man. This was a decision, taken at about half past midnight and re-taken at two and again at four, and defended each time with a reason he would not have said out loud to anybody:

that sleep was not a room he had ever been alone in, and he had only recently found that out.

So he watched things. He worked. He let the flat get bad in the specific way a flat gets bad when the person in it has stopped believing in the future tense — nothing dirty, just an accumulation of unmade decisions. A chair with clothes on it. Four mugs. Post on the table by the door, unopened, which he noticed one evening and stood looking at for some time.

He slept in pieces, sitting up, with the lamp on, and dreamed nothing at all, and woke each time with a jolt and checked his arm.

By the ninth day his hands had a tremor that had nothing to do with anything cosmic. By the eleventh he had begun to make small mistakes in front of other people — a word wrong, a step off a kerb, a sentence he could not land — and he understood, standing in a stairwell at work with his forehead against the cool of the wall, that he was going to have to sleep.

Not because he had stopped being afraid.

Because the body does not negotiate. It files the paperwork and then it does what it was going to do.

So he decided to do it on purpose.

That was the only piece of ground he could find, and he took it: if he was going to go under, he would go under *deliberately*, at a time he chose, having decided to. He wrote out the conditions the way he wrote out anything — lamp off, flat on his back, phone across the room, because that was how he had been lying at 6:41 on the twenty-sixth.

He set it for the following night, which gave him a day, and the day was the worst one yet, and at no point in it did he consider not going through with it.

It did not occur to him, once, in twenty-four hours, that he was preparing very carefully to lie down in the one place they had ever been able to reach him.
