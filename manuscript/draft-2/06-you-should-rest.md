# Chapter 6 — You should rest

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 3. Roadmap 5. First person
present. Completes the Act 1 splits when 7 lands.*

---

There are five of them and they all stand up.

Not at once — that would be a thing you would notice and object to. They come up out of the lobby chairs in a ragged sequence over about four seconds, the way people rise when somebody they have been waiting for finally comes through a door and they would rather not make a performance of it.

I have gone over those four seconds many times. The ragged sequence is the part that stays with me, because a ragged sequence is what you get from five people who have each independently decided to be casual about the same thing.

Nobody is behind the reception desk.

The desk is lit. There is a monitor on it, angled, showing a screensaver. There is a chair pulled out at an angle that says somebody left it in a hurry, and a jacket over the back of the chair, and no one has been in that seat for hours — I know that the way I know everything in this place — and the jacket has been arranged.

"You made good time," says the woman nearest me.

---

She is somewhere in her forties, in a grey suit that fits, with an air of having come from something more important and being entirely willing to be here instead.

She is carrying a lanyard.

Not wearing it. Carrying it, folded once, in her left hand, with the card turned in against her palm — and she keeps it there for the whole of the conversation, and she is still holding it like that when the lift doors open.

I do not think about it at the time. I think about it a very great deal afterwards, because there is only one reason a person folds a badge before they speak to you, and it is not that they have forgotten to put it on.

She does not offer her name.

I do not think to ask for it. That is the thing I want written down about her: not that she withheld it, but that in seven hours in that building it never once occurred to me to ask, and I am a man who asks. It is the single most efficient thing anybody does to me all night and it is done in the first ten seconds, by omission, at no cost.

"Good time from where," I say.

She smiles like I have made a joke that is a little old between us. "You'll be tired," she says. "We've put you on nine."

---

The lift is mirrored on three sides.

I watch five reflections of myself watching five reflections, and none of them is wearing a coat.

Two of the others have come up with us. They stand behind me, which I do not like, and they do not speak, which I like less. In the mirror I can see one of them looking at the back of my head with the flat unembarrassed attention of a man watching a screen — not hostile, not curious. *Monitoring.*

"What's the event," I say.

"Tomorrow evening," the woman says. "You're early. Most people are."

"Early for what."

The floor numbers climb. She lets three of them go by.

"You'll want to eat something," she says. "There's a kitchen."

I try one more.

"Who else is coming?"

And she turns her head and looks at me — properly, for about a second and a half, in a mirrored box with two silent men behind us — and what is in her face is the same thing that was in the face of the man with the ring when I asked about the rules.

Sympathy. Filed away instantly.

"Some of them are already here," she says.

---

The apartment is on the ninth floor and the door has no number.

The others do. I walk the corridor behind her and they are numbered along both sides in brushed steel figures, ordinary, sequential, the kind of thing nobody looks at.

Mine is between 906 and 908 and there is nothing on it.

Inside it is warm and low-lit and entirely ordinary. A short hall. A kitchen off it. A sitting room with two sofas at right angles and a bedroom through a door left ajar, so I can see the corner of a made bed.

There are lamps on. Somebody turned lamps on in here before I arrived.

"Bathroom's through there," she says from the doorway. "Anything you need, the phone goes to the desk."

"There's nobody at the desk."

"The phone goes to the desk," she says, pleasantly.

And it is not a correction. She does not adjust it — not one word, not the stress, not the shape of it. She says the identical sentence again at the identical speed, the way an announcement repeats, and the fact that a woman capable of that half-second of sympathy in a lift is also capable of *that* is the first thing all night that raises the hair on my arms.

Then she says: "You should rest."

"I'm not tired."

"You should rest," she says, and smiles, and closes the door.

---

The door does not lock.

I check immediately, ashamed of myself and checking anyway. The handle turns. The door opens onto a corridor that is empty and carpeted and silent, with its numbered doors going away in both directions.

Nothing stops me.

Nothing has stopped me all night. There was a padlock on a gate that was hanging through nothing. There was an open front door in the first building and a set of steps down into the cold and a row of cars I did not have to get into. I have walked four miles beside a live railway and nobody has come out to ask me what I am doing, in a world that has men whose entire job is to come out and ask you what you are doing.

I keep noticing that nothing stops me.

I keep failing to draw the conclusion, which is that nobody needs to stop a man who is already going exactly where you want him.

---

I close the door and stand in the little hall and look at the flat properly, and it is furnished the way a show home is furnished: everything chosen, nothing owned.

Two sofas nobody has sat on. Cushions at angles. A rug that has never had a chair dragged over it.

There is a bowl on the kitchen counter with nothing in it.

And there is no post.

I notice that standing in the hall with my coat — I have no coat. I notice it standing in the hall with my hands cold, and it is so small a thing that I nearly walk past it: there is a little console table by the door, exactly where mine is, and it is empty. No envelopes. No leaflet from a takeaway. No card from a courier saying they tried.

My own table has post on it. It has had post on it for three weeks. It is the single most reliable fact about my flat and it is the reason I eat standing at the counter.

Somebody has built a version of my home in which nobody has ever tried to reach me.

---

On the low table by the window there is a book.

It is face-down, splayed open to keep the page, spine up.

I know the cover.

I know the exact whiteness of the crease down the spine, because I put it there, on a train to the coast, four years ago, forcing the book flat one-handed because I had a coffee in the other. It has annoyed me every time I have seen it since. I have thought, more than once, in the mild way you think about small domestic failures, that I ought to have been more careful with it.

I stand over it for a long time without touching it.

Then I pick it up.

It is the right weight. The cover has the give that a cover has when a book has been read once and shelved. There is a mark on page forty-one where something was spilled and dried, which I had forgotten about entirely and which is there.

And it is open at the page I stopped at.

I put it back down, face-down, splayed, at the angle it was at — and I get that wrong twice and have to do it again, and the second time my hands are not good — and then I go and stand in the kitchen with both palms flat on the counter until my breathing is my own.

---

*They have been somewhere they should not have been.*

That is what I get. Standing in that kitchen, with a made bed through a doorway and a bowl with nothing in it, that is the whole of what I am able to build: that somebody has been in my life. Close enough to know a crease in a book. Close enough to know which page.

It is the wrong answer.

I want to be very clear that the right one is *available*. It is not hidden and it is not subtle. It is that they did not come and look at my things, they **derived** them; that a crease in a spine is not surveillance but arithmetic; that a flat with no post in it is not an oversight but a variable somebody set to zero because it did not matter to the run.

Everything in that apartment is a prediction with a very small error bar.

I do not reach it. Not that night, not that week, not for months. I stand in a kitchen at four in the morning and arrive, with total conviction, at *somebody has been in my flat* — and then I go and check the bedroom to see whether they have been in there too.

Everything I do for the next four months, I do as a man who believes he has been burgled by something.

---

I do not rest.

I want that on the record as a decision, because for a long time afterwards I thought of it as the one moment in that building where I did something of my own. Four in the morning, a warm flat, a made bed, seven hours of walking in my legs — and a woman in a grey suit had told me twice to sleep, and I did not.

It was the proudest thing I had.

I have since read the file on a woman in Halverton who did the same, and the file on a man with a stopped watch, and there is a word for what we all did in that flat at four in the morning, and the word is not *resisted*.

I put my coat on. I have no coat.

I go out into the corridor, past 908, and I start on nine, and I work down.
