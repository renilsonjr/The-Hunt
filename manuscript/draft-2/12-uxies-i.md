# Chapter 12 — Uxies I

*Draft 2, new. Third person present: it separates her from Fridan's past-tense
retrospective and says her story is still happening while his is being
recounted. Placed after his waking, so she exists in the reader before the
faction does. Roughly contemporaneous with Draft 1's Chapter 5.*

---

The city is arguing.

It has been arguing since before Uxies was born and it will be arguing after, and no Virden alive has ever heard it stop, so none of them hears it at all. It comes up off the freight road and down out of the towers and sideways across the west span: eleven million people being right at each other, machinery, weather, the long overlapping insistence of a world that has never once agreed on anything and has never particularly wanted to.

Her neighbour on the landing is arguing now, with somebody, through a doorway, about a delivery. He sees Uxies and breaks off to greet her and then goes straight back in, mid-sentence, without any sense that he has done something odd. That is the correct way to conduct oneself. Uxies has watched people do it her whole life and can perform a decent imitation and has never once felt it from the inside.

She is fifty-one. She works in the calibration hall under the span, which is a job that suits her for reasons she has stopped trying to explain at gatherings: she sits with instruments that are very slightly wrong and makes them right, one at a time, and the room is required by regulation to be quiet.

Nobody wants that job. There is always a vacancy. She has held hers for twenty-six years.

---

Her sister Malde comes on the fourth day of the week, which she has done for eleven years, and which Uxies is glad of in a way she is bad at showing.

Malde talks. That is not a complaint; Malde is warm and quick and genuinely funny and the talk is how she loves people, and she has three children and a partner and a life that generates enough material for all of it. She fills the flat like weather. Uxies makes the tea and puts in the two things Malde likes and says perhaps forty words in an hour, and both of them consider this a good evening.

Near the end, Malde says: "You've been going out there again."

"I go out there every week."

"You've been going twice."

Uxies does not answer at once. Through the window the sky is doing what it does, moving fast, the colour of the inside of a shell.

"It's a walk," she says.

"It's an industrial yard."

"It's a walk that ends at an industrial yard."

Malde laughs, and then does not, and puts her cup down.

"Ux," she says. "I'm not — I'm not starting. I'm not saying the thing. I know you hate the thing." She turns the cup a quarter turn on the table. "It's only that when you were nineteen you went twice a week for a while, and then you stopped, and then you were ill for a year and a half."

"That wasn't connected."

"No," says Malde. "That's what everyone said."

---

The calibration hall is four floors under the west span and holds forty-one
benches, and on a full shift perhaps nine of them are occupied.

The work is this: instruments come in that are very slightly wrong. Not broken
— broken is a different department and a different sort of person. These are
the ones that are out by a fraction that would not matter anywhere except that
it does, somewhere, at the end of a chain of other things, and somebody
downstream has noticed a number they cannot account for. Uxies takes one, and
finds where it is lying, and makes it true.

There is no trick to it. There is only the willingness to sit with one object
for as long as it takes, doing nothing visible, while it tells you.

Virden are not built for it. That is not a slander, it is the plain fact of a
species that came out of a crucible: her people are fast, argumentative,
brilliant in bursts, and constitutionally unable to sit still in a quiet room
and wait for something to reveal itself. The hall has been short-staffed for
two hundred years. Every generation it is redesigned to be more appealing and
every generation the benches stay empty, and the work goes on being necessary,
and a small number of odd people go on doing it.

Uxies is the best calibrator in the hall and has been for a decade, and the way
this is discussed is that she is *patient*, which is not the word. Patience is
enduring something. She is not enduring anything. In the quiet, with one wrong
instrument in front of her, she is the only version of herself that has ever
felt like the correct size.

Her supervisor, who likes her, once said — meaning it warmly, as the highest
thing he had — "You'd have made a wonderful machine."

He apologised immediately. Uxies told him not to, and meant it, and could not
explain why: that of all the things anybody had ever said about the shape of
her, it was the first that had not been an apology for it.

---

Here is what has been said to Uxies about the yard, kindly and constantly, for forty-two years.

That she is cold. That she does not participate. That she is unwell in a way that has never resolved into anything a physician can name, and that the not-resolving is itself a kind of evidence. That there is a hole where the ordinary appetite for the world ought to be — for noise, for company, for the good honest business of disagreeing with people, which on Earth 2 is not rudeness but the closest thing the species has to prayer.

Nobody has ever been cruel about it. That is the part she cannot make anyone understand. If they had been cruel she could have gone to war and it would have been simpler and she would have been someone who was wronged. Instead she has been the object of forty-two years of patient, affectionate concern, and there is no war to be had with that, and every year of it has confirmed to her that the thing at the centre of her life is a defect.

She has never once been able to say what the yard is for.

She has tried. At nine she said it was quiet, and was taken to a physician. At twenty-three she said it was where she could hear herself, and Malde cried, and Uxies did not use that sentence again. Somewhere after thirty she stopped offering explanations and started calling it a walk, and everyone was relieved, and she understood that being thought slightly eccentric was a great deal cheaper than being thought unwell.

She still does not know what it is for. That is the thing nobody has ever grasped: they think she is protecting a reason. She is not. She goes because the going answers something, and she has never been able to see the question.

---

She goes on the sixth day, in the late afternoon, in weather.

Under the west span, along the freight road, through the gap in the fence that has been a gap since before she was born. She has taken this route so many times that her feet do the corners without her, and she has long ago stopped seeing any of it — until the last forty metres, which she has never stopped seeing, because of what happens to the sound.

It thins.

Not all at once. It goes the way a fever goes: you notice you are not in it some time after you have left. The argument comes down and down and then, in the last few paces, it is simply not there — and Uxies steps into a flat open place with stacks in it and a rail and a fast sky, and the world stops talking.

She stands in the middle of it.

She does not do anything. She has never done anything. She does not meditate, she does not pray — Virden do not, particularly — she does not think about her sister or her work or the twenty-six years in the calibration hall. She stands with her arms at her sides in a disused yard on the edge of an arguing city, and something in the exact centre of her leans very slightly forward, the way you lean toward a voice you cannot quite hear through a wall.

It has been leaning her whole life.

She has never told anybody that part. Not Malde, not the physician at nine, not the one at twenty-three. It is the only true sentence about the yard she has and she has never once said it aloud, because she knows what it sounds like — and because there is no possible reply to it that would not make things worse.

She stays about an hour. She goes home when it starts to get dark, and the argument comes back up around her at the fence, and by the freight road she cannot hear the difference any more.

---

Four streets from her building, a vehicle is parked with its nose to the wall in a bay that is usually empty.

Uxies does not notice it. There is no reason she would; it is a plain grey thing and this is a city of nine hundred thousand vehicles and she has never in her life had a reason to look at one.

Inside it, someone writes down the time she came back.

They have written down the time she came back four hundred and ten times before. They have written down the time she came back since before they were assigned to her, in a file that opens on the day she was born, and the person doing it tonight has never spoken to her, will never speak to her, and could not tell you what she is for. The instruction is fifty-one years old and has never changed and reads, in effect: *note it, and do nothing.*

Under the entry, as under all the others, they put the word the file has always used.

*Unexplained.*

Then they close it, and they wait, because the whole of the job is waiting, and in eleven years of it nothing has ever happened.
