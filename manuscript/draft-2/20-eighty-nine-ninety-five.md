# Chapter 20 — Eighty-nine ninety-five

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 8. Roadmap 17. Third person past.
Follows Chapter 18 directly in emotional logic: having failed to be believed by
a person, he goes to a company.*

---

He tried to do it without them, and without anybody, and he wanted that on the record later, when there was nobody left to put it on the record for.

Four days after Cass's he ordered a consumer ancestry kit.

It was the kind advertised on the sides of buses — he had, in fact, scheduled the buses those advertisements were on — and it came in a white box with a cheerful leaflet and a diagram of a tube. He sat at his kitchen table at nine on a Tuesday night and spat into it up to a printed line, and rocked it eight times as instructed, and screwed the cap down until the blue fluid released.

It cost him eighty-nine pounds and ninety-five pence.

He walked it to the postbox on the corner and stood there for a second with his hand in the slot, holding on to it, and then let go.

Walking back he thought: *that is the sanest thing I have done in four months.*

A checkable question, asked of a company with a customer services number and a complaints procedure, which would come back on a date they had already told him, from people who had never once been inside his head — and, crucially, from people who did not have to *believe* him.

That was the part he could not stop turning over. Cass had read twenty-two pages and taken his hand. The company would not read anything. It would not form a view about him. It would look at the material and produce a number, and the number would be true or false regardless of what anybody thought of the man who sent it.

Six weeks. He had six weeks of something to wait for, and he got through them better than he had got through the four before.

---

He was also, in that period, getting worse in ways he catalogued and did not interpret.

He was sleeping four or five hours, badly, sitting up, and he was tired in a way that had stopped being about sleep — a flat grey shortage that food did not touch and rest did not move. His hands were unreliable in the mornings. Twice he lost a word mid-sentence at work, and the second time it was a common word, and the woman he was speaking to waited for it with the particular kindness people reserve for a colleague who is visibly coming apart.

And the air had gone wrong.

That was the one he could not get anyone near. Rooms had begun to feel *thin*. Offices, the train, his own front room with the heating on — a sense of insufficiency at the top of every breath, like a glass filled not quite to where he expected it to be. Not breathlessness. He could run for a bus. It was that each breath arrived slightly short of the amount it should have contained, and the shortfall did not accumulate into anything a doctor would recognise, and it never once went away.

He had started opening windows.

In the second week of November he sat through an entire meeting with the window behind him open onto a car park in the rain. The others put on coats. Two of them looked at each other. Nobody said anything, and he did not offer to close it, because the alternative was to sit in that room and drown politely.

He wrote in the notebook: *ask a doctor.*

It was the fourth time he had written it. He put a box around it, which he had not done before, and then sat looking at the box.

---

The results came on the ninth of December, by email, with a subject line that had his name in it and the word *ready*, and a small illustration of a helix.

He opened it standing up in his kitchen with his coat still on.

He was 41 per cent one thing and 22 per cent another and 19 per cent a third. There was a map with soft-edged blobs on it and a slider that let him watch the blobs move through six hundred years. There was a paragraph about a great-grandmother. There was a section headed **Traits** which told him, with a small graphic of a leaf, that he was likely to dislike coriander.

*Unassigned: 0.6%.*

He read the whole report twice. He read the methodology page, which was long and honest and told him about reference panels. He read the FAQ, which explained in a friendly font that unassigned percentages were common, that they reflected regions the panel could not confidently place, and that they were nothing to be concerned about.

There was a link at the bottom that said *Share your results.*

---

Fridan sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against the cupboard door and laughed.

It went somewhere unpleasant almost immediately and he let it. There was nobody in the flat and nobody was coming, and he sat on the floor in his coat for some time making a noise that was not really laughing any more, with the phone face-up on the lino beside him and the helix still on the screen.

He had been so sure.

Not that it would find something — he had known, in the reasonable part of him, that it probably would not. What he had been sure of was the *shape*: that at the end of six weeks he would be able to hold up a piece of ordinary paper from an ordinary company, printed in a friendly font, and say — to himself, if to nobody else — *this is checkable, and I checked it, and here is what it said.*

He had wanted one fact that did not come out of somebody's mouth.

He had wanted one thing in the whole affair that did not require a single person on earth to take his word.

Nought point six per cent. Nothing to be concerned about. Likely to dislike coriander.

---

**"Of course it was normal,"** Anser said that night, without impatience.

"Don't."

**"I'm not going to gloat, and I'm not going to pretend I didn't know what it would say. Both of those would be worse than telling you why."** A pause. **"They compare you to a panel of people. Everyone on the panel is human. You are almost entirely human. The test asked whether you resemble humans, and the answer, correctly, was yes."**

"Then it isn't there."

**"It's there. It is not in the part they read."** Another pause. **"And it would not mean anything to them if it were. Meaning requires a second example, and they have never seen one, and they would file it as an artefact and improve their pipeline."**

Fridan lay in the dark of his own bedroom with the window open onto December.

"Then show me."

---

**"I said I would, and I meant it. But I want to tell you the price first, because if I don't tell you the price and you find it out afterwards, everything I have ever said to you turns into something else."**

"Go on."

**"To show you, I need you properly asleep. Not this — not the shallow business you have been doing in a chair with the lamp on. Down. Four hours, maybe five, in the state you have been refusing since August."**

The parking structure hummed.

**"And while you are there you will be unguarded, and open, and I am not the only thing that knows how to use that. I don't believe anything else will come. I can't prove it to you. You would be taking my word, and my word is the only thing I have ever given you."**

Fridan lay very still.

He thought about a man on a platform in Halverton, shifting a canvas bag on his shoulder, saying *some of it comes in through the sleeping, and that's the bit you want to be careful about, because you can't lock that.*

He thought about eighty-nine pounds and ninety-five pence, and a friendly font, and nought point six per cent.

"How long do I have to decide?"

**"You don't have to decide anything,"** said Anser. **"You will get tired enough eventually and it will happen without a decision. I'd rather you did it on purpose. People are better afterwards when they chose."**

Which was — Fridan thought, much later, when it was far too late for the thought to be any use to him — a remarkably honest description of the entire process by which he was taken.

He lay in the dark and listened to a building he had lived in for nine years.

"Tonight," he said.
