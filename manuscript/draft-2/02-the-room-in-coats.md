# Chapter 2 — The room in coats

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 1. Roadmap 1. First person
present. Opens on the line Chapter 1 now hands over.*

---

The room is too warm and nobody has taken off their coat.

That is the first thing I notice, and I notice it the way you notice things here: completely, and without being able to say when I started. There are perhaps thirty people in a room built for more, standing in the loose arrangement of people who have been told to wait somewhere expensive. The ceiling is high and dark and I cannot find where it stops. The light comes from low down, from lamps on side tables, so every face is lit from underneath and nobody looks quite like themselves.

They are all wearing coats. Long ones, good ones — wool, and one that is not wool and cost more than the wool. It is warm enough in here that the back of my neck is damp, and not one of them has so much as opened a button.

I understand what that means before I have finished counting them. Not by reasoning. By knowing, the way I know everything in this place: taking off your coat here would say that you intended to stay, and none of them intends to stay. They intend to win and leave.

I am not wearing a coat.

I did not bring one. I am in the clothes I put on this morning — whatever morning that was — and the difference is the loudest thing in the room. Nobody remarks on it. A woman near the window looks at me the way you look at a chair that has been moved.

---

There is one man with his coat over his arm.

He is standing by the far wall with a glass he is not drinking from, and he is the only person in the room who is not looking at the door at the other end, and after a while I work out that he is not looking at anything.

I take him for staff, at first. He is not. Twenty minutes later, when the door opens and the room begins to move, he does not move with it — he stands with his coat over his arm and lets them all go past him, and then he goes and sits down on one of the chairs nobody has been sitting on, and puts his head back.

I never learn his name and I never see him again. But I have thought about him a great deal, because he is the only person I encounter that entire night who has stopped, and he is the only one who looks comfortable.

---

Fragments of the room, as I have them, and I have all of them.

"—no, they moved it, it was a Thursday—"

A woman in her fifties describing a house she has apparently bought and not yet seen, to a younger woman who is listening with the specific attention of an employee.

Two men, close together, one of them saying something short and both of them laughing at it in a way that does not carry.

Somebody near the drinks table saying "—fourth—" and somebody else saying "—no—" and then nothing.

A very old woman sitting in the only occupied chair with her hands in her lap and her coat done up to the throat, who does not speak once, and who is helped up when the door opens by two people who arrived separately and both go to her.

Not one person in that room is nervous. That is the thing I keep returning to. There is a stillness in there that I would afterwards recognise in exactly one other place, which is a waiting area outside an operating theatre — the particular calm of people who have paid a great deal and are now obliged to wait for something they cannot influence.

---

Then a man beside me speaks without turning his head.

"First time."

It is not a question, so I do not answer it. He is sixty, perhaps, with a heavy ring on his right hand and a face that has been photographed a lot. He is watching the far end of the room, where a door has not yet opened.

"They usually bring one," he says. "Every run. Somebody who doesn't know the rules." He tips his glass an inch toward me — a toast so small it would be deniable in any company. "It keeps the rest of us honest."

"Brought by who?"

He smiles at the door. He does not answer, and I understand from the shape of the smile that he thinks I already know, and that pretending otherwise would be a kind of rudeness.

So I try the other end of it.

"What are the rules?"

And that does turn him. He looks at me properly for the first time, for about two seconds, and what is in his face is not suspicion. It is something much worse. It is a flicker of *sympathy*, immediately packed away.

"There's one," he says. "Everything else is manners." He looks back at the door. "You'll find it. Everybody finds it. It's not hidden — it's just not the sort of thing anybody can tell you, because saying it out loud makes it a different rule."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agrees, comfortably. "It isn't."

---

Here is what I know, and I have no idea how I know it.

I know that everyone in this room is here to find one specific object, and that only one of us will.

I know that the object is small.

I know that it is somewhere in this building, and that the building is larger on the inside than the outside allowed for, and that this is not strange — it is simply a property of the building, the way a coastline is long.

I know that there are rules, and that these people are not going to explain them to me, and that the man with the ring has just told me the truth about why.

And I know — this is the part I have never been able to make anybody understand — that I am going to be the one who finds it.

Not that I might. That I *am*.

It sits in me with the flat weight of a remembered fact, like the date of my own birthday. There is no excitement in it. It is not hope and it is not confidence, both of which have a texture of effort about them, a sense of leaning forward. This leans nowhere. It is simply among the things that are the case, filed with my own name and the number of my flat.

I try, standing there in that room, to doubt it.

I want that written down, because of everything afterwards. I am not a credulous man. I make timetables; my entire working life is the discipline of assuming the optimistic version is wrong. And I stand in a warm room among thirty strangers in coats and I take that certainty and I turn it over deliberately, looking for the seam, the way you press a floorboard to see if it gives —

and there is nothing to press.

The doubt will not form. Not *is defeated*: will not form. The shape of it starts in me and then does not continue, the way a sentence stops when you have forgotten what you were going to say, except that I have not forgotten anything.

I am the only person in this room who does not belong here, and I am the only person in this room who is going to win.

Both are true. Neither cancels the other. I find, standing there, that this steadies me — and it should not steady me, it should be the single most frightening thing that has ever happened to me, and it is four months before I understand which of those two reactions was mine.

---

The door at the far end opens and the room stops.

Nobody comes through it. It simply stands open onto a corridor with a runner of dark red carpet, and the people in coats begin to move toward it in ones and twos, unhurried, the way people move at a funeral.

There is no announcement. Everyone knew. Everyone was waiting to be seen not hurrying.

I go last, because I do not know where I am going and because I would rather watch which way they turn.

They turn every way. That is the first useful thing I learn all night: at the end of the corridor the group comes apart like water on a stone, and by the time I reach it they are gone — up a staircase, through two side doors, one of them simply not there any more — and each of them has gone as though they had decided in advance, which they had.

---

The corridor is long and the carpet swallows the sound.

There are doors on both sides. Some stand open onto rooms with the lights off, and I look into them as I pass, and what is in them is furniture under sheets. A piano with its lid down. A tall clock, stopped.

And one whole wall of books turned spine-in.

I stop at that one.

Every book on every shelf has been shelved backwards, so that what faces the room is the block of paper edges — a wall of cream and grey, thousands of them, absolutely silent about themselves. It is a thing wealthy people do for the look of it; I know that; I have seen photographs.

It is also, standing in a dark doorway at whatever hour that was, the single most hostile object I have ever looked at. A room full of things that will not tell you what they are, arranged that way on purpose, by somebody who found it restful.

The building smells of wax and cold air and, underneath, very faintly, of incense.

That is when I understand what this reminds me of.

It is not an auction, which is what the coats and the low voices first suggested. It is not a viewing and it is not a sale.

It is a *service*.

These people are not shopping. They are observing something. The room upstairs was a narthex; the waiting was liturgical; the not-hurrying through the door was not manners but form. Every one of them knows the responses.

I do not.

Somewhere ahead of me, out of sight, someone laughs — once, and stops.
