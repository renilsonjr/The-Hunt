# Chapter 11 — The document

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 5. Roadmap 11. Third person past
— Act 2's register, and the book's first gearshift. Chapter 10 ended on the
waking, so this begins in the minute after it.*

---

His name was Fridan, and he was thirty-four years old, and he lived on the fourth floor of a building in Nor Yesey with a window that faced a parking structure.

He lay flat on his back in the grey light with his heart going and his right arm shaking from the shoulder down, and he watched it happen the way you watch weather through glass.

The shaking lasted about forty seconds. He held the arm up in front of his face and watched the tremor run out of it in stages, and the hair on the forearm stayed standing long after the shaking stopped — every follicle up, wrist to elbow, so that when he finally ran his other hand along the skin it felt like the nap of something.

There was pressure in both ears. It had a sound in it, or under it: not a ringing, lower than that, a held thing on the very edge of what a room can contain. It faded over about four minutes and he lay still through all four of them, listening to it go, the way you listen to a train you have decided not to get on.

The clock said 6:41.

The alarm was set for seven. It had nineteen minutes to run and he lay there and let it, because getting up would mean the day starting, and the day starting would mean deciding what had happened.

The window said Wednesday. The top deck of the parking structure was wet. A man in a fleece was walking to a car with a lunch bag.

---

He did not think *what a dream*.

That was the first thing wrong and he registered it while it was happening. There was no swim. There was none of the small routine embarrassment of a man reassembling a room around himself — no moment of *ah, of course, this is where I am* — because he had never stopped knowing where he was.

He knew where he was, and he knew where he had been, and both of them were simply true, side by side, with nothing in his head prepared to rank them.

He got up. He went into the kitchen and stood in the middle of it with his hands at his sides for a while, the way you do in the first minute of a power cut.

Then he went back to the bedroom, took his phone off the charger, sat down on the edge of the mattress, and started to write.

---

He wrote a header first.

He was not able, afterwards, to say why. The impulse was procedural rather than emotional — the same instinct that makes a man reach for a form. He had the sense, vague and insistent, that this was going to need to be a *document* rather than a note, and that a document has a header.

> **The Hunt — REM — Dream 1 — 08/26/26 — Nor Yesey — UN**

And then, underneath, because he did not know what else to call it:

> *Script of what I remember from my dream*

He wrote for fifty minutes with his thumbs, sitting on the edge of an unmade bed with the alarm going off at seven and being silenced without his looking at it.

He got the coats. He got the room that was too warm and the man with the heavy ring, and *they usually bring one*. He got the bus stop, and the six-minute layover at the outer end, and the fact that the Sunday service had been correct. He got the footbridge, and the rail going red — he wrote *it made a train rail red so I could follow that*, and stopped, and looked at the sentence for some time, and did not change it.

He got the woman in the grey suit. He got the lanyard folded in her hand and the door with no number and the empty console table. He got the sixteen doors and the wear in the carpet and the man leaning on the wall two feet from a door.

He got the ball, and the two marks in front and the one behind, and he drew them, badly, with his thumb.

He wrote the chord. He wrote *like those sound tracks* and could not finish the comparison and left it unfinished, and it is unfinished in every subsequent version.

He did not write down the last thing anybody said in that room.

---

He noticed himself not writing it.

He noticed the small deliberate step around it — the way you notice yourself walking around a wet patch on a floor, aware of the adjustment and not making anything of it. There was a moment, at about the forty-minute mark, when the sentence was available and he went past it and wrote the next thing instead.

He told himself he would come back to it.

He came back to it eleven days later, and by then it had grown into the largest single object in his life.

---

He went to work.

He has been asked about that more than any other decision he made that year, usually by people who mean it as evidence of something. The honest answer is that it was twenty past seven on a Wednesday morning, he had a job, and there was nothing in the world he could have said to anybody that would have made not going make sense.

He got the 14 in, which he did most days, and stood in the aisle because the seats were gone by his stop.

At the office he produced a passable imitation of a man doing his job. He looked at the northbound run and the two minutes he had moved on Tuesday, and decided they would hold for about a fortnight, which was what he had decided on Tuesday.

Priya asked him whether he wanted anything from the place on the corner and he said a coffee and then changed it to a tea, and she said, "You always do that," and he said, "I know."

That was the fourth time he had had that exchange in a month. He knew it was the fourth. He knew the other three.

---

The thing that broke him was not the size of any of it.

It was the durability.

He knew what dreams did. Everybody knows what dreams do. You wake with a whole country in your head and by the time the kettle boils you have three images and a mood; by lunch you have one image; by the evening you have the fact that there was a dream, and the feeling of it, and nothing you could put in a sentence. That is not forgetting. That is what dreams *are* — weather, structurally incapable of persisting.

At 11:20 that morning, in a meeting he had not cancelled, he checked.

He did it properly, because he did not know how to do anything else. He picked a specific thing and went back to it: the room on the third floor with the long table. Dark wood, unpolished. Nine chairs down one side and eight down the other. The bowl. Three apples, one on its side, the one nearest him. Cold room. Light from the window on the left.

And a dust-shadow on the table. A clean ring in the film of dust, about the size of a saucer, a little way from the bowl, where something round had stood for a long time and been moved.

He had not written that down.

It was there. It was not *recalled* — it was **available**, in the way that the layout of his own flat was available, waiting rather than surfacing. When he went looking for the far end of the third-floor corridor he found the exact angle of the young man's phone torch, and the pattern of the older man's shirt, which had been checked, small blue check, and which he had never once consciously looked at.

He sat in the meeting with his hands flat on his knees and let somebody else talk.

---

Then, because he was the kind of man he was, he ran a control.

He needed a comparison, and he needed one from roughly the same distance, and he took the only thing available: the Tuesday. Not the dream — the actual Tuesday, the twenty-fifth, twenty-six hours earlier. An ordinary day he had lived through with his eyes open.

*What was Priya wearing.*

Nothing. A colour, possibly. He was not confident about the colour.

*What was on the desk to his left.*

He could construct it — there was usually a monitor and usually a plant — but he was constructing it, and he could feel himself constructing it, the way you feel yourself filling in a form.

*What had Cass said about the hedge.*

That there had been letters. Not the words. The gist, worn smooth, exactly as a memory should be: a summary his brain had made at the time and kept instead of the thing itself.

He went back and forth between the two for the rest of the meeting — the Tuesday and the night — and the difference was not one of degree. It was a difference of *kind*. One of them was memory: constructed, lossy, summarised, his. The other was a recording somebody else had made and left in him.

He wrote a single line in the margin of the agenda in front of him, in handwriting that came out smaller than his own:

*It is not memory. Memory is a thing you make.*

---

Twelve hours later nothing had gone.

By the following Sunday nothing had gone. Four years later he could still tell you which of the sixteen doors had the child's drawing in it — the fifth, on the right, folded twice, the folds gone soft — and he could tell you the colour of the plastic tag on the key ring in the ninth, and that the seventh had nothing on the table and a cheap pair of glasses folded on the arm of the sofa with one arm slightly bent.

It was the single most frightening fact of his life and he could never explain to anybody why, because to everyone he ever tried it on it sounded like a man boasting about his memory.

He was not describing a good memory. He was describing the absence of one.

Memory is a thing you make. This had been *installed*, complete, by something that had not asked, in a man who had gone to sleep on an ordinary Tuesday thinking about a bus.
