# Chapter 4 — The footbridge

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 2. Roadmap 3. First person
present. Ends on her first instruction.*

---

I walk for a long time before I admit I do not know where I am going.

The street outside the building runs downhill and I take it because it runs downhill. There are no other pedestrians. There are parked cars with frost on the windscreens, and lit windows four floors up, and somewhere out of sight a bus sighs and pulls away — and all of it is completely ordinary and none of it is a place I have ever been.

It is cold in the specific way that gets into the base of your neck. I have no coat. I put my hands in my pockets and take them out again because it changes nothing, and after four streets I stop being able to feel the tops of my ears.

I try to think of the name of the city.

There isn't one.

Not a blank where the name should be. That is what I want to be exact about, because everybody I have ever tried to describe this to has heard *I couldn't remember* and that is not it. There is no sense of a gap at all — no edge, no reaching, none of the small physical hunt you do when a word is missing. It is like the absence of a sensation you have never had. I only find it because I go looking, and I only go looking because I am cold and bored and walking downhill.

That should stop me.

It does not, quite. It goes into the same pocket as the building that was bigger inside than out, and the pocket holds a very great deal tonight and does not appear to be filling up.

---

There is a bus stop about a mile down.

I go to it the way you go to a lit window. It is a standard flag on a standard pole, and there is a timetable case bolted to it, and inside the case there is a timetable.

I read it for eleven minutes.

I want to explain what that eleven minutes did to me, and to do that I have to say something about myself, which is that I have spent my entire working life looking at these. I can tell you inside thirty seconds whether a printed timetable was made by somebody who has stood at the stop it belongs to. Most of them were not.

This one is *good*.

The headways are sensible. There is a proper evening taper, and the last departures are staggered across the three routes so that they do not all arrive at the interchange in the same four minutes, which is the single most common mistake anybody makes. Somebody has built in a real layover at the outer end — six minutes, which is what you would allow on a route that length — and has not stolen it back to make the frequency look better on paper, which is what everybody does.

It is the work of a professional. It is the work of somebody who has done my job, and done it with care, and taken pleasure in it.

And the places are not places.

Not invented-sounding. That would be easier. They are ordinary — a road, a hospital, a retail park, three villages — and I have never heard of any of them, and the more of them I read the more certain I am that no network in the world runs those roads in that order, because the geography implied by the running times is not geography. Leg three takes eleven minutes and leg four takes nine, and if both are true then the interchange is somewhere the other legs have already been.

I stand at a bus stop in the cold at some hour with no name and read a beautiful, careful, impossible timetable, and what goes through me is not wonder.

It is the feeling of being *catered for*.

Somebody built this. Not for the city, because there is no city. For the man who would stop and read it. Somebody knew that whoever came down this hill tonight would go to a lit case on a pole the way a dog goes to a smell, and would look first at the layover allowances, and they made sure the layover allowances were right.

I stop reading. I make myself walk away from it, and I get about forty metres, and then I go back and read the rest, because I have to know whether the Sunday service is there.

It is there. It is correct.

---

What has stopped is the pull.

In the doorway of that building it was there — ahead of me, insistent, a hand at the wrist. Four streets later it is gone, and what is left is a man walking downhill in the cold with no coat, getting further from something at a steady four miles an hour.

I stop at a corner under a dead streetlight and say, out loud, to nobody:

"All right. Which way."

Nothing answers.

But saying it makes the not-answering into a shape. I notice the shape. I file it, with the pocket and the timetable, and I keep walking.

---

The rail line is what I find instead.

I come down a flight of steps between two buildings and there it is below me, in a cutting, running left to right across the whole width of the world: four tracks, ballast, the grey grid of the sleepers, catenary posts going away in both directions in exact perspective until they stop being posts and become a texture.

There is a footbridge.

I go up onto it and put my hands on the rail — the handrail, cold, gritty with old paint that has been painted over — and look down at the tracks, and for the first time all night I feel something that is neither certainty nor fear.

It is relief.

The tracks go two ways. That is two more directions than I had a minute ago, and every one of them is a direction somebody else built, which means somewhere at the end of it there are people who did not build it for me.

Left, the line runs toward a mass of towers with their tops lit. Right, it goes into a low dark that could be anything.

---

I stand up there long enough for my hands to hurt.

And I cannot choose.

I want to be careful here, because *cannot choose* sounds like indecision and this is not indecision. I am not weighing anything. There is nothing to weigh. I have no reason, no clue, no bearing, no preference — not even the small animal tug that would make a person turn left rather than right at a fork in a wood.

I have only the memory of having had one.

The certainty in that building was not mine.

I understand that on the footbridge, holding the rail, and it is the coldest thing that has happened to me all night, colder than the room that came round to itself through the wrong wall. It was given to me. It has been taken back. And the difference between those two states is the difference between being a person and being a tool that has been set down on a bench.

I have been walking around inside somebody else's confidence all night, wearing it like a coat I did not bring, and mistaking it for character.

So I say it out loud, because out loud has started to seem like the only lever I have:

"I don't know where to go."

And she says: **"I know."**

---

She is not in the air.

She is not behind me and she is not above me and there is no sound, in the sense of something arriving at my ears. I do not turn round. There is nothing to turn round to, and some part of me understands that instantly and completely, in the same moment, and does not try.

She is in the place where my own voice is.

That interior room where you hear yourself think, in your own voice, in the accent you have when nobody is listening — she is *in there*, and it is not my thought, and there is no mistaking it for one. You could not mistake it any more than you could mistake a hand on your shoulder for your own hand.

She is warm.

That is the first thing, and it stays the first thing, all the way through everything that comes afterwards, and I have never once been able to make it sound like anything other than a man being sentimental. It is not kindness. Kindness is aimed. This is warm the way a room is warm — not doing it at me, simply its condition, the way a room that has had a fire in it all day is warm to whoever opens the door.

I do not move. My hands stay on the rail.

"Who's that," I say.

**"You are cold,"** she says. **"You have been walking for eleven minutes. That was necessary. It will not be necessary again."**

Eleven minutes. I had thought an hour. I turn that over and cannot get anywhere with it and put it in the pocket with everything else.

"Who *are* you?"

**"Look at the line."**

---

Later — much later, on a morning when the room is a real room and the light is real light and the silence has an entirely different quality — I will go over these first four minutes the way you go over an accident.

I will look for the moment I should have known.

I will not find it. What I will find is this: I ask her three questions in the first minute and she answers none of them, and it does not once occur to me to hold that against her. Because every single time she does not answer, she gives me something *better* — something that moves me forward, that solves the actual problem I am standing in the middle of.

You are cold. You have been walking eleven minutes. It will not be necessary again.

None of that is an answer. All of it is a relief.

She is so useful that the not-answering never rises to the level of being noticed. It is not concealed from me. It is simply never the most interesting thing in any given second, and there are a great many seconds, and by the time I have any of them to spare I am four miles down a service path beside a rail she has made red.

That is not a lie. She never tells me one. I want to be exact about that afterwards, when exactness is the only thing I have left, and I want it written down in my own hand rather than reported by somebody who did not stand on that bridge: **she never once lied to me.**

She simply never answered what I asked, and gave me instead what I needed.

And a man being given what he needs does not audit the transaction.

---

"Look at the line," she says.

And I look at the line.
