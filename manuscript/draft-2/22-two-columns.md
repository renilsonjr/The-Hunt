# Chapter 22 — Two columns

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 9. Roadmap 19. Third person past.*

---

For nine days after that he did not do very much at all.

He went to work and produced a passable imitation of a man doing his job. He ate when it occurred to him. He sat in the evenings in the chair by the open window with a blanket over him and the December cold coming in, and turned the same fact over and over the way you turn a stone in your pocket until it has no surface left.

She had known.

Not *found out*. Known — for the whole of him, presumably. For the boy of nine on a bicycle with a bent mudguard. For the man of twenty-six standing in his first flat with the keys still in his hand, thinking that this was the beginning of something. For every unremarkable Tuesday of a life she had apparently been able to look at any time she liked.

There had been a piece of her brother sitting in the middle of a man in Nor Yesey, and she had seen it, and she had done nothing about it.

And then one night in August something had walked into that man's sleep and used the fragment as a doorknob.

---

**"You keep saying she let it happen,"** Anser observed, on the sixth evening. **"I want to be careful with you here, because it would be very easy for me to agree, and it wouldn't be honest."**

"Go on, then."

**"I don't think she knew what was being done. I think she knew what you *were*, and thought it was nothing — a fragment, inert, a smudge left over from an old quarrel, no more significant in a man than a birthmark."** A pause. **"And I think it never once occurred to her that anybody could build a use for it. Because she has never in her existence had to think like something that is locked out."**

Fridan sat with the blanket and the cold.

"That's worse."

**"It might be. It's certainly worse for her."**

"No — you don't —" He stopped, and started again, because it mattered and he wanted to say it properly. "If she'd known and allowed it, that's a decision. I could be angry at a decision. You can *argue* with something that decided."

**"And instead?"**

"Instead she didn't think about me." He was looking at the ceiling. "For thirty-four years. Not cruelly. Not even carelessly. I was simply never interesting enough to be a problem, and that is how a thing gets left lying about until somebody picks it up and uses it."

Anser did not say anything to that, which was unusual, and which Fridan did not notice for some hours.

---

What he did notice — dimly, right at the back, the way you notice a draught — was that every road Anser walked him down ended in the same place.

*She is not what you thought. And I am here.*

Six months of conversations and he could not point at a single one where he had been told an untruth, or pushed, or flattered. The tone had never changed. The answers had never been anything but complete, and several of them had been actively unflattering to the answerer.

And every single one of them terminated at the same door.

He noticed it on the sixth evening, and set it down, and did not pick it up again — because it was the sixth evening, and he had run out of whatever it is that lets a man suspect the only thing still talking to him.

---

On the tenth day he decided to speak to her.

He was clear with himself about the reasons, and he made himself write them down in the notebook, in two columns, because he had learned that much.

The left-hand column said:

> *Because she needs to know. Something is using her, every night, on an industrial scale, through people who wake up with nothing and never find out, and she cannot see it, and I am the only person alive who can tell her.*

Every word of it was true. He checked it line by line the way he would check a running time, and it held.

The right-hand column said:

> *Because I want her to answer me.*

---

He looked at the two columns for a long time.

Then he did the thing he had been doing for four months whenever something frightened him, which was to attack it with procedure. He tried to talk himself out of it on paper, under headings, the way he would have killed a bad timetable proposal.

*Risk: it breaks the one instruction I have been given.* — Noted. Anser had been right about everything checkable. Anser had also never explained why, and had said so: *it's a reason, it's just not one you like.*

*Risk: I do not know what she does with it.* — Also noted. Neither did Anser, who had said as much, which was either honesty or the most efficient possible way to make a man stop asking.

*Risk: she has heard eleven days of me already and has not answered.* — Then nothing is lost.

*Risk:* — and here he stopped, because the next one was the real one and he made himself put it down in the same handwriting as the others —

*Risk: I am about to do the single most dangerous thing available to me, for reasons that are two-thirds noble and one-third a man who wants to be spoken to, and I cannot separate the thirds, and I am not going to be able to separate them afterwards either.*

He sat with that.

Then he closed the notebook and went to find the keys for the roof — because he had discovered, at some point in his thirty-fourth year, that a true reason and a real reason sit side by side in a man perfectly comfortably, and that the second one is the one that moves his legs.

---

The roof of his building was flat, with gravel on it, and a low parapet, and the extract fans in a housing at the north end that had been humming since before he moved in.

He went up at ten past five in the morning.

He had not slept. He had a coat on, which he noted, because he had spent four months noticing every time he had a coat. He had not written anything down about what he was going to say, and had decided deliberately against it, on the grounds that a man reading from a piece of paper on a roof at five in the morning would be doing something slightly different from what he intended to do.

The city was doing its early business. The parking structure had four cars in it. There was one lit window in the block opposite, and a man in it moving about a kitchen, and Fridan watched him for a while — a stranger making breakfast at five, forty metres away, with no idea he was the last ordinary thing anybody was going to see.

It was cold and clear. The air, up there, was the closest thing to enough that he had breathed in a month.

Fridan went to the parapet and put his hands on it, the way he had put them on a handrail over four tracks in a cutting in a city with no name.

And he began to speak.
