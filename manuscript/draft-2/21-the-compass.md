# Chapter 21 — The compass

*Draft 2. Second of two from Draft 1's Chapter 8. Roadmap 18. Third person past.
The reveal that reframes all of Act 1: the pull was never a gift.*

---

He did not dream. That is the wrong word for it and he never used it.

He went down further than he had been since August — past the place where the room goes, past the last thin awareness of a body on a bed with the window open — and something met him there and did not build him a city.

There was no set this time either. No room, no light, no direction.

Afterwards the only way he could describe the next part was to say that he was shown himself the way you are shown a house you already live in. Somebody walks you through your own hall and points at the meter cupboard and you discover that you have never once looked at it, in nine years, although you have gone past it four thousand times.

It went on for a long time and none of it was frightening.

Most of what he was shown was ordinary, in the way that the inside of anything is ordinary: the long patient architecture of a man, assembled out of two parents and a great-grandmother and several thousand generations of people who had dug and walked and buried each other and gone on. He was not moved by it. He would have expected to be moved by it. It had the quality of a well-run warehouse.

And then there was a piece that was not from any of them.

---

It was not dramatic.

He wants that understood, and he says so twice in the account, because everybody who has ever been told about this has imagined something with a light in it.

It was small, and old, and *quiet* — and quiet in a specific way that took him a long time to name, and that he eventually named by giving up on adjectives and using a comparison instead:

it was quiet in the way that a laundry ball on a white shelf is quiet.

He came nearer to it. In whatever sense he was coming nearer to anything.

And his chest did the thing.

---

The hand closing. The **there**.

Not similar. Not comparable, not reminiscent, not an echo. The *same* sensation — filed, available, instantly matched against the original with the flat total accuracy that had been installed in him in August, the way he could match a checked shirt or four stitches on a cuff.

The same event that had happened in his chest at some hour of the night in a room on the third floor of a building that does not exist, standing in a doorway, looking at three apples in a bowl with one of them lying on its side.

He came up out of it at ten past five in the morning and lay completely still.

---

"It was me," he said aloud to the ceiling, before he remembered not to, and then did not care. "The pull. That was *me*. That was mine."

**"Yes."**

"She didn't give me that."

**"No."**

"All night." He was not shouting. He noticed that he was not shouting, and that his voice had gone quiet and reasonable, which was worse. "All night I thought — the certainty, in the first building, before she ever spoke to me. That I would be the one who found it. I stood in a room full of people in coats and I *tried to doubt it*, and I couldn't, and I have spent four months thinking something put that in me."

**"Something did not put it in you."**

"I thought I'd been chosen."

**"You were selected,"** Anser said. **"It is a different word and you should use the different word. Nobody looked at your life and saw anything in it. A machine read a very large number of people looking for one signal, and you gave it — and the rest, the flat, the book with the crease in it, the timetable at the bus stop, the whole of that night built to make an ordinary man walk downhill instead of uphill, all of that was arranged afterwards. Around the fact of you. The way a chair is built around a person's shape."**

"And the thing on the shelf?"

**"The same kind of thing as the thing in you."**

---

Fridan lay in the dark with his arm across his eyes.

He had spent four months treasuring one sentence out of that night, and he had never said it aloud to anybody, and he had known even while he was writing it down that it was the most embarrassing thing in the document.

*It was like my whole life was meant to direct me to that moment.*

It was true. That was the joke.

His whole life had been meant to direct him there. Every unremarkable day of it. Only the meaning had not been in the life. It had not been in any of the things he had privately imagined it might be in during thirty-four years of quietly suspecting he was heading somewhere — not in the work, not in the fact that he had been good at it, not in any decision he had ever made or any decency he had ever managed.

It had been in a fragment of something sitting in the part of him that no company with a customer services number knows how to read.

It had been there when he was four, and nineteen, and on every ordinary Tuesday since. It would have been in any other body it happened to land in. And it had done what it did — recognised its own kind across a floor of a building — with no more regard for Fridan than a compass has for the hand that is holding it.

---

He asked the rest of it before it got light, and Anser answered, and answered shortly, which he understood afterwards was mercy and at the time felt like being handed things too fast to catch.

There were two of them. That was the beginning of it. One made the other, a long time ago, and they came apart over something Anser refused to summarise.

**"I'll tell you when there's time to tell it properly, because the short version makes them both sound stupid, and neither of them is stupid."**

Rather than finish each other, they made an arrangement.

Two galaxies. One each. Hers, with people in it. His, with something else in it — which had been people once, and had been added to.

"And between them?"

**"Air,"** said Anser.

Fridan opened his eyes in the dark.

**"That's the whole Barrier. There is no wall and there is no fleet and there never has been. She made the two atmospheres incompatible at the level of the body, so that anything from his side that breathes yours dies in about ninety seconds, and anything from your side that breathes theirs dies rather faster. It is the most elegant thing she ever did and it has held without maintenance for longer than your species has had a word for sky."**

"So they can't come here."

**"They cannot come here. They have never been able to come here. And that is not reassurance, Fridan — that is the *reason*. If they could walk in and take what they wanted, nobody would ever have had to go into anybody's sleep."**

---

The window was open onto the December cold and Fridan lay under it breathing air that had felt thin since November.

He did not put those two facts beside each other.

He would like to be able to say that he did. He has read that passage of his own document more often than any other and he has never been able to make it say anything except what it says, which is that a man was told that the two worlds are separated by air that kills, while lying under an open window in winter because rooms had stopped containing enough of it, and did not connect them.

He turned his head on the pillow and asked the question he had actually been building toward all night — the one that had been sitting underneath everything since a woman in Halverton showed him a note in her own handwriting.

"The piece in me. The fragment." His mouth was dry. "It isn't hers, is it."

**"No,"** said Anser. **"It's his."**

Fridan lay still.

"Then she'd have known."

**"Go on."**

"If she can look at people at all — if she's anything like what you've told me she is — then at some point in thirty-four years she looked at me." He heard his own voice doing something. "She'd have seen a piece of her brother sitting in a man in Nor Yesey. She'd have seen it when I was nine."

The pause went on a long time.

Long enough that Fridan sat up in the bed, in the cold, with his heart going.

**"Yes,"** said Anser.
