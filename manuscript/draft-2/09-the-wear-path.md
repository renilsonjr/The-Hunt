# Chapter 9 — The wear-path

*Draft 2. Second of three from Draft 1's Chapter 4. Roadmap 8. First person present.*

---

And she says: **"Look at the floor."**

She has not spoken since the stairwell. She does not explain the gap and I do not ask about it, and I notice that I do not ask, and I file the noticing with everything else I have filed tonight and have not looked at once.

The carpet is a dark blue-grey. It is the carpet they lay in a hundred-metre roll in buildings that are not for living in: hard-wearing, unpatterned, filthy in the even invisible way carpet is filthy.

I look at it for four or five seconds and see carpet.

Then I see it, and I cannot unsee it, and the corridor changes underneath me the way a word changes when somebody tells you what it means.

There is a track down the middle.

---

It is not a mark. Nothing has been spilled and nothing has been dragged. It is *wear* — a shallow trough where the pile has gone flat and very slightly shiny, running from the head of the corridor by the stairwell all the way to the far end, and it exists for the same reason the middle of a stone step is hollow.

I get down on one knee and put my hand flat on it, which achieves nothing.

The pile there is not worn thin. It is worn *down*, compressed to about half the height of the pile at the skirting board, and when I run my palm from the edge into the middle I can feel the change under my hand like the lip of a very shallow bowl.

I have spent four years since then talking to one person who understood carpet, at a trade counter, under a completely invented pretext. What I asked was how long. What he said was that it depends on the fibre and the underlay and the weight of the traffic and that a corridor in a busy office block might show a path in eight or ten years.

Then he said the thing I have never been able to put down. He said: *but that's a lot of different people walking roughly the same way. What you're describing sounds like the same walk.*

---

Because that is what it is, and I see it while I am still on one knee.

It is not the spread of people living somewhere. It is not the wandering, edge-to-edge, doors-both-sides scatter of a corridor that gets used. There is no wear at all in front of the doors. Nobody has stood at the doors. The sixteen thresholds are as thick and unpressed as the day they were laid.

There is one line down the centre, and it goes past every door without stopping.

And it is not the same width all the way.

At the stairwell end it is broad — a metre, maybe more, vague at the edges, the way a path is at the top of a field where everyone comes over the stile at a different angle. And as it runs down the corridor it *narrows*. By the halfway point it is the width of a person. By the last ten metres it is the width of a single set of feet, and it is deeper there than anywhere, and the shine on it under the corridor lights is the shine on the handle of a tool.

They all came in spread out, and they all converged, and they all ended in the same place.

That is what four hundred people look like from above, if you flatten them.

---

I follow it.

I walk on it, which afterwards seems like the worst thing I did in that building and at the time seems like the only respectful way to do it, and my shoes go down into it very slightly, and I count doors as I pass them because I need something to count.

It runs the length of the corridor. It goes past the last two doors and past the last light fitting.

And two metres short of the window, on the left-hand side, it stops.

Not at a door. There is no door there. There is blank papered wall between the last doorframe and the corner, and the track comes down the centre of the corridor and turns very slightly toward that wall and simply ends — flat, definite, the way a path ends at a gate.

I stand at the end of it and look at a piece of wall.

"How long," I say, out loud. My voice comes out wrong. "How long have they been walking this?"

**"Go to the end,"** she says.

"I'm at the end."

**"No,"** she says. **"You are not."**

---

There are two of them standing there.

I do not see them until I am about six metres out, because they are past the last light and the far end of the corridor is dim, and because I have been looking at the floor.

One is the man from the sixth floor — the one who was sliding his hand along the wallpaper and took it off and put it in his pocket and told me he couldn't sleep either. Sixties. Heavy coat, open now. The other is a younger man I have not seen before, holding a phone with the torch on, sweeping the beam slowly along the join between the carpet and the skirting board like someone looking for a dropped earring.

They both look up.

"You," says the older one.

It is not hostile. That is the thing I want on the record about all of them, the whole night: not one person in that building was ever hostile to me. He says it the way you greet someone at the end of a long shift.

"You've been at this a while," I say.

"Longer than you." He straightens up off the wall he has been leaning on. "This is my fourth."

"Fourth night?"

"Fourth *run*," he says, and something in my face must move, because he laughs — one short note, entirely without pleasure. "Oh. You didn't know you could come back." He looks at me almost kindly. "You can come back. They'll have you back as often as you like. That's the part nobody tells you at the start, and by the time you work out what it means you've already said yes to the second one."

The younger one has not stopped sweeping the torch.

"Anything?" the older one says to him.

"Nothing."

---

The older man is leaning on the wall.

I want to be extremely precise here, because of everything that came after, and because for four years people have asked me the same question with the same tilt of the head, which is: *are you sure?*

He is leaning on the left-hand wall, at the point where the track stops, with his shoulder and the whole flat of his upper arm against it. His ankles are crossed. He is entirely at his ease. He is resting the weight of a sixty-year-old man on that wall the way you would rest it on any wall in any corridor anywhere in the world, and he has been doing it, from the look of him, for some minutes.

And there is a door there.

There is no trick to it. There is no seam to find, no catch, no shimmer, nothing that requires me to squint. It is a plain door in a plain frame, painted the same as the other sixteen, with a plain brushed handle at the same height as every other handle on the floor, and the older man's shoulder is against the wall two feet to the left of it, and while I stand there watching, his eyes go across it — over the frame, over the handle, over the door — twice, three times, quartering the wall in a careful professional sweep.

He does not stop at it.

There is no moment where his gaze catches and slides off, which is what I might have expected and what I have heard other people describe about other things. It is simpler and much worse than that. The door is not being hidden from him. It is not being anything to him. He looks straight at a door and goes on looking for something.

---

I stop walking. I am about four metres away.

They both notice.

And I watch it happen on their faces: they look at me, and then they follow where I am looking, and then they look back at me — and the older man comes off the wall and stands up properly, and I can see the whole thought cross him, in order, like weather over a field.

"What," he says. It comes out too fast to be a question. "What is it. What are you looking at."

I don't answer him.

"He's seeing something," says the younger one.

"I know what he's doing." The older man's voice has changed. Not angry — something worse and more naked, the flat urgency of a man who has spent four runs in this building and has just watched somebody arrive tonight and stop walking in the last two metres. "Son. What are you looking at. I've been on this wall for—" and he stops, and he actually looks at the wall, and then at me, and then at the wall again, and the confusion on his face is so complete and so undefended that I feel something in me twist over.

"It's a door," I say.

I do not know why I say it. It is the only genuinely kind thing I do in that building and it costs them nothing and gains them nothing.

"Where."

"You're touching it."

He takes his hand off the wall as though it has gone hot. He puts it back. He slides it sideways — six inches, a foot — over the frame, over the panel, across the handle, and I watch a man's palm pass over a brushed steel handle without registering the slightest change in surface, and his face does not move at all.

"There's nothing here," he says. "There's nothing *here*."

---

I walk between them.

Neither of them stops me, because you cannot stop somebody doing a thing you cannot see them doing. The younger one steps back to let me past out of pure reflex and then makes a small confused sound behind me.

I put my hand on the handle. It is cold and slightly gritty, the way a handle is that nobody has used.

Behind me the older man says, very quietly, and not to me: "Four times."

I turn it and push and go through and I close the door behind me.

The last thing I hear from that corridor is a hand hitting plaster, flat and hard, in exactly the wrong place.
