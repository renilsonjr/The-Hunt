# Chapter 17 — On paper

*Draft 2, new. Fills §2.3: nobody in Act 2 ever tries to tell anyone, despite
writing being explicitly permitted. Third person past, Act 2's register. Sits
between Draft 1's Chapter 7 (Halverton, the platform) and Chapter 8 (the
ancestry kit).*

---

He worked out the loophole on the train back from Halverton, somewhere around the second hour, with his forehead against the glass and the fields going by in the dark.

*She hears what is said aloud.* That was the whole instruction. Not *don't tell anyone* — Anser had been precise, and Anser was always precise, and the precision was the thing Fridan had begun to lean on the way you lean on a bannister. **Write what you like. She doesn't read.**

So he could write it.

He could write all of it, and hand it to a person, and that person could read it in silence in front of him, and not one syllable would cross the air.

It took him nine days to do, and he was never able to decide afterwards whether the nine days were caution or cowardice, and he suspected the honest answer was that they were neither: he had simply needed nine days to work up to being disbelieved by the only person left who would have believed anything.

---

He did it properly. That was the mistake, although it took him a long time to see it that way.

He built the document like a case file, because a case file was what he knew how to build. Twenty-two pages. No adjectives he could avoid. Every claim separated from every inference, the way he had separated them in the notebook: *observed*, *told to me*, *concluded*. He put the weakest material at the front, on the grounds that a reader who has been softened up resents it.

He included the things that told against him. That the voice on the footbridge had never once given him a verifiable fact. That the only physical object in the entire account — a piece of paper in a drawer in Halverton — was in a stranger's handwriting and said four words that could mean anything. That he had no photographs, no recording, no witness, and no explanation for why a man in his condition should be believed about anything.

He wrote out the Vera Lund visit exactly as it happened, including the lie he had told on her doorstep.

And at the end, on page twenty-two, he wrote: *I am not asking you to believe this. I am asking you to keep it, and to date it, and to not be surprised later.*

He printed it and drove to Cass's on the Sunday.

---

She made him eat first, which he had not budgeted for and which cost him forty minutes and most of his nerve.

Her kitchen was warm and loud in the way her house was always loud — the radio on low that nobody was listening to, the dishwasher, a neighbour's dog — and she talked about the hedge dispute, which had gone to letters, and about her eldest's exam timetable, and he sat at her table and made the correct noises and thought about the fact that everything either of them said aloud was arriving instantly somewhere else.

That was new. He had known it for three weeks and it had not stopped being new.

When she cleared the plates he took the envelope out of his bag and put it on the table.

"I need you to read something," he said. "Not now — actually, yes. Now. While I'm here."

Cass looked at the envelope, and then at him.

"Fridan."

"It's twenty-two pages. It'll take you half an hour."

"Fridan, what is this."

"It's what's been going on since August," he said. "I can't tell you. I can only give it to you."

---

She read it at the table with a glass of water she did not touch.

He had planned for this part. He had planned to watch her face and to not watch her face, and he had decided in the car that he would look at his hands, and in the event he did neither: he sat there and watched his sister read twenty-two pages about him, and it was the longest half hour of his life to that point, and the ranking would not last the year.

He knew it had gone wrong at about page four.

Not from anything she did. From the *speed*. She slowed down, in the way people slow down when the text stops being information and starts being evidence about the person who wrote it, and she went back and reread something, and after that she read every page at the same careful pace, all the way to the end, including the pages that were mostly dates.

She squared the pages. She put them down.

"Okay," she said.

And then she did the thing he had not planned for at all, because it had genuinely not occurred to him.

She reached across the table and took his hand.

---

"I'm going to ask you some things," she said, "and I want you to know before I start that I'm not — I'm not doing the thing. I'm not going to say the thing."

"You've read it. You know what's in it."

"I've read twenty-two pages of the most careful writing I have ever seen in my life," said Cass. "That's what I've read." Her thumb moved once across his knuckles. "Are you sleeping?"

"That's in there. Page six."

"I know it's in there. I'm asking you."

"Four hours. Sitting up."

"And eating."

"Cass."

"And you've lost — what, a stone? More?" She was not crying. That was almost worse; she was doing the thing she did in a crisis, which was become extremely calm and extremely organised, and he had watched her do it at their mother's bedside and had been grateful for it then. "And you went to a woman's house in Halverton and lied to her to get inside, and you've written that down yourself, on page fifteen, in your own words, because you thought it was important that I know."

"Because it's true."

"Yes," she said. "That's what worries me."

---

He tried, then. He wanted the record to show that he tried.

He said: "Ask me anything in it. Anything. I'll show you my working."

So she did, and for about ten minutes it went well — better than well; she asked about the timeline and he gave it to her without hesitating, dates and hours, the 14 bus and the man in the green jacket with four stitches along the top edge of a repair, the shape of the phone in his hand, the platform at Halverton, the scar through the man's eyebrow.

And he watched it stop helping.

He could see the exact moment. It was somewhere in the second recitation of a detail nobody could possibly have retained, and what came into her face was not doubt. It was recognition. She had asked him for evidence and he had produced, fluently and at length, the one symptom that would frighten a person most.

"How do you *remember* that," she said quietly.

"That's page two. That's the point. Nothing has gone."

"Fridan—"

"It's not memory. Memory is a thing you make. This was *installed*—"

He heard it as it came out of his mouth.

---

She let go of his hand and sat back.

"Right," she said. "Okay. I want you to do something for me and I want you to do it now, tonight, while I'm sitting here. I want you to tell me. Not the pages. You. Out loud."

And there it was.

He had run the conversation forty times in nine days and had never once run this. He had built the entire approach on the loophole — *she doesn't read* — and had somehow failed to notice that a person who is handed a document by their brother will not accept a document as the end of it. That the document is the beginning. That the whole of what a person wants, at that table, is to hear you say it.

"I can't," he said.

"You can't tell me."

"Not out loud."

"Why not?"

"That's page nineteen."

"I don't want page nineteen," said Cass, and her voice went for the first time. "I want *you*. I want you to look at me and say a sentence about what is happening to you."

He sat in his sister's kitchen with the radio on low and the dishwasher going and a document on the table between them, and he closed his mouth.

He would think about the next forty seconds for years. Not the argument — there was no argument. There was a woman asking her brother to speak, and a man declining, in silence, while she waited.

Forty seconds is a very long time.

---

He said, eventually, "I know how this looks."

"Do you?"

"Yes." He made himself say it plainly, because plainness was all he had left. "It looks like a man who's stopped sleeping and stopped eating, who's built an enormous careful structure, and who won't say the central thing out loud. I know that's the shape. I'd think it too."

"Then say it."

"If I say it, something hears it."

Cass put both hands flat on the table.

"Okay," she said.

That was all. And it was the most frightening thing anybody said to him in the whole of that year, including everything Anser said, because it was the sound of somebody stopping asking.

---

She did not throw him out. She made more tea. She kept the document — he had asked her to date it and she did, in front of him, on the first page, and he was grateful in a way that was out of all proportion to the act.

At the door she said, "I'm going to come and stay for a bit."

"You're not."

"Fridan."

"You've got three kids and a job and a hedge war." He got his coat. "And no."

"Why no?"

And he could not tell her that either, because the answer was that he could not have his sister in the flat while he slept, given what came into the flat while he slept — and that sentence was not a sentence he was permitted to say aloud in a house where she was standing.

"I'll ring you Tuesday," he said.

She looked at him for a long moment on her own doorstep, in the light from the hall, with the document in her hand.

"I asked you," she said. "In August. I said, *are you all right*, and you said, *I'm fine, it's Tuesday.*"

"I was fine. In August I was fine."

"Yes," said Cass. "That's what I keep thinking about."

---

He never tried again.

He wrote that down, later, in the account, in the flat factual register he used for everything by then, and it is the only line in ninety-one pages that anybody has ever accused him of self-pity over:

*I had one person left who would have taken it seriously, and the instruction that was keeping me alive was the same instruction that made me look like a man coming apart, and there is no version of that conversation where I win it. The thing they had done to me was not a secret. It was a shape. Anyone I told would see the shape before they got to the content, and the shape says: sit down, we'll get you some help.*

*I understood then why nobody in four hundred runs has ever been believed. It is not that we are not believed. It is that we cannot be told about — and the difference between those two is the whole of the thing.*

The GP's surgery rang him eleven days later, to offer him an appointment he had not made.

He did not go.
