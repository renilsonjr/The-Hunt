# Chapter 5 — The red rail

*Draft 2. Second of two from Draft 1's Chapter 2. Roadmap 4. First person
present. Carries the speech-not-thought discovery, which is the mechanic the
whole book turns on, and must stay unremarked.*

---

For a moment it is four tracks in a cutting at night.

Then the rail turns red.

Not lit. I have to be careful with the word, because *lit* implies a source, and there is no source: no glow on the ballast beside it, no reflection on the underside of the bridge, nothing on my hands. The near rail of the left-hand track simply *becomes* red, along the whole of its length, out to the limit of what I can see in both directions, in one instant.

There is no moment of becoming. That is the part I have never been able to get across. It is not that it changes quickly. It is that it was one colour and is now another, and there was no interval in which it was doing that, the way a word is one thing and then means another thing after somebody has told you.

It is not hot. There is no shimmer over it.

It is red the way a thing is the colour it is.

My breath goes out of me. I take my hands off the handrail and put them back, and I hear myself make a small noise that I would not have chosen.

"What did you do."

**"That is your route,"** she says. **"Follow it. Not on the track — beside it. There is a service path on the eastern side. You will find a gate; it is not locked tonight."**

"You changed it." My voice goes up. "You changed something *real*."

A pause.

Her pauses are strange. They do not feel like thinking. Thinking has a texture — I know what it is like to be in a room with someone who is working something out. This is nothing like that. Her pauses feel like waiting for something to be permitted.

**"You needed to see it,"** she says.

Which is true, and is not an answer, and I do not notice that for four months.

---

I find the gate.

It is in a run of green palisade fencing at the bottom of the steps, and it is not locked. There is a padlock on it. The padlock is closed and it is hanging from one staple, through nothing.

I stand there with my hand on it for a second, because there is a version of tonight in which that padlock is the thing that stops me, and I can feel the shape of that version standing next to me, and then I open the gate and go through and it is gone.

The service path is a strip of hardcore and weeds between the fence and the ballast, wide enough for one. There are the usual things along it: a cable trough with three sections of lid missing, a stack of concrete sleepers gone green on top, a marker post with a number on it.

I check the number.

It is a real kind of number, in the real format, and it does not mean anything to me, and I understand that I have just done the bus-stop thing again. I have gone looking for the seam in the world by reading the smallest available print, and the smallest available print is correct.

I stop checking things after that.

---

I walk it for what my legs say is an hour and what my head refuses to estimate.

The red rail runs at my left the whole way. Steady. At the exact edge of my vision.

And I learn something within the first few hundred metres that governs the rest of the night: I must not look at it directly for long.

When I do, the wrongness comes up through my chest like cold water going into a sleeve — not fear exactly, something more physical and more stupid than fear, the body's flat refusal of a thing it has no category for. And when I keep it at the edge instead, it is simply a red line, and I can follow it, and following it is easy.

Being easy is the entire point. I understand that much later and it does not help.

The cutting shallows out. The fence changes from palisade to chain-link and then back. Twice a train goes past on the far tracks and both times the noise is completely ordinary and both times I stand still and let it, because the ordinariness of it is the only comfortable thing available.

Neither train has anybody in it. I look. The windows are lit and the seats are empty and it is going somewhere at seventy miles an hour in the middle of the night, and after the second one I stop looking at trains as well.

---

She talks to me.

Not constantly. In intervals, and always to purpose.

**"There is a fence in ninety metres. Go over it at the post, not at the panel."**

I go over it at the post. The panel would have folded; I can see that once I am on top of it, and I would not have seen it from the ground.

**"The path narrows. Keep to the left."**

It narrows. There is a drop on the right that I do not see until I am past it.

**"You are doing this correctly."**

That one undoes something in me.

I want it on the record, because of what it says about the state I was in and because I have never been able to explain it to anybody who has not been alone in the cold at four in the morning: a voice that will not tell me its name, in a city with no name, tells me that I am doing a thing correctly — a thing I do not understand, for a reason I have not been given — and something in my chest comes loose, and I am *grateful*, and I am ashamed of how much I want to hear it again.

I walk faster for about ten minutes afterwards. I notice myself doing it. I do not stop.

---

Somewhere in the second mile I get careless.

I think a question instead of saying it.

There is no reason for this. It is not a test; the idea of testing her has not entered my head and would not for weeks. I am tired and my mouth is cold and the question is one I have asked twice already in other forms, and I simply think it, hard, the way you press a bruise:

*What is it that I am looking for. What does it actually look like.*

And I wait.

Nothing.

I keep walking. The fence goes by. It does not occur to me, at the time, that anything has happened.

Half a mile later I say it aloud, into the cold, to the rail: "What does it look like?"

**"You will know it,"** she says — immediately, warm as ever, no gap at all. **"There is a bridge in four hundred metres. Go under, not over."**

I stop walking.

---

I stand on a strip of weeds beside a red rail in a city with no name and I turn the two things over.

The thought that went nowhere.

The words that were answered before I had finished saying them.

She had not heard me think it.

I get exactly that far. I want to be precise about how far, because for four years people have wanted me to have understood more than I did, and I did not: I get to *she cannot hear my thoughts, only my speech*, which is a complete and correct observation, and I hold it in my hand and look at it.

And the next sentence — the one that asks what kind of thing hears speech and not thought, and why a thing like that would need me to walk somewhere, and what it means that everything she has said tonight has been a direction and never once a fact —

that sentence does not arrive.

Something in me declines to build it.

It is not that I decide against it. There is no argument, no moment of *I'd rather not think about that*. The shape of the question forms, and my mind puts it down, gently, without comment, the way you set down a glass you have decided not to drink from.

And I look up, and there is the bridge, four hundred metres, exactly as she said.

I go under, not over.

---

The second building is on the far side of a road I do not remember crossing.

It is tall and unlit for most of its height. There is a canopy over the entrance and light coming out of the ground floor in a long spill across wet paving, and it is the middle of the night. It has been the middle of the night for hours. The night here does not seem to be going anywhere.

The red rail ends thirty metres short of the doors.

Not fading — *ending*, at a point, the way a road ends at a wall. On one side of that point the rail is red and on the other side it is the colour of rail, and there is no transition, and standing over the join I have the strong and unhelpful thought that if I put my finger on it I would feel an edge.

The route is used up.

I stand at the end of it and look at the doors, and I can see people inside. Four or five of them, standing about in a lobby in the middle of the night. Not sitting. Not talking much. Waiting the way staff wait.

They are expecting somebody.

"Is it in there?" I say.

**"Yes."**

---

The first straight answer of the entire night.

It lands in me like a coin dropping into water and every part of me lifts. I am aware of my own heart. I am, for about two seconds, as happy as I have ever been.

And I should ask her something else.

I know that now and I half know it then. Standing at the end of a rail she made red, thirty metres from a lit door with five people behind it who have been waiting in an empty lobby at three in the morning for someone to arrive — there is a question here, and it is not *is it in there*.

It is *how do they know I am coming*.

**"They are expecting you,"** she says, warm, unhurried, as though it were good news.

**"Go in."**

I go in.
