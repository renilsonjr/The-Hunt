# Chapter 7 — The ones who are guessing

*Draft 2. Second of two from Draft 1's Chapter 3. Roadmap 6. First person
present. Completes every Act 1 split.*

---

It is a strange kind of searching and I get better at it as the night goes on.

I am not looking for a small object in a building with two hundred rooms. That would be hopeless, and I would have given up somewhere on the seventh floor, and I understood the hopelessness of it in the first ten minutes and had to find something else to do instead.

What I am looking for is the *feeling*.

The apple in the bowl. The hand closing. The **there** in my chest. That is the only instrument I have, and the rooms are not the target — they are the medium the feeling might arrive through, and my job is to move through as many of them as possible with the instrument switched on.

So I walk. I open the doors that open. I stand in the middle of empty function rooms with my eyes shut, waiting to be pulled, and I feel like an idiot every time and I do it in eleven rooms in a row.

Nine is flats. Eight is flats. Seven is something else — meeting rooms, a long room with a projector in the ceiling and forty stacked chairs, a kitchen with an urn in it that is warm. I put my hand on the urn twice because it is the only warm object I have touched since the lobby.

Six is where I start seeing the others.

---

Twice, before that, she says something.

**"Not this floor."**

And, much later, so quietly that it is almost not there: **"Slower."**

That is all. In four hours.

She has gone thin in here. On the rail she was a warm room; in this building she is a voice through a wall in a house you are not in, and I find myself talking out loud in empty corridors to keep hold of her — *still there? anything? which way?* — and getting, almost always, nothing at all.

I do not read anything into it at the time. I put it down to the building. I am a man in a building that is bigger inside than out; if the voice in my head is faint, that is one more property of the building, like the clocks.

It does not occur to me that there might be something in here she does not want to be loud in front of.

---

The first one is a man at the end of a corridor on six with his hand flat against the wall.

He is walking very slowly, sliding his palm along the paper as he goes, the way you would feel for a hidden door in a film. When he sees me he takes his hand off the wall and puts it in his pocket in one movement and says, "Can't sleep either," and walks past me toward the stairs.

He is the man with the heavy ring. I do not recognise him for four or five seconds, because he has taken his coat off, and because a man with his hand on a wall in an empty corridor is not the same object as a man with a glass in a warm room.

He does not acknowledge that we have met.

---

The second is on five.

A woman standing on a chair, with a ceiling tile pushed up and out of its frame, her arm in the void to the shoulder. She hears me and looks over her shoulder without hurrying, and says:

"Bulb's gone."

There is no light fitting within four metres of her. The nearest one is behind her and it is on.

I say, "Right," and go past, and she goes back to putting her arm into the ceiling.

---

The third does not see me at all.

He is in an empty office on four with all the drawers of a filing cabinet open, and he is going through the hanging files one at a time, from the front, lifting each one and letting it fall. There is nothing in them. I can see from the doorway that there is nothing in them; the whole cabinet is empty and he is going through it file by file by file.

He is perhaps twenty-five. He has been crying at some point and has stopped.

I stand in that doorway for longer than I should and then I go away without saying anything, and I have never been able to decide whether that was cowardice or the only decent thing available.

---

By the fourth floor I have stopped pretending I do not see them, and they have stopped pretending they are not looking.

What settles between us is not hostility.

It is much worse than hostility. It is the mutual professional courtesy of people doing the same job on different contracts — the nod you give another electrician on a site. Two of them say goodnight to me. One holds a door.

And they are searching *badly*.

That is the thing I keep coming back to, on the stairs between three and four with my legs going. They have been in this building longer than me and they know it better than me and I have watched five of them work now, and every one of them is doing the same thing, which is looking in places where a small object could physically be.

Cupboards. Ceiling voids. Behind radiators. Under the lip of a desk, along the back of a drawer runner, inside the cistern of a lavatory on the fifth floor, which I know because the lid was off and leaning against the wall.

They are working through the *set of hiding places*. Methodically. Exhaustively. Sixty or seventy years of accumulated technique between them, and it is the technique of people who have been told there is something in a building and have no other information at all.

They are guessing.

They are guessing, and I am being *pulled*, and the difference between those two is not a difference of degree. It is the difference between a man reading a map and a man with a compass, and it has taken me until the fourth floor at five in the morning to notice that I am the only one holding a compass.

---

The woman in the grey suit finds me on two.

I am sitting on the bottom step of a stairwell with my elbows on my knees. I have been sitting there for some minutes because my legs have stopped being reliable and because I have run out of floors.

She sits down beside me.

It is companionable. She hitches the trousers of a good suit and sits on a concrete step next to a man who has been walking for nine hours, and it is the least threatening thing anybody has done all night, which is precisely why every part of me goes still.

"You've been up all night," she says.

"So has everyone."

"Mm." She looks at her hands. She is not holding the lanyard now. "Can I ask you something? It's not a test."

"That's what somebody would say before a test."

She laughs — and the laugh is real, I would swear to it under any conditions you like. It is the only completely unguarded noise anybody makes in that building.

Then she says: "What are you looking for?"

---

The corridor light hums. Somewhere above us a door closes and does not open again.

And it arrives in one piece, complete, the way the certainty came in the first building — except that this comes from *me*, and I know the difference now. I have spent all night learning the difference. That is how I know this one is mine.

*She does not know.*

Not *she is testing whether I will admit it*. Not *she wants to hear how I say it*.

She does not know what it looks like.

None of them do. Five people waiting in a lobby at three in the morning. A man going through an empty filing cabinet one hanging file at a time. A woman with her arm in a ceiling void telling a stranger a bulb has gone. And a woman in a grey suit sitting down on a concrete step at five in the morning next to the man who arrived last, tonight, off a street, to ask him **what are you looking for** —

because they need somebody to say it out loud.

They cannot find it.

I can. That is what I am. That is what the coat was, and the certainty was, and the timetable at the bus stop was, and the flat with no post in it — I am not a competitor who was generously admitted at the last minute to keep the rest of them honest.

I am the instrument they could not build, walked into the building and pointed at the floor.

---

I sit with it. It takes perhaps four seconds.

Then I say, "I'll know it when I see it."

Which is true. And which is *hers* — it is the sentence she gave me on a footbridge four hours ago, handed back to a woman in a grey suit on a concrete step — and it is the first thing I have said all night that I chose for what it would do to somebody else.

She nods slowly, like that is fair. She takes it the way you take a card you were expecting to be dealt.

Then she pats the step twice as she gets up, the way you would say *well, anyway*.

"Third floor's nice," she says. "Quiet."

She goes up. I sit on the step.

---

And here is the part I would give a great deal to be able to explain, afterwards, to anybody who asks me how it happened — because everybody asks, in the end, and they all ask it the same way, with that particular tilt of the head.

I knew.

On that step, at that hour, with my legs shaking from nine floors of stairs, I knew that I was being used. By people who had been in my life without entering it. To find a thing they wanted and could not reach, in a building where four hundred people before me had failed to find it, and where nobody had ever once tried to stop me doing anything.

I had every piece. I have gone through it a thousand times looking for the piece I was missing and there is no missing piece. I was not deceived on that step. I was, for about ninety seconds, the best-informed man in the building.

And I got up and went to the third floor.

Because I wanted it.

Not what they wanted. Not what she wanted. **It.** The thing at the end of the pull — the only thing that had ever been meant for me specifically, in thirty-four years of a life in which nothing had ever been meant for me specifically at all. I had been walking toward it since a room full of coats and I was closer to it than anyone had ever been.

That is the whole of my defence and I know exactly how it sounds.

I got up off the step. My knees did something audible.

The third floor is quiet.
