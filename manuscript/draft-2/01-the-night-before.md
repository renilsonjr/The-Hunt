# Chapter 1 — The night before

*Draft 2, new. Replaces the cold open as the book's first chapter; Draft 1's
Chapter One becomes Chapter Two and loses nothing. First person present, like
the rest of Fridan's document — he writes this part last, looking for the seam.*

---

I have gone over the evening of the twenty-fifth of August more times than I have gone over any other day of my life, and there is nothing in it.

That is the first thing I want written down, because everyone who has ever taken this seriously has asked me the same question in the same careful voice — *was there anything unusual?* — and the answer is no, and the no is the most frightening part. If there had been a stranger on the platform, a wrong number, a light in the flat opposite, I could have said: there. That is the joint. That is where the world was opened and closed again.

There is no joint. It is an ordinary Tuesday all the way through, and it goes ordinarily into the dark, and then I am standing in a room with thirty people in coats.

So this is the Tuesday.

---

I schedule buses.

That is not what it says on anything, but it is what it is. I work in service planning for the city network — the timetables, the layover allowances, the four minutes you build in at the top of a route so that one late passenger at the second stop does not put the whole afternoon out. It is unglamorous and I have never once been able to explain to anybody why I like it. The closest I have got is that a timetable is a promise made to strangers by somebody they will never meet, and that when it is built properly nobody notices it at all.

I am good at it. I want that written down too, because of everything afterwards. I was thirty-four and I was good at an ordinary job.

On the twenty-fifth I spend the morning on the 14, which is our worst route, because it runs the length of the ring road and picks up the hospital, and nothing you do to the northbound timing survives contact with a Friday. I move two minutes out of the layover at Halverton Road and into the run between the hospital and the flyover. I remember the shape of the spreadsheet. I remember thinking it would hold for about a fortnight.

At half eleven a woman called Priya asks me whether I want anything from the place on the corner and I say a coffee and then change it to a tea, and she says, "You always do that," and I say, "I know," and that is the entire conversation. It is the last completely weightless exchange I have with another human being, and I did not know that, and there is no version of a life where you would.

---

I leave at six. The evening is warm and going gold and the parking structure opposite my building has that hour on it where the concrete goes the colour of a paper bag.

Cass rings while I am walking up from the stop.

She is my sister. She is two years older and she rings on Tuesdays, which started when our mother was ill and never stopped after, and neither of us has ever mentioned that it started then. We talk for eleven minutes. She tells me about a dispute with a neighbour over a hedge, in detail, with dates. I tell her about the 14 and the two minutes, and she says, "Nobody will ever know you did that," and I say, "That's the job," and she laughs.

Near the end she says: "Are you all right? You sound flat."

And I say, "I'm fine. It's Tuesday."

I have thought about those two sentences for a long time. There is nothing in them. She asked because she asks, and I answered because I was fine. But it is the last time anybody asks me that question and gets a true answer, and when I finally tell her — a year later, badly, in a hospital car park — the first thing she says is *I asked you, I asked you that night*, as though a question could have held a door.

---

I eat at the counter, standing up, because I live alone and the table has post on it.

Afterwards I do the washing up, which takes four minutes, and I put the radio on and then off again. I water the one plant. I look at my phone for longer than I would admit and put it face down.

And then — this is the part I always come back to, because if there is anything at all in that evening it is this, and it is nothing —

I take a book off the shelf.

It is one I have read. I know exactly which one and I am not going to write the title, because when I said it out loud once the person I was talking to went and bought it, and looked for something in it, and there is nothing in it. It is a paperback with a white crease down the spine that I put there myself four years ago on a train to the coast, forcing it flat one-handed because I had a coffee in the other, and the crease has annoyed me every time I have seen it since.

I sit in the chair by the window and read eleven pages and do not take any of it in, and I put it face-down on the low table, splayed open, spine up, the way I have been told my whole life not to leave a book.

I leave it like that.

I want to be exact here, because exactness is the only thing I have: on the evening of the twenty-fifth of August I left a paperback face-down on a table by a window in my flat in Nor Yesey, at an angle, open at a page I had not really read.

Nobody saw me do it. I have checked everything a person can check. There is no camera on that room and no window opposite it above the second floor and no one in the building I had told about the book, because it is a book, and you do not tell people about a book.

Eleven days later I stood in a furnished flat on the ninth floor of a building that does not exist, in a city with no name, and there it was on the low table by the window, face-down, spine up, creased white in the one place, open at the page I had stopped at.

I have had four years and a hospital stay and a great many nights to arrive at the right answer to that, which is that they did not watch me and did not need to. But the wrong answer got there first and it got there in a warm kitchen with my hands flat on a counter, and the wrong answer is the reason I did everything I did afterwards, and I would like the record to show what it felt like to be an ordinary man arriving at it.

---

I go to bed at about half eleven.

The window is open because it is August. The parking structure hums the way it hums. Somewhere below, a car door, and a man's voice saying something short to somebody, and a laugh that is not about anything.

I set an alarm for seven. I do not remember getting into bed, which is not sinister; nobody remembers getting into bed.

I remember lying there for a while thinking about the northbound run and whether two minutes was enough, and deciding it probably was not, and that I would look at it again on Wednesday.

That is the last thought I ever had that was entirely my own, and it was about a bus.

Then I go under, the ordinary way — the way you have gone under every night of your life without once being asked whether you agreed to it.
