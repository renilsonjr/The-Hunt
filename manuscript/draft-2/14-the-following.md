# Chapter 14 — The following

*Draft 2. First of two from Draft 1's Chapter 6, which carried roadmap 13–14
in one chapter. Roadmap 13. Third person past. Overlaps Chapter 13's eleven
days and says so in its first line.*

---

The following had started on the sixth day, or he had noticed it on the sixth day, which are not the same thing and he was careful about the distinction from the beginning.

There was a man on the 14 bus, in the seat across the aisle and one back. Dark green jacket with a repair on the left cuff — a rectangle of slightly wrong thread, four stitches visible along the top edge where whoever did it had run out of patience.

He had also been standing outside the pharmacy on the corner of Fridan's street on the Sunday, four days earlier, in the same jacket, holding a phone in his left hand at chest height with the screen dark.

Fridan knew this the way he knew the layout of his own flat.

---

That was the entire problem, and he saw it immediately, sitting on the 14 with his bag on his knees.

A normal man notices a face and thinks *have I seen him before?* — and cannot answer. And the not-answering is what keeps him sane. The question dissolves because there is nothing to hold it up. By the evening he has forgotten he asked it.

Fridan did not have that.

He had four stitches along the top edge of a repair. He had the exact angle of a dark phone screen at chest height. He had it with the flat, total, unfading availability of everything since the twenty-sixth, which meant the question *am I imagining this* was not available to him at all.

And neither, he realised somewhere around the flyover, was the question he actually needed.

Because *am I imagining this* was the wrong question. He was not imagining the man. The man was a fact. The question he needed was *am I imagining that it means something* — and for that, certainty was no use whatsoever.

He had been given perfect recall and it had cost him doubt.

Doubt, it turned out, had been doing an enormous amount of work. It had been quietly disposing of nine-tenths of everything he had ever noticed, all his life, without ever telling him it was doing it. Now nothing was disposed of. Everything he saw stayed, at full weight, forever, and he had to adjudicate all of it himself.

---

There were four more over the next five days.

A woman twice, at different stations, eleven hours apart. Fifties, dark red coat, a canvas bag with a hard corner in it. He could not decide whether a city of nine million was large enough to make that a coincidence or small enough to make it one, and he did the arithmetic three separate ways and got three answers he did not trust.

A car — the same registration on the Tuesday and the Thursday, parked in two different streets, both within four hundred metres of his building.

A man in the supermarket who looked at him a half-second too long, and then, worse, looked away with real embarrassment. Like someone caught.

And a woman at the barrier at the station on the Friday who did not look at him at all, and stood in a position from which she could not comfortably see anything else.

He wrote them all down.

The list, read back, was either the most frightening document he had ever produced or clinical evidence about the man who had written it, and he could not tell which, and it was written in handwriting that got smaller toward the bottom of each page.

---

On the ninth day he ran a test.

He was almost cheerful about it, in the flat unpleasant way of a man who has finally found something in his own competence to reach for. He did not know how to establish whether a god was speaking to him. He did know how to establish whether somebody was following him on a bus network, because he had spent eleven years building the thing.

He designed a route.

Not a random one — random was useless, random could be coincidence. He built a journey that no reasonable person would ever make: the 14 southbound to the interchange, then the 6 back *northbound* for two stops, then off, then the 22 east, which ran a loop that returned within three hundred metres of where he had started. Forty-one minutes to end up where he began. Nobody does that. There is no errand that produces that shape.

And it was a shape only somebody with the timetable could follow smoothly — which is to say that anybody tailing him would either fall behind at the interchange, where the 6 left four minutes after the 14 arrived, or would have to have planned for it.

He ran it at half past four on a Thursday.

The man in the green jacket was not there. The woman in the red coat was not there. Nobody was on both the 14 and the 22. He checked every face on the 22 twice and photographed the top deck with his phone at his hip, badly, and looked at the photographs that night and there was nobody in them he had seen before.

He got home at twenty past five having proved nothing at all.

---

That was the part he had not budgeted for, and it took him until about midnight to understand what had happened to him.

He had designed a good test. It was a genuinely good test; he would defend it now. And a good test that comes back negative should reduce your belief, and this one had not reduced anything, because he had immediately — instantly, without noticing himself do it — produced the reason.

*They knew I would do that.*

Or: *they only follow on certain days.* Or: *they were on the 22 and got off before I looked.* Or: *the point is not to follow me, it is to be seen occasionally, and they took a day off.*

He sat at the kitchen table at midnight with the photographs on his phone and the list in front of him and wrote, in the notebook, in the small handwriting:

*Ran a control. Negative. I do not believe the result. Note that.*

Then, underneath, after some time:

*A structure that cannot be disconfirmed is not a structure. It is a symptom. I know that. I have known that since I was nineteen years old.*

*I still think there was a man on the bus.*

---

By the eleventh day he had stopped going out except to work.

He was sleeping four hours in pieces, sitting up, with the lamp on. Twice he had lost a word mid-sentence in front of other people, and the second time it was a common word — an ordinary word, one of the two hundred you use every day — and the woman he was speaking to had waited for it with the particular kindness people reserve for a colleague who is visibly coming apart.

He had made the decision the day before, and written out the conditions, and the day between deciding and doing had been the worst one yet.

At about eleven that night he turned the lamp off, put his phone face down across the room, and lay flat on his back the way he had been lying at 6:41 on the twenty-sixth.

The window was open. The parking structure hummed.

He said, out loud, to the ceiling, in the level voice he had been using for eleven days:

"I'm going to sleep now. If that's where you are, I'll be there."

He was asleep in about nine minutes, which was faster than he had gone under in a year.

And something was waiting.
