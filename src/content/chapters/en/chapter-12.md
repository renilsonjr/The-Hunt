The sentence would not come right, and it took him three weeks to understand that this was because the sentence was not the problem.

He drafted forty-one versions. He drafted it plain and he drafted it pointed, the way Anser wanted, with the instant in it and the anchor and the place it ran from; he drafted it as a warning and as a coordinate and once, at four in the morning, as a single word. He read them back and every one of them was correct and none of them was going to work, and he could not say why, and the not being able to say why went on long enough that he began to think the tiredness had finally got into the part of him that did the thinking.

It came apart for him on the Tuesday, in the launderette again, which he had started going to on purpose because it was warm and because something about it had once been lucky.

He had been thinking about the drum. About rain.

And the thing he had missed in January, sitting right in the middle of the good idea, was this: rain is *made of the world she is standing in*. A million drops falling at once is still weather. She had spent four months not attending to a man in a kitchen, and she had not attended to him because attending to human speech from Earth 1 was a thing she had decided against — and a discontinuity assembled out of several million pieces of exactly that material was still, at bottom, the thing she had decided against, arriving in a novel shape.

Louder would not do it. Sharper would not do it. Simultaneous would not do it.

*Different material* would do it.

And he sat in a launderette in February with his hands flat on his knees and went back, for the ten thousandth time, to the mechanism that had been used on him — and saw it properly for the first time, from the other end.

They had not made their signal loud. Their signal had been one man, asleep, in New Jersey. It had been the quietest possible thing. It had worked because of what it was *made of*: a fragment of her brother, sitting in a human, acting as an anchor — a thing she had no law against and no capacity to read, arriving inside the only channel she keeps open.

They had not shouted at her. They had spoken to her in a material she could not refuse.

So: what is the material she cannot refuse?

Not human speech. She has ruled on that.

Her own.

---

He put it together over two days and the shape of it was so clean it frightened him.

If a fragment of Balian in an Earth 1 human made an anchor that Earth 2 could speak through — then somewhere on the other side there had to be the mirror. There would have to be. Not for symmetry's sake, not because the universe is tidy, but because they had done this by *taking a piece of a god and putting it where it should not be*, and there had been two gods in that room, and only one of them had been careful.

A Virden. Alive now. Carrying a dormant fragment of Zalian.

And a fragment of Zalian, waking up on the far side of the Barrier and calling — that is not noise. That is not a message from Earth 1 that she may choose to attend to or not. That is her own signature arriving from a place her signature categorically cannot be: from inside the world she sealed, in the material she is made of, across a wall she built herself and has never once had reason to doubt.

You can ignore a man on a roof. You can ignore several million men on several million roofs.

You cannot ignore *yourself, calling from somewhere you have never been.*

It was the discontinuity he had been after since January. He had simply had it made of the wrong stuff.

---

He did not tell Anser.

That was a decision, and it took about ninety seconds, and it was the largest one he ever made. Because Anser had records — Anser had told him so, casually, months ago — and one question would have got him an answer, and the answer would have arrived in a warm level voice inside a night, and he would have had it by Thursday.

And whoever else was listening at that end would have known, by Thursday, exactly what Fridan of Nor Yesey had worked out.

*They do not want her warned. They want her to look.* It was still the only entry in the third column, and he had spent five weeks pretending it was a mood.

So he went in himself.

---

It should not have been possible and he knew that, and he did it anyway, and the reason he could do it at all is the reason none of it was ever fair.

The thing that had been put into him in August had not been a gift and it had not been an accident. It was a residue: whatever had come through the sphere while the current ran the other way, embedded in him, unasked for, unusable, the way a house near a foundry ends up with iron in the dust. Anser had shown him where it lived, once, in December, in the long architecture of himself. He had not gone back since. He had been afraid of it the way you are afraid of a room in your own house.

He went down on the Friday night, deliberately, alone, without telling anything.

He was not built for it. That was the whole of the experience and he never found a better way to put it: he was a man doing, by hand, with a residue, something that had been done to him by an infrastructure. It was like reading a language you have never learned by recognising the shape of four words in it. He found the edges of something enormous and moved along them the way you move along a wall in the dark, and twice something very large went past him without noticing him, and the second time he understood that not being noticed was the only reason he was still there.

He came out of it at twenty past two on the Saturday afternoon.

He had gone down on Friday at eleven at night.

There was blood on the pillow from his nose and both his eyes were bloodshot from edge to edge, which lasted nine days, and for about a week afterwards words would occasionally arrive in the wrong order and he would have to stop mid-sentence and rebuild them. He lost four pounds in three days. He did not go to work for a fortnight and told them, truthfully, that he had been unwell.

And he had it.

---

Not much. A record. Not even a whole one — a fragment of a fragment, the way you come back from a fire with one photograph.

There was a designation, and it was a long one, and inside it there was a name, and the name was *Uxies*.

Female. Virden. Alive. Fifty-one of their years, which he could not convert with any confidence. A city he could not pronounce and could see the shape of, and the shape of it was towers and weather.

The record was not a dossier. It was worse than a dossier. It was a *log* — the flat, unbroken, uninterpreted observational record of one person's entire life, going back to her birth, kept by something that had never once approached her.

He could only read pieces. Most of it was in structures he had no purchase on. What came through was the banal part, which is always the part that gets you: an address, changed four times. A trade of some sort. A period of illness at nineteen. Two relationships, logged and ended. Sixteen thousand entries of nothing at all.

And a recurring line, tagged and re-tagged across three decades, that he read eleven times before he understood he was going to be able to think about nothing else for the rest of his life.

She went to a place where the noise was least.

That was all it said, or all he could get out of it. There was a location, industrial, on the edge of the city, of no interest to anyone — and she went there, and she had been going there since she was nine, and she stood in it, and did nothing, and went home. Four hundred and eleven logged occurrences. No stated purpose. No known association. Under *assessment*, across thirty years, across presumably many different observers, the same single word each time, the meaning of which he was fairly sure he had reconstructed correctly:

*Unexplained.*

She had been going to the quietest place she could find, alone, for forty-two years, in a world Anser described as a permanent argument — and nobody had ever asked her why, because you do not ask a specimen questions.

She did not know what she was. There was nothing in the record that suggested anybody had ever told her.

---

The last thing he understood, sitting up in bed on the Sunday with his eyes full of blood and the notebook on his knees, was why they had never used her.

They knew. The log went back fifty-one years; they had known her whole life. They had a woman carrying a fragment of the god they were robbing, and they had done *nothing* with her, and the reason was not mercy and it was not incompetence.

It was that she was the only thing in either galaxy that could tell.

Everything else about the operation was quiet: a man in New Jersey who wakes up rested, a fishery, an industry with seasonal variation. She was the single component that, if it ever went off, could not be walked back. So they had put a watch on her at birth and had spent half a century making sure that absolutely nothing ever happened to her.

Fridan sat with that.

He had read a document like this before. It had been his own — somewhere, in some structure he had never seen, there was a log of a boy on a bicycle and a first flat and a book creased on a train, kept flat and uninterpreted for thirty-four years, with a great deal of nothing in it and one tagged anomaly. There was one for Vera Lund of fourteen Cowl Street, Halverton, and it had a fortnight in it in the August, and a note in her own handwriting.

They had all been read their whole lives by something that never spoke to them.

And now there was a fourth reader.

---

He put the notebook down and sat in his flat in Nor Yesey with the window open onto the last of the February cold, and made himself say the shape of it in the plainest words available, because he had promised himself in January that he would always do that.

To stop it, she has to be told. To tell her, the message has to be made of her. The only piece of her within reach of anything he can do is asleep in a body on the other side of the Barrier, in a woman who has never been spoken to, who does not know what she carries, who has spent her whole life walking to the quietest place she can find because something in her is trying to hear something she has no way to name.

And he would have to go into her sleep without asking. There is no version where he asks. Asking requires reaching her while she is awake, and the only channel that crosses is the one they used on him, and it opens on a sleeping mind and nothing else.

He would have to do it to her exactly the way it had been done to him. In the dark. Uninvited. Because of what she is rather than who she is, using a piece of a god in her body as a doorknob, for reasons that would be entirely convincing to everybody except her.

For seven months Fridan had been unable to understand how anyone could have done that to a person.

Sitting on his bed on that Sunday afternoon, he found he had the beginning of an answer, and it was the worst thing that had ever happened to him.
