The bottom of it was a Thursday in the middle of January and there was nothing dramatic about it.

He did not do anything to himself. He had thought about the parapet on the roof in an abstract, administrative way for several weeks, the way you think about a door you have no intention of opening, and the thinking had frightened him less than he expected, which frightened him more.

What actually happened on the Thursday was that he stood in a supermarket for eleven minutes holding a box of teabags, entirely unable to work out what came next, while people went round him.

He put the teabags back on the shelf and went home and sat on the floor of the hall with his coat on until it got dark.

And then, at some point that evening, for no reason he could ever identify and certainly not because anything got better, he got up and found the notebook and began, for the first time since August, to work.

---

He did it properly. That was the only thing he had left that was any good — he had once been competent, in a small way, at taking a mess and giving it an order — and he had a mess of unprecedented quality.

He rebuilt the seven pages into something like a file. Everything he had, sorted not by when it happened but by what kind of fact it was: things he had observed himself; things Anser had told him; things he had inferred. He was ruthless about the third column and it turned out to be almost empty, and the almost-emptiness of it was the first honest thing he had felt in two months.

Then he took the roof apart.

He had it whole. He would have it whole when he was eighty. So he went through it the way you go through footage, and he stopped where he had been stopping for weeks without letting himself look properly, and he made himself look properly, and there were two things there.

The first was the halt. The wheel on the stone. Not revulsion — he had spent nine weeks calling it revulsion because revulsion was survivable and what it actually resembled was worse. And behind the halt, going through and past him, the thing he had settled for calling grief.

You do not grieve a contaminated object. You grieve something you have lost, which means it was yours, which means she had thought of him as *hers*.

The second thing was the sentence.

**"You are not the one I was speaking to."**

He wrote it out at the top of a clean page and sat looking at it.

Not *you are not what you were.* Not *you are not human.* Not *you are unclean* — and she was, in her way, an economical speaker; she had said seventeen sentences to him in his entire life and not one had contained a word doing no work.

*The one I was speaking to.* A person. A specific, particular person she had been in the middle of talking to.

The man in the dream.

---

It took him three days to get the rest of it and it arrived, in the end, from the wrong direction entirely — from the four months of kitchens.

Because she had heard those. He had proof: she had come at the first sentence on the roof, in under a second, from wherever a thing like that stands. Which meant the speech had been reaching her the entire time — while he washed cups, while he sat on a roof at five in the morning saying *please* — all of it, arriving, for four months.

And she had not been ignoring a channel. She had not shut anything. There was nothing to shut.

She hears speech. She hears everybody's speech. That is not a line running from a man to a god that can be cut; it is a property of the universe she made, like the fact that things fall — every word said aloud by every person alive, arriving, always, all of it at once, and always had been, and he had spent four months of pain thinking he had been unplugged when the truth was much simpler and much worse:

She was still hearing every word he said.

She had decided not to listen.

---

**"That is a distinction without a difference,"** Anser said. **"It reaches her and produces nothing. Functionally you are cut off."**

"It's the whole difference." Fridan was walking, coat on, along the road under the parking structure at half past eleven at night, because he had discovered that he thought better on his feet and because his flat had become a place he could only sit down in. He was talking under his breath, which was ridiculous, and he had stopped caring. "A wire that's cut needs a new wire. A decision needs a reason. Nobody has ever needed a reason to ignore me before."

**"She looked at you and found something not hers in the middle of you."**

"Then she'd have been angry, or she'd have been frightened, or she'd have done something about it. She's a god, and she found a piece of her brother in a man on a roof, and her entire response was to stop and go quiet and leave." He came to the end of the road and turned. "That's not disgust. I know what she felt. I was inside it."

**"You were inside it for one second and you have wanted it to mean something ever since."**

That stopped him on the pavement.

It was well aimed and it was probably true and it was, he noticed, the first time in five months that Anser had said something to him that was not useful.

"Say I'm right," he said. "Say it wasn't about me at all. What does she do, if she's just found out something is using her, and she can't see how, and she knows they got in through a man she can see perfectly well?"

Nothing.

"She stops looking at him," said Fridan, to the underside of a parking structure. "Because whatever's on the other end of this is reading *her*, and anything she pays attention to, they get for free. She's been watching me my whole life and never once thought about it, and the second it mattered she worked out that her attention is the leak."

**"That is an enormous amount of architecture to build on one facial expression from a being that does not have a face."**

"Yes," Fridan agreed. "It is."

And he went on walking, and he did not take it back, and somewhere in that thirty seconds — he located it precisely, years later — he stopped being a man who was being told things.

---

The problem, stated cleanly, was this.

She hears everything said aloud, by everyone, permanently. She attends to a vanishing fraction of it. She has decided, for reasons he could not verify and had chosen to believe were protective, that Fridan of Nor Yesey is a thing she will not attend to.

He could not make himself louder. Volume was not the axis; she was not far away and never had been.

He could not make himself special. Special was exactly the property that had been withdrawn.

He sat with that for two weeks and got nowhere, and then it came to him in a launderette on a Sunday afternoon, watching a drum go round, in the form of a memory of a sound.

Rain on a roof.

You can hear rain, or you can hear a single drop, and they are not the same faculty. Nobody attends to a raindrop. But if every drop that fell on a roof for one second fell at the *same instant* — if the whole of the noise arrived as one event — you would not need to be listening. You would not be able to not notice. It would stop being noise and become an *object*, and the noticing would not be a decision anybody made.

She was not deaf to him. She was standing in weather.

So do not send her a message. Send her a discontinuity.

---

He worked it for eleven days and the shape of it held up.

Not many people saying many things. Many people saying *one* thing, in one sentence, at one instant, across a whole world — the same words, spoken aloud, simultaneously. It did not need to be everyone. He did the arithmetic on the back of an envelope and then more carefully and the number that came out was large and not absurd; it was the size of a broadcast audience, the size of a countdown, the size of a stadium multiplied by a few thousand.

And it did not need her to care about Fridan, which was the beautiful part. It needed nothing from her at all. It would arrive in the only faculty she was not permitted to switch off, as a shape in the rain that no ignoring could survive, and inside the shape would be the sentence, and the sentence would be the warning.

He was happier for about four days than he had been since August.

Then he sat down to work out how you actually get several million people to say a specific sentence at a specific second, and the whole thing came down in about ninety minutes.

Because you have to *tell* them the sentence.

You have to publish it. You have to say it, out loud, first, into a world where every word said aloud arrives instantly at the one place it must not arrive early — and she would hear it, stripped of its shape, as one more crank on one more roof, and be inoculated against it before it ever formed. And that was the smaller problem.

The larger problem was that the people who had built a city for him out of a book he creased on a train, who had run this operation for longer than his species had had writing, who had watchers on trains with two children at university, would also hear it. They would have months. They would need about a week.

Fridan sat at his kitchen table in February with the envelope in front of him and understood that his plan was good, and that it was unusable by any private individual on Earth, and that to run it he would need a way to reach an enormous number of minds at once without saying anything out loud in advance.

There is one machine like that. He had been inside it. It had built him a rail line and a woman in a grey suit and sixteen furnished flats, and it had reached into a sleeping man in New Jersey and walked him through a night, and it had done that, according to the only source he had, every night for thousands of years.

---

He held out for nine days, which he thought at the time was integrity.

He went to bed on the tenth, in the dark, with the window open onto the cold, and said it aloud into his own room — because it did not matter any more, because she was standing in weather, because the only being that could hear him properly was the one he was about to ask.

"I need to talk to you about what you can build."

**"I wondered when you'd ask,"** said Anser.

And Fridan lay very still in the dark, and filed that too, in the part of him that nothing could hear.
