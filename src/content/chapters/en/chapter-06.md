He did not sleep properly for eleven days.

It was not insomnia. Insomnia is a thing that happens to a man. This was a decision, taken every night at about half past midnight, and re-taken at two, and again at four, and defended each time with a reason he would not have said out loud to anyone: that sleep was not a room he had ever been alone in, and he had only recently found that out.

So he watched things. He worked. He let the flat get bad in the specific way a flat gets bad when the person in it has stopped believing in the future tense — nothing dirty, just an accumulation of unmade decisions, a chair with clothes on it, four mugs.

He slept in pieces, sitting up, with the lamp on, and dreamed nothing at all, and woke each time with a jolt and checked his arm.

By the ninth day his hands had a tremor in them that had nothing to do with anything cosmic. By the eleventh he had begun to make small mistakes in front of other people — a word wrong, a step off a kerb — and he understood that he was going to have to sleep the way you understand you are going to have to go to the dentist.

---

The following started on the sixth day, or he noticed it on the sixth day, which are not the same thing and he was careful about the distinction from the beginning.

There was a man on the 14 bus, in the seat across the aisle and one back, in a dark green jacket with a repair on the left cuff — a rectangle of slightly wrong thread, four stitches visible along the top edge — who had also been standing outside the pharmacy on the corner of his street on the Sunday, four days earlier, in the same jacket, holding a phone in his left hand at chest height with the screen dark.

Fridan knew this the way he knew the layout of his flat.

That was the entire problem, and he saw it immediately, sitting on the 14 with his bag on his knees. A normal man notices a face and thinks *have I seen him before?* and cannot answer, and the not-answering is what keeps him sane. Fridan did not have that. He had four stitches along the top edge of a repair. He had the exact angle of a dark phone screen. He had it with the flat, total, unfading availability of everything since that night, and so the question *am I imagining this* was not available to him — and neither, he realised, was the question he actually needed, which was *am I imagining that it means something.*

Certainty had stopped being evidence. He had been given perfect recall and it had cost him doubt, and doubt, it turned out, had been doing an enormous amount of work.

There were four more over the next five days. A woman twice, at different stations, and he could not decide whether a city of nine million was large enough to make that a coincidence or small enough to make it one. A car. A man who looked at him a half-second too long in a supermarket and then, worse, looked away with real embarrassment, like someone caught.

He wrote them all down. The list, read back, was either the most frightening document he had ever produced or clinical evidence about its author, and it was written in handwriting that got smaller toward the bottom of each page.

---

On the eleventh night he went to bed on purpose.

He did it properly, because he had decided it was an experiment and an experiment deserves conditions. He turned the lamp off. He lay flat, which was how he had woken. He put his phone face down across the room. And before he closed his eyes he said, out loud, to the ceiling, in the level voice he had been using for eleven days:

"I'm going to sleep now. If that's where you are, I'll be there."

The building did its night noises.

He slept in about nine minutes, which was faster than he had slept in a year, and something was waiting.

---

There was a room.

That was all. A room of no particular size with light in it of no particular colour, and no window, and nothing in it that he could describe afterwards except the fact of standing in it, which was itself a kind of information — because the last time they had wanted something from him they had built him a city, a rail line, a building with sixteen furnished flats and a wear-path in the carpet, and this time they had not bothered with so much as a chair.

He noticed it, and he understood it as a compliment before he understood it as economy, and that was the first thing they got from him for free.

Then somebody spoke, and it was not her.

It was in the same place — the interior room, where his own voice lived — and it arrived the same way, and every other thing about it was different. Where she had been warm the way a room is warm, this was simply *level*. Not cold in the sense of cruel. Cold in the sense of a surface at the temperature of the air around it. Nothing was being done to him.

**"You're not asleep the way you were,"** it said. **"This will be shorter, and you will remember it. Do you want to sit down?"**

Fridan said, "There's nowhere to sit."

**"No,"** it agreed. **"You can call me Anser."**

He stood in the room and turned that over. A name, offered in the first ten seconds, unasked. She had gone an entire night without one and he had walked halfway across a city for her.

"That's not your name."

**"No. It's a name. You needed one, and I'd rather you had it than spend the next month calling me *it*."** A pause — a short one, nothing like hers, a pause for him rather than for permission. **"You have questions and I'm going to answer them. That's the whole reason I'm here. Ask in whatever order you like."**

And Fridan, who had spent eleven days speaking into an empty kitchen, felt something in his chest come loose so fast it frightened him.

---

He asked, "Was it real?"

**"Yes."**

"All of it."

**"The building was constructed for you. The city was constructed for you. The people in it were real people doing a job. The object was real and is still real and is no longer where you found it."** A beat. **"Yes. All of it. You were not ill and you are not ill now."**

He had not known how much of him was standing on that until it took his weight.

"Why do I remember?"

**"Because of us,"** Anser said. **"You weren't supposed to. The arrangement is that the run happens and the sleeper wakes with nothing — a good night, a vague sense of having been busy. That's not cruelty, it's hygiene; it's how it has always worked and how it worked for every one of the others. We interrupted the process while it was running. We didn't do it to give you your memory. Your memory is debris. But it is yours, and it is the reason you and I can have this conversation, so I'll take the credit I'm not owed."**

"The others."

**"Yes."**

"How many."

**"You saw sixteen doors,"** Anser said. **"That was one floor of one building in one city."**

Fridan stood in a room with no chairs and put his hand over his mouth like a man watching a car go into a wall.

---

Here is what he would try to explain, afterwards, to people who had never had it happen to them and who therefore always, always asked the same question in the same tone:

She had never lied to him. Not once. And she had never told him a single thing.

This one answered everything. Immediately, without hedging, in complete sentences, including the parts that made them look bad — *we'll take the credit we're not owed* — and every answer landed on a question he had been carrying up onto roofs at five in the morning like a man carrying a dead animal around a city looking for somewhere to put it down.

Nobody had ever been that useful to him. Nobody had ever come close.

He was, by any measure, an intelligent man. And he did not once, in that room, ask himself the only question that mattered, which is not *is this true* — you cannot check — but *why is this being given to me for nothing.*

---

"Who took it?" he said.

**"A civilisation. Not yours. Not hers either, though she made them, a long time ago, by a route that will take longer to explain than we have tonight."** Anser let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. **"They are not what you're already imagining. They are not coming for your world; they have not been able to reach your world for longer than your species has had writing, and that is not going to change. What they wanted was information, and they have been getting it the only way that is open to them, which is through people asleep. It's ugly. I'm not going to pretend to you that it's not ugly. But you should know that there is no army behind it and nothing is going to arrive."**

"They used me."

**"Yes."**

"For what?"

**"To ask her something they cannot ask her themselves,"** said Anser. **"She doesn't hear thoughts. She never has. She hears what's said and she hears what's dreamed, because in dreaming the two are the same thing — and that is the entire mechanism, Fridan, that's all of it, there is no deeper secret underneath. They needed a mouth she was listening to. They looked for a very long time and they found you, and the reason they found you is in your blood and I will show it to you when you're ready to look."**

The room did not change. Nothing dramatic happened. Fridan simply stood in it and discovered that he was crying, in the loose, mechanical way of a man whose body has decided something ahead of him.

"Are they going to do it again?"

**"To you? No. You're finished; you're used. To someone else — tonight, probably."** And then, and he would come back to this for the rest of his life: **"That's why I'm talking to you and not to them."**

---

At the end Anser gave him one instruction, and gave it lightly, almost as an afterthought, the way you tell someone to mind a step.

**"Don't say any of this out loud."**

Fridan said, "Why."

**"Because she'll hear it."** A pause, and this one was longer. **"Not the shape of it — the words. Exactly the words, exactly as you say them, wherever you are, whether or not you meant them for her. That's not a metaphor and it is not a punishment; it's how she was built. You have spent eleven days talking to your kitchen. She heard all of it."**

Something went through Fridan then that he had no name for, and would still have no name for a year later: the specific sick vertigo of being told that the thing you thought had abandoned you had been in the room the entire time, listening, and had chosen not to speak.

"Then why hasn't she—"

**"That's the question,"** Anser said. **"That's the right question and it's the first good one you've asked, and I'm not going to answer it tonight, because you're about to wake up and I would rather you had it whole."** The room was already thinning. **"Say nothing. Not aloud, not to anyone. Write what you like — she doesn't read. If you speak about what happened in that building, you will tell her, and I don't yet know what she does with it, and neither do you, and the honest position is that neither of us should find out by accident."**

"That's not a reason. That's just—"

**"It's a reason,"** said Anser. **"It's just not one you like."**

---

He woke at 4:12 in the morning with his face wet and the light off and no tremor in his arm at all.

He lay there for a long time in the dark. The parking structure hummed. Somewhere out in the building a pipe knocked twice and settled.

And Fridan opened his mouth — out of eleven days of habit, out of the whole animal need to say a thing to something — and found the first sentence sitting ready on his tongue, and it was, *are you there, did you hear that.*

He closed his mouth.

He turned onto his side and he lay in the dark with his jaw shut and said nothing at all, and if anything in the universe was listening, that was the night it stopped having any way of knowing.
