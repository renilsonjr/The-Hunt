He was found on the Thursday by a neighbour who had noticed the window.

It had been open for two days in April weather and the curtain had been going in and out of it, and she knocked, and then knocked harder, and then went and got the man from the ground floor who had a key for the meter. They found Fridan on top of the bed in his coat with a printed document on the kitchen table and an envelope addressed to his sister, and they were sufficiently certain of what they were looking at that the man from the ground floor went out onto the landing before he made the call.

He was in hospital for nineteen days.

They found, in order: severe dehydration, an electrolyte picture one registrar described in his notes as *frankly strange*, and a blood oxygen saturation that stayed at ninety-one per cent in a man sitting up in a chair on room air, which nobody could explain, and which did not change, and which never changed again for the rest of his life. He was seen by three consultants. The last of them, a kind and thorough woman in her fifties, sat on the end of the bed at the end of it all and said, "I want to be straight with you. There's something going on and I can't find it, and I've stopped believing I'm going to."

"That's all right," Fridan said.

"It isn't, particularly."

"No," he agreed. "But it's honest, and I've got a very high opinion of that."

He was discharged on a Tuesday with an oxygen concentrator he never used and an outpatient appointment he kept for four years.

---

Nothing happened to the world that anybody noticed.

That is the part he was never able to make anyone feel. There was no night sky event, no interruption of service, no hour in which every human being on Earth stopped what they were doing. Buses ran. The parking structure filled up in the mornings and emptied in the evenings.

What happened, over the following eighteen months, was a small and entirely unremarkable shift in the literature: a handful of sleep laboratories in different countries reporting the same odd thing, that the architecture of the fourth and fifth cycles had altered slightly in their cohorts — a flattening, a loss of something at the deep end — and that self-reported dream recall had dropped, everywhere, by a margin that was statistically solid and clinically meaningless.

Two papers were published. A third was rejected. A well-regarded researcher in Lisbon gave a conference talk in the second year proposing that the effect was an artefact of changed screen habits, and the room found this plausible, and that was more or less that.

People still dream. Fridan wanted that in the record and put it in the document twice. Everybody still dreams, every night, exactly as before.

Nothing is listening at the other end of it.

---

He went up to the roof on the morning of the eighth of May, at ten past five, because he had spent every morning of that spring failing to sleep past four and because the roof was the only place he could get a full breath.

It was cold and clear. The parking structure had two cars in it. There was one lit window in the block opposite, and no man moving about in it this time — just the light, on a timer, in an empty kitchen.

He was not there for any reason. He had stopped talking to the sky in March. He had gone up there to sit down.

And she came.

---

There was no arrival. There never had been.

She was there at the same size as everything, in the way weight is present in a bridge, and Fridan sat on a gravel roof with his back against the fan housing and did not get up, because he could not have, and because it would not have meant anything.

He was not frightened and he was not glad. He had spent a year in the specific condition of a man who has been left, and it turns out that the arrival of the thing that left you does not undo any of it; it simply sits down beside it.

He said, "You closed it."

**"Yes."**

"All of it. Everyone's."

**"Yes."**

"That was the only way you had of reaching us." His voice did what it had been doing since April, which was run out at the ends of sentences. "That was the whole of it. Prayer goes one way. The dreaming was the only place you could actually be with anybody."

**"Yes,"** she said.

And then she said: **"You were right about the roof."**

Fridan closed his eyes.

**"I stopped attending to you because whatever is at the other end of this reads my attention, and I could not work out how much of me they were getting, and you were the only thing I could take out of their reach by an act of will. I did not know how to tell you that without telling them. So I did not tell you, and you spent a year believing you had been discarded, and I could hear you believing it."** A pause; the old kind, the kind that felt like waiting for something to be permitted. **"That was the cost of doing it correctly. I want you to know that I knew what it was costing, and paid it anyway, and that I would do it again, and that it was the worst thing I have ever done."**

"You could have—"

**"No,"** she said. **"That is the entire subject. I could not."**

---

He asked her, because he had one chance and had had a year to decide what to spend it on, whether she had understood the trap.

**"Yes. It came with everything else."** Something moved through her, and after a year of practice he was better at reading it, and this one he was fairly sure was amusement, and it did not have any pleasure in it. **"They were very good. It was a superb piece of work. He built the wall's own repair into it — I was to be shown a hole, and the hole was real, and the fixing of it was to be the reaching, and they have been standing where my hand would land for a very long time."**

"And you didn't reach."

**"I did not touch the Barrier,"** she said. **"The Barrier was never the vulnerability. The vulnerability was the door I had left open in every sleeping mind in the world, because it was the only place I could hear my own creation properly, and because in eleven thousand years it had never once occurred to me that anyone would want to stand in it."**

The wind came across the roof.

"So you shut the door."

**"I shut the door."**

"And now nothing gets in."

**"And now nothing gets in,"** she agreed, **"and nothing gets out, and I have made myself into a thing that can hear every word every one of you says and can no longer answer any of it. There is no version where I keep both. I have been over it in a way you do not have the equipment to imagine and there is no third option and there never was."**

Fridan sat with that for a while.

"That's what you did to me," he said. "In February. That's exactly what you did to me."

**"Yes,"** she said. **"You have been living in it since the winter. You are the only one of them who has. That is not a consolation and I am not offering it as one — but you are the only human being alive who already knows what this is, and there will be a very long time in which nobody else does."**

---

He did not ask her about Uxies, because he had already decided that if the answer was bad he did not want to be carrying it, and because he was fairly sure that a god who had just closed her own mouth forever would not be able to see across a wall she had never been able to see across anyway.

He asked one thing more.

"You said I wasn't the one you were speaking to."

**"You were not. He was thirty-four years old and entirely human and I had been in the middle of a conversation with him for his whole life, in a room he did not know he was in, and I came when you called and he was not there any more."** A pause. **"You are not him. You have never been him. You are what he became, and I have had a year to look at it from a distance, and I would like to say — because it is the last thing I am ever going to say to anyone, and I have thought about what to spend it on — that I do not think he would have done any of this. I do not think he could have. He was a great deal easier to love and he would not have got out of the chair."**

The sky over the parking structure had started to go.

"Is that the last thing?"

**"No,"** she said.

And then she told him her name.

---

He never wrote it down. It is not in the document; there is a line in the final section where it plainly ought to be, and there is nothing in that line, and he left it that way in all four revisions.

He told one person, twenty years later, in a hospital corridor, and made them promise, and they kept the promise.

He said afterwards — the only time he ever tried to describe it — that it was not a word, and that it was not long, and that it had been in the world the entire time.

Then she was gone, and this time it was not a door closing, because there was no door left to close. She simply was not attending, anywhere, to anyone, and would not be again; and the difference between that and the first time was that Fridan understood, sitting on a roof in Nor Yesey with the sun coming up over a parking structure, that from now on every single person on Earth was in exactly the same room as him, and that not one of them would ever know it.

---

He went down at about seven.

He made tea. He sat at the kitchen table with the document, which was ninety-one pages by then, and turned to the front, and looked for a while at a header he had typed with his thumbs on the morning of the twenty-sixth of August in a bedroom in Nor Yesey, before any of it.

Then he got a pen, and under the last line, at the bottom of the ninety-first page, he wrote the only sentence in the whole account that he ever addressed to a reader:

*I don't expect to be believed. I'd just like it written down somewhere that she didn't leave.*
