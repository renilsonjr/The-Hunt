He tried to do it without them first. He wanted that on the record, later, when there was nobody left to put it on the record for.

Four days after Halverton he ordered a consumer ancestry kit, the kind advertised on the sides of buses, and it came in a white box with a cheerful leaflet, and he sat at his kitchen table at nine on a Tuesday night and spat into a plastic tube up to a printed line while the parking structure hummed outside the window.

It cost him eighty-nine ninety-five. He put it in a postbox on the corner. He remembered thinking, walking back, that it was the sanest thing he had done in a month: a checkable question asked of a company with a customer services number, an answer that would arrive on a date they had already told him, from people who had never once been inside his head.

Six weeks. He had six weeks of something to wait for, and he got through them better than he had got through the fortnight before.

---

He was also, in that period, getting worse in ways he catalogued and did not interpret.

He was sleeping four or five hours, badly, sitting up, and he was tired in a manner that had stopped being about sleep — a flat grey shortage that food did not touch. His hands were unreliable in the mornings. Twice he lost a word mid-sentence at work, and the second time it was a common word, and the woman he was speaking to waited for it with the particular kindness people reserve for a colleague who is visibly coming apart.

And the air had gone wrong.

That was the one he could not get anyone near. Rooms had begun to feel *thin* — offices, the train, his own front room with the heating on — a sense of insufficiency at the top of every breath, like a glass filled not quite to where he expected. He had started opening windows. In the second week of November he sat through an entire meeting with the window behind him open onto a car park in the rain, and the others put on coats and nobody said anything, and he did not offer to close it because the alternative was to sit in that room and drown politely.

He wrote in the notebook: *ask a doctor.* It was the fourth time he had written it. He put a box around it.

---

The results came on the ninth of December, by email, with a subject line that said his name and the word *ready* and a small illustration of a helix.

He opened it on his phone standing up in his kitchen.

He was 41 per cent one thing and 22 per cent another and 19 per cent a third, and there was a map with soft-edged blobs on it, and a paragraph about a great-grandmother, and a section headed *Traits* that told him he was likely to dislike coriander.

*Unassigned: 0.6%.*

He read the whole report twice. He read the methodology page. He read the FAQ, which explained in a friendly font that unassigned percentages were common and reflected regions the reference panel could not confidently place, and that they were nothing to be concerned about.

Then Fridan sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against the cupboard, and laughed, and the laugh went somewhere unpleasant almost immediately and he let it.

He had been so sure. Not that it would find something — he had known, in the reasonable part of him, that it probably wouldn't. He had been sure of the *shape* of it: that he would be able to hold up a piece of ordinary paper from an ordinary company and say, to himself if to nobody else, *this is checkable and I checked it.*

He had wanted one fact that did not come out of somebody's mouth.

---

**"Of course it was normal,"** Anser said that night, without impatience. **"They compare you to a panel of people. Everyone on the panel is human. You are almost entirely human. The test asked whether you resemble humans and the answer, correctly, was yes."**

"Then it isn't there."

**"It's there. It is not in the part they read, and it would not mean anything to them if it were, because meaning requires a second example and they have never seen one."** A pause. **"I said I'd show you. I meant it. But I want to tell you the price first, because if I don't tell you the price and you find it out afterwards, everything I've said to you turns into something else."**

"Go on."

**"To show you, I need you properly asleep. Not this — not the shallow business you've been doing in a chair. Down. Four hours, maybe five, in the state you have been refusing since August."** Anser let it stand a moment. **"And while you're there you will be unguarded, and open, and I am not the only thing that knows how to use that. I don't believe anything else will come. I can't prove it to you. You would be taking my word, and my word is the only thing I have ever given you."**

Fridan lay in the dark of his own bedroom in the flat with the window open onto the cold.

He thought about a man on a platform in Halverton saying *some of it comes in through the sleeping, and that's the bit you want to be careful about, because you can't lock that.*

He thought about eighty-nine ninety-five and 0.6 per cent.

"How long do I have to decide?"

**"You don't have to decide anything,"** said Anser. **"You will get tired enough eventually and it will happen without a decision. I'd rather you did it on purpose. People are better afterwards when they chose."**

Which was, Fridan thought — much later, when it was far too late to matter — a remarkably honest description of the entire process by which he was taken.

He said, "Tonight."

---

He did not dream. That is the wrong word for it and he never used it.

He went down further than he had been since August, past the place where the room goes, and something met him there and did not build him a city. There was no set this time either — no room, no light, no direction — and afterwards the only way he could describe the next part was to say that he was shown himself the way you are shown a house you already live in.

And there, somewhere in the long patient architecture of him, in among all the ordinary machinery that had been assembled out of two parents and a great-grandmother and several thousand generations of people who had dug and walked and buried each other — there was a piece that was not from any of them.

It was not dramatic. It was not glowing. It was small, and old, and *quiet*, in a way that reminded him of nothing so much as a laundry ball on a white shelf.

And when he came near it, in whatever sense he was coming near anything, his chest did the thing.

The hand closing. The *there*.

The exact sensation — not similar, not comparable, the *same* sensation, filed and available and instantly matched against the original, the way he could match a checked shirt or four stitches on a cuff — that he had felt standing in a doorway on the third floor of a building that did not exist, looking at three apples in a bowl with one of them lying on its side.

---

He came up out of it at ten past five in the morning and lay completely still.

"It was me," he said, aloud, to the ceiling, before he remembered not to, and then did not care. "The pull. That was me. That was mine."

**"Yes."**

"She didn't give me that."

**"No."**

"All night." He was not shouting. He noticed that he was not shouting, and that his voice had gone very quiet and reasonable, which was worse. "All night I thought — the certainty, in the first building, before she ever spoke to me, that I would be the one who found it. I thought something had *put* that in me. I thought I'd been chosen."

**"You were selected,"** Anser said. **"It's a different word and you should use the different word. Nobody looked at your life and saw anything in it. A machine read a very large number of people looking for one signal, and you gave it, and the rest — the flat, the book with the crease in it, the whole of that night built to make an ordinary man walk downhill instead of uphill — that was arranged afterwards, around the fact of you, the way a chair is built around a person's shape."**

"And the thing on the shelf?"

**"The same kind of thing as the thing in you."**

Fridan lay in the dark with his arm across his eyes.

He had spent four months treasuring one sentence out of that night, and he had never said it aloud to anybody, and he had known even while he was writing it down that it was the most embarrassing thing in the document: *it was like my whole life was meant to direct me to that moment.*

It was true. That was the joke of it. His whole life had been meant to direct him there. Only the meaning had not been in his life. It had been in a fragment of something in the part of him that no company with a customer services number knows how to read, and it had been in him at four years old and at nineteen and on every unremarkable day of a life he had privately believed was heading somewhere, and it would have been in any other body it happened to land in, and it had done what it did — recognised its own kind across a floor of a building — with no more regard for Fridan than a compass has for the hand that holds it.

---

He asked, before it got light, the questions he had been saving, and Anser answered them, and answered them shortly, which he understood afterwards was mercy and at the time felt like being handed things too fast to catch.

There were two of them. That was the beginning of it. One made the other, a long time ago, and they came apart over something Anser refused to summarise — **"I'll tell you when there's time to tell it properly, because the short version makes them both sound stupid, and neither of them is stupid"** — and rather than finish each other they made an arrangement.

Two galaxies. One each. Hers, with people in it. His, with something else in it, which had been people once and had been added to.

"And between them?"

**"Air,"** said Anser.

Fridan opened his eyes in the dark.

**"That's the whole Barrier. There's no wall and there's no fleet and there never has been. She made the two atmospheres incompatible, at the level of the body, so that anything from his side that breathes yours dies in about ninety seconds and anything from your side that breathes theirs dies rather faster. It is the most elegant thing she ever did and it has held without maintenance for longer than your species has had a word for sky. They cannot come here. They have never been able to come here. That is not reassurance, Fridan — that is the reason any of this happened. If they could walk in and take what they wanted, nobody would ever have had to go into anybody's sleep."**

The window was open onto the November cold and Fridan lay under it breathing air that had felt thin for a month.

He did not put those two facts beside each other. He would like to be able to say that he did. He turned his head on the pillow and asked the question he had actually been building toward all night, the one that had been sitting under everything since a woman in Halverton showed him a note in her own handwriting.

"The piece in me. The fragment." His mouth was dry. "It isn't hers, is it."

**"No,"** said Anser. **"It's his."**

"Then she'd have known. If she can look at people at all — if she's what you say she is — she'd have looked at me at some point in thirty-four years and seen a piece of her brother sitting in a man in Nor Yesey."

The pause went on a long time.

Long enough that Fridan sat up in the bed, in the cold, with his heart going.

**"Yes,"** said Anser.
