He gave himself eleven days and used nine of them.

He finished the document first. It had been seven pages in August and it was a great deal more than that by April, and he went through the whole of it and made it into one account, in order, in plain words, from the room where nobody had taken off their coat to a chair by an open window in March. He did not soften anything and he did not explain himself. He put in the two columns from the notebook, both of them, including the right-hand one.

He printed it twice. One copy went into a envelope addressed to his sister, sealed, with a note on the front saying not to open it unless she had not heard from him in a month. The other he left on the kitchen table.

He paid the rent three months forward. He cancelled two direct debits and left the rest. He wrote a short letter to his manager that was mostly apology and did not send it, and then sent it.

On the ninth day he cleaned the flat, which he could not have justified to anybody, and which he was glad of afterwards for reasons he never entirely worked out.

Then he opened the window as far as it would go, and lay down on top of the bed with his coat on, at nine in the evening, in the cold, and went looking for a woman he had never met on the other side of a wall made of air.

---

He had one advantage and it was the only one he ever had, and it was that he was nothing.

Everything on that side of the operation was built to see infrastructure. It watched for signal, for apparatus, for the great patient machinery of a civilisation reaching across; it had been watching for eleven thousand years and it was extremely good at it. A man on a bed in Nor Yesey with a residue in him and no equipment at all was not below its threshold in the sense of being hidden. He was below it in the sense that a moth is below a customs regime.

He went out under everything.

He would not ever describe the crossing, to anyone, including the one person who asked. What he wrote, later, was: *it is very cold and it is not empty and it does not take any time at all, and that is the worst part, because it means the distance was never the difficulty.*

Twice something enormous went past. Neither of them noticed him.

---

He did not have a location. You cannot have a location for a mind.

What he had was four hundred and eleven entries in a log, tagged and re-tagged for thirty years by observers who had each in turn found her boring, and the four words he had been able to get out of them.

*She goes where the noise is least.*

So he did not look for Uxies. He looked for quiet, in a world Anser had described as a permanent argument, and the whole of that world was one great weather of noise — an atmosphere of it, minds and machines and speech and the eleven thousand-year hum of the operation running underneath everything like plant in a basement — and he moved through it the way you move upstream, and it thinned, and it thinned, and then he came out into somewhere it had stopped.

It was a yard.

That was all it was: a flat industrial yard on the edge of a city, in the dream of somebody who had been walking to it since she was nine. There were stacks of something that had a shape but no name he could put on it. There was a rail of some kind. Above it the sky was that world's sky, moving fast, the colour of the inside of a shell, with the towers a long way off and lit.

And there was no sound. Not silence in the sense of an absence of noise — a *pocket*, a place where the argument did not reach, four hundred and eleven times, for forty-two years.

She was standing in the middle of it with her back to him.

---

She was not a woman in the sense he had been braced for and he was glad of that almost immediately; it is easier to be honest with something you cannot sentimentalise. She was tall, and her proportions were wrong in ways he stopped cataloguing after about four seconds because cataloguing her felt like the beginning of the thing he had come here specifically not to do. Her skin had a colour under it rather than on it. When she turned her head there were more joints in the movement than there should have been.

She looked at him with complete composure.

"You're not from here," she said.

"No."

"No." She considered him for a while. "Nothing gets in here. That's the entire point of it."

"I know. I came because of that." His voice was doing something strange; the crossing had taken more out of him than he had budgeted and he had known it would and had budgeted wrong anyway. "You've been coming here since you were nine years old."

Uxies of Earth 2 stood in the quiet place in her own sleep and did not move at all.

"Say that again," she said.

---

Here is what he had intended to do, and he was clear about it in advance, in writing, and he stood by the intention for the rest of his life even though it turned out to be worthless.

He had intended to be *kind* about it. He was not going to build her a city. He was not going to walk her through a set or dress a room out of her life or put a thing on a shelf where she would find it. He would come into the quiet place, and go to the fragment — he could feel it from where he stood, and it was like standing near a struck bell four rooms away — and he would set one harmonic against it, gently, and leave, and she would wake in the morning having dreamed about a yard, which she did most nights anyway.

She would never know. That was the merciful part, as he had constructed it. She would never have to carry any of it.

He got about four seconds into it.

The fragment would not sound.

He was near it. He was as near it as he had been to the sphere on the shelf, and the *there* was in his chest and it was ringing at him — and when he set the harmonic against it, carefully, correctly, in the manner he had spent nine days working out, nothing happened at all. It sat in her like a stone in a field. It was not resisting him. It was not defended. It simply would not do the one thing he needed it to do, and he stood in a yard in another galaxy and understood, with the enormous slowness of a man watching his own house catch, why.

Zalian does not hear thought.

She hears what is *said*: what a mind decides to put out of itself and offer. That is not a preference and it is not a rule of etiquette, it is the load-bearing beam of the entire universe — the whole reason there had to be a Hunt, the whole reason eleven thousand years of machinery had been pointed at the one place where a mind speaks without knowing it is speaking. Everything on that side had been built to get around it.

A fragment struck from outside is a thing that happened to her. It is noise. It is theft, and theft is precisely what she is deaf to, and if it were otherwise nobody would ever have needed to steal.

For that fragment to *say* anything, the person carrying it had to mean it.

He had to ask her.

---

"You've gone a strange colour," Uxies observed. "Are you dying?"

"Probably." He sat down on the ground, which was not dignified and was necessary. "Not here. Later, at home, in a few hours, I think. I want to be accurate with you about everything so I'm going to be accurate about that too."

"That's the first thing you've said that sounded true."

"Everything I say from now on is going to be true," Fridan said, "and I need you to understand that it would be much easier for me if I lied to you, and that I know how, because it was done to me."

And he told her.

He told her all of it, sitting on the ground of a yard in a dream, in the order it had happened, at the speed a man talks when he can feel the floor of himself going. The room where nobody took off their coat. The apple on its side. The rail turning red and the warm voice that never once lied and never once answered. A woman in a grey suit in a doorway saying thank you. Sixteen doors on the third floor and a stopped watch on a table. Halverton, and a fortnight in the August, and a piece of paper in a drawer for nineteen years in a woman's own handwriting.

He told her what was in him and where it came from, and what was in her, and where it came from.

He told her about the log. That was the hardest part and he made himself do it properly: that there was a record of her from the day she was born, that it went to sixteen thousand entries of nothing, that it noted every time she came to this yard and had done for thirty years, and that against every single one of those entries, for three decades, somebody had written *unexplained*.

"They have never approached you," he said. "They never will. You're the one thing in either galaxy that could tell her, and so they have spent fifty-one years making absolutely certain that nothing interesting ever happens to you."

The sky went over, fast, the colour of the inside of a shell.

"And you want to use me," said Uxies.

"Yes."

"Because of what's in me."

"Yes. Not because of who you are. I want to be exact about that because everybody who has ever wanted anything from you has wanted it for what you are, and I am not going to stand here and pretend to be the exception." His hands had started shaking and he put them on his knees. "I came here intending to do it without telling you. I had a whole argument for why that was the kind version. It doesn't work — that's the only reason I'm asking. I want that on the record too."

She was quiet for a long moment.

"No," she said.

---

He had not prepared for it and afterwards he was ashamed of that, because it was obviously the correct answer, and any competent person would have expected it.

"All right," he said.

"That's it? All right?"

"I can't make it work without you. That's not a bargaining position, it's the mechanism." He looked up at her. "And if I could make it work without you I'd have done it four seconds after I got here, and you'd have woken up tomorrow and never known, and I'd have gone home thinking I was a decent man. So — yes. All right."

Uxies stood in the quiet place with her arms at her sides.

"Do you know what I have been told about this yard," she said, "for forty-two years."

"No."

"That there is something wrong with me. Not cruelly — that's not how we are, mostly. Kindly. Constantly. That I am *unwell*, that I am cold, that I don't participate, that there is a hole where the ordinary appetite for the world should be." Her head moved, that extra-jointed turn. "I have a sister who cried about it. I stopped coming for two years once, to make her stop crying, and I got very ill, and nobody connected the two things, including me."

"I'm sorry."

"You aren't listening." She came a step closer. "Forty-two years I have walked out here where the noise stops and stood in it, and I have never once been able to say why, and every person who ever loved me has treated it as the flaw in an otherwise adequate woman. And you have come out of the dark in the middle of the night and told me it is a *piece of a god* trying to hear something."

Fridan said nothing.

"Is it true?"

"Yes."

"Say the other thing again. The thing about what it's been doing."

"It's been listening," he said. "It's been listening your whole life for something on the other side of a wall, and there's nothing there, because she's not on this side and she never has been, and it can't know that. That's what the quiet is for. You've been coming out here to help it hear."

Uxies of Earth 2 stood very still for a long time in a yard in her own sleep.

"Then it is not a hole," she said.

"No."

"It is the only part of me that has been right about anything." She looked at the sky. "Fifty-one years."

And then she said: "What do I have to do?"

---

"You have to mean it," Fridan said. "That's all of it. She doesn't hear anything that was taken. She only hears what somebody decided to say — that's the law she made and it's why all of this was necessary in the first place. So it can't be me. I can only stand next to it." His vision had gone strange at the edges. "You have to want it to sound, and you have to know what you're doing when you do, and you have to do it knowing they've watched you your whole life and this is the first thing you will ever have done that they'll see."

"Will they hurt me?"

"I don't know," he said. "I've told you every true thing I have and that one I don't know. I think they've never been able to touch you and I think that stops being true about four seconds afterwards."

"That's an honest answer."

"It's the only kind I've got left."

Uxies looked around the yard — the stacks, the rail, the fast sky, the pocket where the argument did not reach and had not reached since she was nine years old.

"Fifty-one years of quiet," she said, "so that I can make one noise."

"Yes."

"All right," said Uxies.

And she reached in and found the small old thing that had been sitting in the middle of her since before she had a name for herself, the thing that had cost her a sister's tears and forty-two years of walking out to an industrial yard on the edge of the city — and she took hold of it, deliberately, the way you take hold of a bell rope, and she *meant* it.

---

Fridan was there for the first part.

He never had adequate words and he never pretended to. What he wrote was: *it did not sound like a noise. It sounded like a place where something had always been missing being filled in.*

It went up out of her, and out of the yard, and out of the noise of that whole arguing world, and it did not go the way a signal goes. It did not travel. It was not on this side and then it was also on the other side, in the material she was made of, arriving from inside a world she had sealed with her own hands eleven thousand years ago and had never once had reason to look at since — her own voice, in a place her voice categorically could not be.

Not loud. It did not need to be loud.

*Unignorable* is a different property from loud, and it had taken a man in Nor Yesey four months and a launderette to work out the difference.

Somewhere on the other side of the Barrier, at a second nobody had known in advance and nobody had bought, something the size of everything stopped.

And Fridan, kneeling in a yard in another galaxy with his hands in the dirt, felt the very edge of it turn — felt an attention of unimaginable scale come round like weather changing over a coast — and had one clear thought, the last one he had over there, which was that he was not going to be able to stay and see it.

"Go," Uxies said, from a long way off. "You're going. Go home."

He went home.
