I walk for a long time before I admit I don't know where I'm going.

The street outside the building runs downhill and I take it because it runs downhill. There are no other pedestrians. There are parked cars with frost on the windscreens, and lit windows four floors up, and somewhere out of sight a bus sighs and pulls away, and all of it is completely ordinary and none of it is a place I have ever been.

I try to think of the name of the city and there isn't one. Not a blank where the name should be — no sense of a gap at all, the way you don't notice you can't see behind your own head. I only find it because I go looking.

That should stop me. It doesn't, quite. It goes into the same pocket as the building that was bigger inside, and the pocket holds a great deal tonight and does not seem to be filling up.

What has stopped is the pull.

In the doorway it was there — ahead of me, insistent, a hand at the wrist. Four streets later it is gone, and what's left is a man walking downhill in the cold with no coat, getting further from something at a steady four miles an hour.

I stop at a corner under a dead streetlight and say, out loud, to nobody: "All right. Which way."

Nothing answers. But saying it makes the not-answering into a shape, and I notice the shape, and I file it, and I keep walking.

---

The rail line is what I find instead.

I come down a flight of steps between two buildings and there it is below me, in a cutting, running left to right across the whole width of the world: four tracks, ballast, the grey grid of sleepers, catenary posts going away in both directions in exact perspective. There is a footbridge. I stand on it and put my hands on the rail — the handrail, cold, gritty with old paint — and look down at the tracks, and for the first time all night I feel something that isn't certainty and isn't fear.

It's relief. The tracks go two ways. That is two more directions than I had a minute ago.

Left, the line runs toward a mass of towers with their tops lit. Right, it goes into a low dark that could be anything.

I stand there long enough for my hands to hurt from the cold and I cannot choose, and the reason I cannot choose is that I have nothing to choose *with* — no reason, no clue, no bearing, only the memory of having had one. The certainty in the building was not mine. I understand that on the footbridge, holding the rail. It was given to me, and now it has been taken back, and the difference between those two states is the difference between being a person and being a tool that has been set down.

I say, out loud, because saying things out loud has started to seem like the only lever I have:

"I don't know where to go."

And she says: **"I know."**

---

She is not in the air. She is not behind me. There is no sound in the sense of something arriving at my ears.

She is *in the place where my own voice is*, the interior room where I hear myself think — except that this is not my thought, and there is no mistaking it for one, no more than you could mistake a hand on your shoulder for your own hand.

She is warm. That is the first thing, and it stays the first thing for a long time afterward, through everything. Not kind exactly. Warm, the way a room is warm — it is not doing it at you, it is simply its condition.

I don't move. My hands stay on the rail.

"Who's that," I say.

**"You are cold,"** she says. **"You have been walking for eleven minutes. That was necessary. It will not be necessary again."**

"Who *are* you?"

**"Look at the line."**

Which is not an answer, and which I obey instantly.

---

Later — much later, on a morning when the room is a real room and the light is real light and the silence has a completely different quality — I will go over these first minutes the way you go over an accident. I will look for the moment I should have known.

I won't find it. What I will find is this: I ask her three questions and she answers none of them, and it does not once occur to me to hold that against her, because every time she doesn't answer she tells me something *better* — something that moves me forward, that solves the actual problem I have. She is so useful that the not-answering never rises to the level of being noticed.

That is not a lie. She never tells me one. I want to be exact about that, afterwards, when exactness is all I have left.

She simply never answers what I ask, and gives me instead what I need, and a person being given what they need does not audit the transaction.

---

"Look at the line," she says, and I look at the line.

For a moment it is four tracks in a cutting at night.

Then the rail turns red.

Not lit — I have to be careful here, because *lit* would mean a source, and there is no source. The near rail of the left-hand track simply becomes red, along its whole length, out to the limit of what I can see, in one instant, the way a word changes when you learn what it means. It is not glowing. It is not hot; there is no shimmer above it. It is red the way a thing is the colour it is, and it was not that colour, and now it is, and there was no moment of becoming.

My breath goes out of me. I take my hands off the handrail and put them back.

"What did you do."

**"That is your route,"** she says. **"Follow it. Not on the track — beside it. There is a service path on the eastern side. You will find a gate; it is not locked tonight."**

"You changed it." I hear my own voice go up. "You changed something *real*."

A pause. Hers are strange, her pauses. They don't feel like thinking. They feel like waiting for something to be permitted.

**"You needed to see it,"** she says.

---

I find the gate. It is not locked.

The service path is a strip of hardcore and weeds between the fence and the ballast, wide enough for one, and I walk it for what my legs say is an hour and my head refuses to estimate. The red rail runs at my left the whole way, steady, at the exact edge of my vision, and I understand very quickly that I must not look at it directly for too long, because when I do the wrongness of it comes up through my chest like cold water, and when I keep it at the edge I can simply follow it, and following it is easy, and being easy is the entire point.

She talks to me.

Not constantly. In intervals, and always to purpose. **"There is a fence in ninety metres. Go over it at the post, not at the panel."** **"The path narrows. Keep to the left."** **"You are doing this correctly."**

That last one undoes something in me. I am ashamed of how much I want to hear it again.

Somewhere along the second mile I get careless and think a question instead of saying it — *what is it that I'm looking for, what does it actually look like* — think it hard, the way you press a bruise, and wait.

Nothing.

Half a mile later I say it aloud, into the cold, to the rail: "What does it look like?"

**"You will know it,"** she says, immediately, warm as ever. **"There is a bridge in four hundred metres. Go under, not over."**

I stop walking.

I stand on a strip of weeds beside a red rail in a city with no name and I turn the two things over: the thought that went nowhere, and the words that were answered before I finished saying them.

She hadn't heard me think it.

I get as far as that. I get exactly that far, and then the sentence I would need next — the one that asks what kind of thing hears speech and not thought, and why a thing like that would be helping me, and what it means that everything she has said tonight has been a direction and never once a fact — that sentence does not arrive.

Something in me declines to build it. It is not that I decide against it. It is that the shape of the question forms, and my mind puts it down, gently, the way you set down a glass you have decided not to drink from, and I look up and there is the bridge, four hundred metres, exactly as she said.

I go under, not over.

---

The second building is on the far side of a road I don't remember crossing.

It is tall and unlit for most of its height, and there is a canopy over the entrance, and light coming out of the ground floor in a long spill onto wet paving. It is the middle of the night. It has been the middle of the night for hours; the night here does not seem to be going anywhere.

The red rail ends thirty metres short of the doors. Not fading — ending, at a point, the way a road ends at a wall. Beyond the point the rail is rail-coloured. The route is used up.

I stand at the end of it and look at the doors, and I can see people inside. Four or five of them, standing about in the lobby in the middle of the night, not sitting, not talking much. Waiting the way staff wait.

They are expecting somebody.

"Is it in there?" I say.

**"Yes."**

The first straight answer of the entire night, and it lands in me like a coin dropping into water, and every part of me lifts.

I should ask her something else. Standing at the end of a rail she made red, thirty metres from a door with people behind it who have been waiting in an empty lobby at three in the morning for someone to arrive — there is a question here, and it is not *is it in there*.

**"They are expecting you,"** she says, warm, unhurried, as though it were good news. **"Go in."**

I go in.
