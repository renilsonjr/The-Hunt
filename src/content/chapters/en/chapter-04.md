I am four steps up the flight to three when I understand what she said to me.

*Third floor's nice. Quiet.*

I stop with my hand on the banister. Because a woman in a grey suit who has spent all night not answering questions has just told me where to go, and she said it the way you say a thing you're allowed to say, and there is only one arrangement in which that is possible.

Either she is guessing — and she does not guess, she has been guessing all night in ceiling voids and she is *bad* at it —

Or she is being told the same things I am.

I stand on the stairs and I say it out loud, to the stairwell, because out loud is the only way anything gets heard around here:

"Is she with you?"

Nothing.

I wait. The light hums. Somewhere above me a fire door releases and settles.

"Are you on their side?"

Nothing, and the nothing goes on long enough to become an answer of a kind — not a denial, not a confirmation, just the great warm patience of something that will not be drawn — and I climb the rest of the flight, because there is a floor at the top of it and the thing I want is either there or it isn't, and I have stopped being able to want anything else.

---

The third floor is apartments.

Not offices, not function rooms. Apartments — a corridor of them, both sides, all the way to a window at the far end that shows nothing but the dark. Doors without numbers. The same door as mine, sixteen times over.

The first one I try opens.

It is my apartment. Not similar — the same. Short hall, kitchen off it, two sofas at right angles, lamps burning at four in the morning for nobody. The same empty bowl on the counter.

On the low table by the window there is a pair of reading glasses in a case.

They aren't mine. I have never owned a pair. I stand in that room a long time looking at a case with somebody's thumbprint worn into the leather, and then I go out and open the next door.

Sofas. Lamps. Bowl. On the table, a wristwatch with a cracked face, stopped.

The next: a child's drawing, folded twice, the folds gone soft.

The next: a paperback in a language I can't read, spine creased white in one place.

Sixteen doors. I open eleven of them and then I have to stop, and I stand in the corridor with my back against the wall and my hands on my knees, breathing like I've been running.

They have done this before.

Not once. Not twice. There is a room down there with a stopped watch in it, laid out on a table by a window, in a flat furnished from somebody's life, waiting for a man who was walked in off a street he'd never seen, in a city with no name, and told *you should rest*.

Every one of them thought they were the first. I'd bet everything I have on it. Every one of them stood in a warm empty flat with their heart going and thought: *how strange, that they know me.*

And at the end of it — this is the part that arrives last and worst, sliding into place while I stand in a corridor with my head down —

*none of them found it.*

Because it is still here.

---

I straighten up and I say, "Help me."

And she says: **"Look at the floor."**

The carpet is a dark blue-grey, cheap, hard-wearing, the kind they lay in a hundred-metre roll. It is filthy the way carpet is filthy — evenly, invisibly.

Except that it isn't.

Once she has told me to look, I cannot stop seeing it: down the centre of the corridor there is a track. A shallow trough worn through the pile, going from the stairwell to the far end, and it is not the spread wandering of people who live somewhere. It is one route. It is a single line walked so many times that the wool has gone flat and slightly shiny under the lights, the way stone goes hollow in the middle of a step.

It runs the length of the corridor, past every door, and it stops.

Not at a door. Two metres short of the window, at a stretch of blank wall on the left-hand side, the track just stops, in the way a thing stops when it has arrived.

"How long," I say. My voice comes out wrong. "How long have they been walking that?"

**"Go to the end,"** she says.

---

There are two of them standing there.

The man from the sixth floor who told me he couldn't sleep either, and a younger one I have not seen before, and they are at the end of the corridor in the last two metres, and they have clearly been there some time.

The young one is holding a phone with the torch on, sweeping it along the join between the carpet and the skirting board.

And the older man is leaning against the wall.

He is leaning against the wall on the left-hand side, at the point where the track stops, with his shoulder and his upper arm flat against it, ankles crossed, entirely at his ease — leaning on the place, resting his weight on it, the way you'd lean on any wall in any corridor in the world.

I come down the track and I see it from about six metres out.

There is a door there.

It is not hidden. There is no trick to it, no seam, no catch; it is a plain door in a plain frame with a plain handle at the height of every other handle on this floor, and the man is leaning on the wall two feet to the left of it with his eyes moving over it, back and forth, twice, three times, while I watch.

"Anything?" he says to the young one.

"Nothing."

I stop walking.

They both look up at me and their faces do a thing I will remember for the rest of my life, which is: they look at me, and then they look at where I am looking, and then they look back at me — and the older man pushes himself off the wall, and I can see it happen, I can watch the whole thought cross him:

*He's seeing something.*

"What," he says. It isn't a question, it comes out of him too fast for that. "What is it. What are you looking at."

I don't answer him.

I walk between them, and neither of them stops me, because neither of them can stop something they can't see me doing, and I put my hand on the handle of a door two feet from the man's shoulder, and behind me the young one says, "*What's he doing—*", and I open it and go through and close it, and the last thing I hear from that corridor is a hand slapping flat against plaster, hard, in exactly the wrong place.

---

The room is small.

A window with the blind down. Bare floor. And on the wall opposite, one shelf — a plain white shelf of the kind that comes in a flat box — with nothing on it but a ball.

I stand with my back to the door.

It is blue. A soft mid-blue, slightly faded on one side as though it has sat somewhere with light on it. It is about the size of a plum, and it is *fuzzy* — a felted, napped surface, matted a little, the way a thing gets when it's been handled.

It is one of those balls you throw in the dryer to take the fur off clothes.

And I stand there for four or five seconds in something I can only describe as insult, because I have walked all night, and gone down nine floors and up three, and passed a stopped watch and a child's drawing on tables in a building that eats people, for a *laundry ball on a shelf* —

and then the feeling comes.

The one from the bowl of apples. The hand closing in my chest. Except that in the room with the apples it was a bearing, a needle swinging, a *this way* — and here there is no direction in it at all, because there is nowhere left to point. It has arrived. It sits in the middle of me and simply says: **yes.**

I go closer.

On the front of it, worked into the nap in a slightly deeper blue, there are two small marks side by side. And when I turn my head, on the back of it, one.

Two in front. One behind.

It means nothing to me. I want to be honest about that, because of everything that came afterwards and everything people have wanted me to say I understood: I looked at that mark and it meant nothing at all, and I still reached for it, and I would like that to be written down somewhere in my own words.

I say, out loud, to the room: "Will you still be here after?"

And she pauses.

I have heard her pause before — those strange waits of hers that never feel like thinking, that feel like a request going somewhere and coming back. This one is longer than any of them. Long enough that I hear the blind move very slightly against the window frame.

Then she says:

**"Take it."**

I take it.

---

There is no moment of contact. That's the first thing that's wrong. My fingers close on a soft light object and there is no cool of it, no give, no texture, no weight — the ball is not in my hand, it is *in the place where my hand is*, the way she was in the place where my voice was, and I have a quarter of a second to understand that they are the same kind of thing and then it starts.

The hair goes up on my forearm. All of it at once, from the wrist to the elbow, a wave of it, and my arm shakes, and then my whole body shakes — not fear, my body has been afraid all night and this is not that — this is a physical event happening in me the way weather happens in a valley.

My ears fill. Pressure, deep, both sides, like altitude, like water — and out of the pressure comes a chord.

Strings. Low, immense, held. Not from anywhere; it is not in the room, the room is silent, the blind is not moving. It is under everything, the way a chord under a scene tells you what the scene means, and it swells, and it does not resolve, and it does not stop.

And then my head opens.

I don't know a better word and I have looked for one. It is not that I learn things. It is that the container goes — the small warm cave I have lived in all my life, the walls of it that are so completely the shape of me that I have never once noticed I was inside anything — and what is on the other side is not information, it is *scale*. I am the size of everything. There is nothing that is not adjacent to me. I understand — and I cannot bring back one single item of it, not one, I have tried for a year — I understand how it is *done*. The making. The whole procedure. It is simple. It is so simple that the simplicity is the most frightening thing I have ever encountered, and I am not frightened, I am *finally at rest*, and I think, with total calm, in the middle of the roar:

*I know what I was supposed to know.*

And underneath the flood, very quietly, going the other way:

something is leaving.

Out through my arm. Out through the fizzing place where my hand ought to be. Steady, unhurried, entirely uninterested in me — a current running out while the ocean runs in — and I feel it for perhaps a second, and I have no room left to wonder what it is or where it is going, and it is the single most important thing that happens to me in my life.

Behind me, from the doorway, a woman's voice says: "Thank you."

The chord stops.

Everything I have been given goes with it, all at once, the way a name goes when you turn to say it — and there is a silence so complete that it has a shape, a held breath in a room the size of the universe —

and I wake up in my own bed, in Nor Yesey, at 6:41 on the morning of the twenty-sixth of August, with my arm still shaking.

And I remember all of it.
