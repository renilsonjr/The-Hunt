Uxies woke into weather.

That was ordinary. It had been weather her whole life: the towers going up into it, the sky moving over the city at a speed that visitors from the southern reaches always remarked on and residents had stopped seeing before they could walk. Her window ran with it. The light came in the colour the light came in.

What was not ordinary was that she woke slowly.

She lay in it for some time before she understood what she was doing, which was *resting*, in the way she had watched other people rest for fifty-one years and had privately concluded was a thing they exaggerated for social reasons. Nothing in her was reaching. Nothing in her was leaning very slightly toward some direction she could never identify and had stopped trying to describe out loud at about the age of twenty.

There was a stillness in the middle of her chest, warm, entirely at rest, of a colour she would have called gold if a colour were the sort of thing that could be inside a person.

It had arrived. It had been listening her whole life and it had arrived, and it had stopped.

And she remembered all of it: the yard, the fast sky, a man the wrong shape sitting down on the ground because he could not stand any more, telling her the truth in order, at speed, with his hands shaking on his knees. She remembered every word. She would still have every word when she was very old.

That, she understood later, was the difference between being asked and being used. The ones who are used wake up rested.

---

The hum had stopped.

Not in her flat. In the world.

She got out of bed and opened the window to the storm and stood in it, and she could hear the city — the argument, the traffic, the perpetual overlapping insistence of eleven million Virden all being right at each other — and underneath it, where there had always been something, there was now nothing.

Nobody had ever named it. That was the strange part. It had been under everything for as long as anyone's grandmother could remember, so far under that it registered as the sound a world makes rather than a sound anything in the world was making, and it had gone in the night, and by mid-morning it was the only thing anyone was talking about. People stood in the street with their heads on one side. Two separate broadcasts led on it. In the market under the west span an old man was telling anyone who came near him that he had said for sixty years that it was there and had been laughed at, and he was crying, and he was not the only one.

Uxies stood at her window with the rain coming in on her arms and thought, *that was the plant in the basement.*

She had never heard the phrase. She had it anyway, in a stranger's voice, in a language she did not speak.

---

They came at about the eleventh hour.

She saw the vehicle first, from the window: not a marked one, nothing official, a plain grey thing that parked with its nose to the wall in a bay that had been empty every morning of the nine years she had lived there. Two of them got out and did not come to the door. They stood on the far side of the road, in the rain, and looked up at her building without any attempt to disguise the looking, because they were not accustomed to needing to.

She looked back at them for a while.

She had spent fifty-one years being observed by something that had written *unexplained* against her four hundred and eleven times, and had done it so lightly and so continuously that she had never once felt watched — and now, on the first morning of the rest of it, they had sent a car, and two people, and a stare.

*This is the first thing you will ever have done that they'll see*, the man had said. And: *I think that stops being true about four seconds afterwards.*

Uxies of Earth 2 stood at her own window in her own city and discovered, with some interest, that she was not afraid of them.

They had known what she was for half a century and had spent the whole of it making sure that nothing ever happened to her. They had kept her small on purpose. They had let a sister cry over her. They had watched a nine-year-old walk out to an industrial yard on the edge of the city because the noise stopped there, and had written it down, and had written it down again, four hundred and eleven times, and had never once said a word to her.

The one entity in two galaxies that had ever bothered to *explain her to herself* had come out of the dark for one night, at the cost of what she was fairly sure had been his life, and had asked.

She put her hand flat on the glass, deliberately, so they would see it. Then she went and got dressed.

---

She went to her sister's first, which surprised her and did not surprise her sister.

She did not tell her the whole of it. She told her one part, sitting in the kitchen with the storm going over: that the thing that had been wrong with her all her life had never been a hole in her, that it had been something *of hers* trying to hear something a long way off, and that it had heard it now, and that she was — she used the word carefully, having never used it about herself — *finished*, in the sense of complete.

Her sister put both hands over her mouth and cried for the second time in Uxies' hearing about the same subject, and this time Uxies let her, and sat with her, and did not walk out into the weather.

---

In the late afternoon she went out to the yard.

She took the same route she had taken since she was nine — under the west span, along the freight road, through the gap in the fence that had been a gap for four decades — and she came out into the flat open place with the stacks in it and the rail and the fast sky.

And it was loud.

Not louder than anywhere else. Just: ordinary. The city came into the yard the way the city came into everywhere, traffic and weather and the far-off argument, and the pocket was gone. There was no quiet left in it at all.

Uxies stood in the middle of the yard in the noise for a long time.

She had thought she would grieve it. She had come out expecting to. But the quiet had never been the point of the place; the quiet had been the *conditions*, a thing she had needed the way you need a dark room to see a faint light, and the light was not faint any more and was not out here, and the yard was just a yard. Four hundred and eleven visits. It had done its work.

She said, out loud, to a flat industrial yard on the edge of a city on the wrong side of a wall made of air: "Thank you."

Nothing heard it. She knew that, now, better than almost anyone alive: that on this side of the Barrier there is nothing listening, that there never had been, that the whole vast machinery of her world had been built around the fact of an absence — and that the presence she had spent her life reaching for had been on the other side the entire time, and had just, this morning, gone quiet for everyone.

She did not mind.

She stood in the noisy yard with the storm coming over and the golden stillness sitting warm and finished in the middle of her, and she looked up, past the towers, at a sky that would not show her a single star until the weather broke and had never in fifty-one years shown her the one she wanted.

She put her hand on her chest, over it.

Somewhere out there was a man who was almost certainly dead, who had crossed a distance she had no arithmetic for, in order to sit down on the ground in front of her and tell her the truth before he asked her for anything.

Nobody had ever told her his name.

She began, that afternoon, to look for it.
