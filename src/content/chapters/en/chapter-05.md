His name was Fridan, and he was thirty-four years old, and he lived on the fourth floor of a building in Nor Yesey with a window that faced a parking structure.

He came awake all at once, the way you do when a phone falls off a table, and lay flat on his back in the grey light with his heart going and his right arm shaking from the shoulder down.

The shaking lasted about forty seconds. He watched it. He held the arm up in front of his face and watched the tremor run out of it in stages, and the hair on the forearm stayed standing long after the shaking stopped, every follicle up, a field of it, so that when he finally ran his other hand along the skin it felt like the nap of something.

There was pressure in both ears. It had a sound in it, or under it — not a ringing, lower than that, a held thing on the very edge of what a room can contain. It faded over about four minutes and he lay still through all four of them, listening to it go, the way you listen to a train you have decided not to get on.

The clock said 6:41.

The window said Tuesday: the parking structure, the top deck wet, a man in a fleece walking to a car with a lunch bag.

---

He did not think *what a dream.*

That was the first thing wrong and he registered it while it was happening. There was no swim, no reassembly, none of the small routine embarrassment of a man reorganising a room around himself. He knew where he was, and he knew where he had been, and both of them were simply true, side by side, with nothing in his head prepared to rank them.

He got up. He went into the kitchen and stood in the middle of it.

Then he went back to the bedroom, took his phone off the charger, sat down on the edge of the mattress, and started to write.

He wrote a header first, because he had the sense — vague, insistent, procedural — that this would need to be a document rather than a note.

> **The Hunt — REM — Dream 1 — 08/26/26 — Nor Yesey — UN**

Then, underneath, because he did not know what else to call it:

> *Script of what I remember from my dream*

He wrote for fifty minutes with his thumbs. He got the coats. He got the man with the ring and the apple lying on its side and the announcement in the pleasant voice. He got the footbridge, and the rail going red — he wrote *it made a train rail red so I could follow that* and stopped and looked at the sentence for a while, and did not change it. He got the woman in the grey suit. He got the sixteen doors, and the wear in the carpet, and the man leaning on a wall two feet from a door.

He got the ball, and the two marks in front and the one behind, and he drew them, badly, with his thumb.

He wrote the chord. He wrote *like those sound tracks* and could not finish the comparison and left it unfinished.

He did not write down the last thing anyone said in that room.

He noticed himself not writing it. He noticed the small deliberate step around it, the way you notice yourself walking around a wet patch on a floor, and he told himself he would come back to it, and he did not come back to it for eleven days.

---

The thing that broke him, in the end, was not the size of any of it. It was the durability.

He knew what dreams did. Everyone knows what dreams do. You wake with a whole country in your head and by the time the kettle boils you have three images and a mood, and by lunch you have one image and by evening you have the fact that there was a dream. That is not forgetting. That is what dreams *are* — weather, structurally incapable of persisting.

At 11:20 that morning, in a meeting he had not cancelled, Fridan looked at a wall and checked.

The bowl. Three apples. Long table, dark wood, and the room had been cold, and the light had come from the window on the left, and the apple on its side had been the one nearest him, and there had been a dust-shadow on the table where something round had been moved.

He had not written down the dust-shadow.

It was there. All of it was there — not remembered, *available*, in the way the layout of his own flat was available, and when he went looking for the far end of the third-floor corridor he found the exact angle of the young man's phone torch and the pattern of the older man's shirt, which had been checked, small blue check, and which he had never once consciously looked at.

He sat in the meeting with his hands flat on his knees.

Nothing had gone. Twelve hours later nothing had gone, and by the following Sunday nothing had gone, and a year after that he could still tell you which of the sixteen doors had been the one with the child's drawing (the fifth, on the right) — and it was the single most frightening fact of his life, and he could not explain to anybody why, because to everyone he ever tried it on it sounded like a man boasting about his memory.

It was not memory. He knew the difference. Memory is a thing you make. This had been *installed.*

---

He started talking to her that afternoon.

It began reasonably. He was washing a cup, and he said, out loud, in the tone of a man testing a light switch, "Are you there?"

The tap ran. The building did its afternoon noises.

He said, "I know you only get it when I say it. So I'm saying it."

Nothing.

He put the cup on the rack and dried his hands and stood with the towel in them for a while, and then he tried the words that had worked, exactly, in the order they had worked, because he had them, because he had everything: "I don't know where to go."

It had taken her no time at all on the footbridge. **I know**, she had said, before he had finished being afraid.

The fridge cycled on.

---

He tried, over the next four days, and he was methodical about it, because being methodical is what a frightened man does with his hands.

He tried in the dark, lying flat, because that was closest to the conditions. He tried in the shower with the water loud, because the rail had been loud, in its way. He tried at 4 a.m., which was the hour of the second building — he set an alarm for it, twice — sitting up in bed in the cold speaking clearly into a room with the lamp off.

He went up to the roof of his own building at five one morning and stood at the parapet in the wind, because she had first spoken to him on the top of a building, and he had a theory about that for about two days, and the theory was wrong.

He said please. He was not proud of it and he wrote it down later anyway.

He worked out, on the fourth day, that he had no name for her — that in one entire night she had not given him one and he had not asked, and that every human being who had ever tried to reach anything like her had at least had a name to put in the sentence. He did not even have a pronoun she had agreed to. He had been calling her *she* since the footbridge on the strength of nothing but the shape of a voice.

That was the first day he understood, properly, in his body, that he had never known who he was talking to.

---

And she had never lied to him.

He kept arriving back at that, from every direction, and it did not help, it made it worse.

She had said *that is your route* and it had been. She had said *there is a gate; it is not locked tonight* and it had not been. She had said *they are expecting you* and they had been standing in the lobby at three in the morning, waiting, exactly as described. Every single thing she had said had been true.

And laid end to end, every true thing had been a direction. She had told him where to go, and when to go over a fence at the post instead of the panel, and that he was doing it correctly, and she had never in one entire night told him a single fact about the world he was standing in.

He had asked her what it looked like and she had said *you will know it.*

He had asked her if she would still be here after and she had said *take it.*

He sat with the notebook — he had a paper one by then; the phone had started to feel like the wrong instrument — and wrote the two of them out one under the other and looked at them for a long time.

A thing that cannot lie, he wrote, and then crossed it out, because he did not know that. He wrote: *a thing that did not lie.*

Then he wrote: *and did not answer.*

---

There were other things and he catalogued them without knowing what he was cataloguing.

His arm was fine. There was nothing to see. But three or four times a day the hair would go up along it, wrist to elbow, for no reason and in no cold, and settle again, like a field under a passing shadow.

He stopped being interested in food and did not lose weight.

And once, on the Thursday, sitting at the kitchen table with a pen in his hand and the notebook open, he became aware that the room was very quiet, and understood after a moment that the quiet was inside him, and that he had not taken a breath for some considerable time.

He did not know how long. That was the part he came back to. He had not been holding his breath — holding it is work, you feel the work — he had simply stopped, the way you stop tapping a foot, and had gone on sitting there writing with no more sense of lack than a man who has stopped tapping his foot.

He breathed in, deliberately, and his body accepted it politely, the way you accept a drink you did not want.

He wrote in the notebook: *ask a doctor.* He underlined it. He did not ask a doctor, then or ever, and when he tried later to reconstruct why, the honest answer was that some part of him had already decided it did not want a test whose result could be *nothing is wrong with you.*

---

On the eleventh day he wrote down the last thing anyone said in that room.

He wrote: *When I picked it up, a woman's voice behind me said thank you.*

He looked at it.

Everything else in the document was something that had happened to him. That one line was the only sentence in seven pages in which somebody else got something.

They had walked him in off a street. They had put him in a flat furnished from a life they had modelled closely enough to get the crease in a book. They had told him to rest, and let him not rest, and stood in corridors asking him what he was looking for — and at the end of it, at the exact moment he had his hand in the middle of the only thing he had ever wanted, a woman had stood in the doorway and been *grateful.*

Not to him. He had gone back over the two words perhaps two hundred times by then, the pitch of them, where the stress fell. She had not been thanking him. She had said it the way a person says it into a phone at the end of a call that has gone well.

Fridan put the pen down and sat in his kitchen in Nor Yesey with the parking structure going about its Tuesday outside the window, and said, aloud, to the empty room, in a perfectly level voice:

"What did they take?"

And the fridge cycled on, and a car door closed in the street, and somewhere below him a television went from one programme to another, and nothing in the universe answered him — not then, and not for a long time afterwards, and never again in the way it once had.
