He built with them for five weeks and lied the whole way through it, and he was better at that than he had any right to be.

It helped that most of it was true. He did want the thing built. He did work on it properly — he gave them his hours and his attention and the peculiar detailed usefulness of a man who forgets nothing, and when Anser asked him for a rehearsal of the delivery he rehearsed it, out loud, in an empty flat, forty times, until the sentence came off his tongue at the same speed every time.

He was also, by the middle of March, quite badly ill.

The thin air had stopped being a sensation and become a condition. He slept sitting up because lying down had begun to feel like being held under. He carried a bag now with a bottle of water in it and did not drink from it and could not have said what it was for. He had lost a stone since Christmas. Twice at work he had put his hand flat on a desk to keep the room where it was, and the second time somebody had asked him, with real concern, whether he had seen anyone about it, and he had said yes, and had gone into a stairwell afterward and stood there for four minutes.

Once — this was the one that frightened him — he came to at his own kitchen table with the notebook open and eleven lines in his own handwriting that he did not remember writing.

He had them for the rest of his life, the way he had everything. They were not in a language.

---

The test was the best thing he ever did and it took him nine days to construct.

The plan, by then, had two halves. There was the **instant**: a single fixed second, agreed to a tolerance he did not fully understand, at which several million sleepers would say the sentence in unison and a man in Nor Yesey would say it aloud into air. And there was the **pointer**: the coordinates, the anchor, the description of the machinery — Anser's addition, the thing that turned a warning into a place.

If Anser wanted the operation stopped, the pointer was what mattered. The instant was arbitrary. Any second in any month would do; you would want it soon, and after that you would not care.

If Anser wanted something else — if what was actually being purchased here was a god's hand moving to a known part of the wall at a known moment — then the pointer could be anything, so long as it made her reach. And the instant would be the only thing in the plan that could not move.

So Fridan went to them and proposed, reasonably, in the voice of a tired collaborator making a sensible suggestion, that they change both.

He said the pointer worried him. He said it was too specific, that if the coordinates were wrong in any particular she would waste the one look she was ever going to give this, and that he had been thinking about a secondary site instead — a decoy, a broader anchor, something that would put her attention in the right *region* without staking everything on one address. Less precise. Safer.

And he said that the instant should move. He gave a reason that was pure fabrication and sounded like operational caution: he wanted it eleven days later, on a date he chose because it was meaningless.

Then he shut up and waited, and listened to which half of his own plan they defended.

---

They gave him the pointer inside a minute.

**"If you'd rather have the region, take the region,"** Anser said. **"You're the one who has to say it and I'd rather you said it in a state of belief than in a state of doubt. Draft it broad. We'll work with it."**

The instant did not move.

It did not move in a temper, and it did not move by refusal, and that was the part Fridan admired even while it was happening to him. Anser produced a reason. It was a good reason: a technical constraint about the population of sleepers, cycles, the width of the window in which a given hemisphere is in the right phase — detailed, coherent, checkable in none of its particulars, and delivered in the same level voice as everything else. And when Fridan pushed once, gently, Anser produced a second reason, which did not contradict the first and did not need it either.

Two reasons. In seven months, for everything else, there had never once been two.

Fridan lay in the dark and said, "All right," and felt the floor of the whole thing give way beneath him with almost no noise at all.

---

He waited four days, because he wanted to be certain and because he was frightened, and then he said it.

He was sitting in the chair by the open window at two in the morning with the blanket over him. He did not stand up. He did not raise his voice. He had thought about how he wanted to do it and had decided that he wanted to do it the way you would end anything, which is quietly and without an audience.

"You don't want it stopped," he said. "You want her to touch the wall."

Nothing, for a while. The extract fans. A car somewhere over toward the ring road.

**"Say the rest of it,"** said Anser.

"She can't be forced. Nothing can force her, there's no lever, she made the lever. But she'll *repair*. Show her a hole in her own floor and she will go and put her hand on it, because that's what she is — and the only way anybody has ever been going to get that wall to move is to give her a reason to move it herself." He was surprised at how level he sounded. "You don't need the message to arrive anywhere useful. You need her hand on the Barrier at a second you've known in advance for a year."

**"Yes."**

Fridan sat in the chair with the blanket and the cold and found he had nothing prepared for that. He had expected an argument. He had spent four days assembling one.

**"I'm not going to insult you with a denial,"** Anser said. **"You built a good test. It was better than anything I would have expected from you in August and I want to say so, because it's true and because there won't be another chance to say it."**

"The extraction's real."

**"The extraction is entirely real. Every word I have ever told you about it is accurate, including the number, and you should keep believing it, because the day it stops being true is a long way off."**

"But it isn't the point."

**"It's the yield,"** said Anser. **"It pays for the operation and it always has. It is also — and this is the part I would like you to hold on to, because it's the piece people get wrong — the only bait in the universe she cannot ignore forever. You do not aim a god. You do not persuade one. You leave something in her house that she will eventually find, and you make sure that when she finds it, the fixing of it requires her to reach for exactly the thing you want moved. That took eleven thousand years to arrange. You are the last four hundred days of it."**

---

He asked the rest of it in the order it came, and got answers, because they had always given him answers and there was no reason to stop now.

"Vera Lund."

**"Didn't take. Neither did the four hundred before her, or the eleven hundred after. It isn't a fault in them; it's very difficult, and the process is unforgiving, and a sleeper who wakes with nothing is a sleeper who goes back to their life. That's not a failure state, Fridan. That's the ordinary result."**

"And the one that works?"

**"Wakes up remembering,"** said Anser. **"And is very frightened, and very alone, and cannot tell anyone, and spends the following year doing exactly what you have done — which is to work out, without help, entirely on his own, that she must be warned. Nobody has to be persuaded of that. You cannot be talked out of it. It is the most reliable thing in the whole apparatus."**

Fridan closed his eyes.

"You didn't break the suppression."

**"No."**

"Somebody hacked the transmission. There was a second faction, and they got in, and that's why I remember — you told me that on the first night. That's the first thing you ever told me."

**"I told you we interrupted the process and that your memory was debris,"** said Anser, **"and that I would take credit I was not owed. You have that conversation somewhere in you exactly as it happened. Go and look at it."**

He looked at it. He did not need to go anywhere.

*We didn't do it to give you your memory. Your memory is debris. But it is yours, and it is the reason you and I can have this conversation, so I'll take the credit I'm not owed.*

"Then who broke it?"

**"There was nothing to break,"** said Anser.

---

Nobody had suppressed him.

That was it. That was the whole of it, and it took about a second and a half to arrive and he never got out from under it.

There had been no rival group and no interference and no failure in the night; there had been no accident, no lucky flaw, no moment when the machinery slipped and a man in New Jersey got to keep something he was not supposed to have. He had spent seven months in possession of a stolen fortnight, holding it the way you hold a thing you were not meant to have, and it had been *handed* to him. They had simply not taken it. They had run him, and got their yield, and then left the memory where it was and stood back and waited for it to do the only thing a memory like that can do to a decent man.

The woman in the grey suit had stood in a doorway and said thank you.

She had not been thanking him for the sphere. The sphere was the yield; the sphere had been in the bag before he touched it. She had been standing in the doorway of a small room on a floor of sixteen furnished flats, watching an ordinary man with his hand in the middle of the only thing he had ever wanted, and what she had been grateful for was that it had *worked* — that the thing they had spent eleven thousand years building was finally, on the four hundred and something-th attempt, standing in front of her with a whole night's memory in his head and a conscience to run it on.

He had walked out of that building carrying their message.

He had been carrying it ever since. He had carried it to Halverton and up onto a roof and into a launderette in February. He had built the delivery system himself, at his kitchen table, out of an envelope and eleven days of arithmetic, and he had been so *proud* of it —

Fridan got up out of the chair, went into the bathroom, and was sick until there was nothing left.

---

He came back and sat down and pulled the blanket over himself again, because it was March and the window was open and he could not close it.

**"You still need us,"** Anser said, not unkindly. **"That hasn't changed and it isn't going to. Whatever you now think of me, there is one machine on this planet capable of putting a sentence into a large number of minds at once, and you have no other way to reach her, and I would rather you got there by thinking it through than by exhausting yourself first."**

"No."

**"Then what are you going to use?"**

And Fridan sat in the dark in his flat in Nor Yesey with his eyes closed and his mouth shut, and did not answer.

It was, he thought — with the small clear part of himself that had never stopped taking notes, even that night, even then — the first time in his life he had ever been on the other end of that.

He understood, at last, exactly how much it told you.
