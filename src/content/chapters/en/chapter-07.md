The name came on the third night, and it came without being asked for, which Fridan understood later was the whole technique.

**"Vera Lund,"** Anser said. **"Fourteen Cowl Street, Halverton. She ran nineteen years ago and she woke with nothing, which is what the process is supposed to produce. She is sixty-eight and she is well. You want to see one, and I would rather you saw one than spent a year deciding whether I invented them."**

"You're sending me to a stranger's house."

**"I'm telling you an address. You'll decide the rest on the train."**

He lay awake afterwards until it got light, in a bed he had gone back to using, and did the arithmetic on himself. Eleven days he had spent on roofs and in bathrooms saying *are you there* into the dark, and now something else had a name and gave him instructions and he was already looking up the trains. He saw it clearly. He watched himself see it.

He went on Saturday.

---

Halverton took two hours and eleven minutes and was a town of the kind that has one of everything. Cowl Street ran up from the church and number fourteen was in a terrace of six, a red door, a small square of garden with a rose in it cut back hard for the winter.

He had prepared a lie on the train and he was ashamed of how good it was.

He told her he was compiling accounts of unusual sleep for a project on the long-term recall of dreams; that she had come up through a survey she would have filled in years ago and forgotten; that it was entirely voluntary and he could leave at any point. Every part of it was designed to be checkable and none of it was true, and he delivered it standing on a doorstep with his hands out of his pockets, and Vera Lund listened to all of it with her head tipped and then said, "You've come from the city on a Saturday for this?"

"Yes."

"Well then you'd better come in, because I've got a cake going stale."

---

The front room was warm and crowded in the way of a house where one person has lived for a long time. Photographs on the sideboard, three generations of them. A chair with a depression in it that was clearly hers, and the other chair, which was clearly for guests, and which had a cushion on it that had been plumped, he thought, some time before he arrived.

She talked while the kettle went. She had been a school secretary for thirty-one years. Her husband had died in the spring of a year she named without ceremony. Her son was in the west and rang on Sundays and her daughter was closer and rang less, which she reported as a fact of physics rather than a complaint.

She brought the tea in on a tray. She put it down on a low table by the window.

On the table there was a case for reading glasses. Brown leather, hard-shell, the seam split a little at one end. And on the front of it, near the clasp, the leather had gone pale and smooth in a patch the size of a thumb.

Fridan looked at it for what he was fairly sure was too long.

Nineteen years ago somebody had modelled a woman's life closely enough to put that on a table in a furnished flat in a building that did not exist, in a city with no name, on the third floor, behind a door that most people could not see.

He had stood over it. He had picked it up. He remembered — because he remembered everything — that he had put it back down at a slight angle and had thought, at the time, that the angle didn't matter.

"That's an old thing," said Vera Lund, following his eyes. "It was my mother's."

"It's nice."

"It's not nice, it's split. But there we are."

She poured the tea.

---

He asked his questions in the order he had written them out, because they were plausible questions and because holding the paper gave his hands something to do.

Did she remember her dreams generally. Not much. Had she ever kept a dream diary. Good heavens no. Was there any period she'd describe as unusual.

"Well," she said. "There's the fortnight."

And Fridan, with a cup halfway up, said, "The fortnight."

"Oh, it's nothing. It's a family joke — my husband used to bring it out at dinner and I'd want to go through the floor." She settled back. She was enjoying herself, and the enjoyment was the worst thing in the room. "Nineteen years ago, thereabouts. Late summer. I slept like the dead for about two weeks. Went to bed at ten, woke at seven, out like a light, every single night. And I *never* slept like that, I was a four-times-a-night woman all my life."

"And after the fortnight?"

"Back to normal. Exactly as before." She laughed. "Roy said I'd been away. That was his joke, for years. He'd say, *she went away for a fortnight in the August and she's never told me where.*"

The clock on the sideboard went on with what it was doing.

"Anything else about that time?" Fridan said. "Anything at all. However small."

"Only the note."

---

She got up and went to the sideboard and opened the second drawer down without hesitating, which told him it had been moved from house to house and kept in the second drawer down for nineteen years.

It was a piece of paper about the size of a postcard, torn along one edge from a larger sheet. It had been folded and unfolded enough times that the fold was soft.

"I found this on the kitchen table," she said, "on a Tuesday morning in that fortnight, in my own handwriting, and I have no more idea what it means than the man in the moon."

She handed it to him.

Two small marks side by side. And underneath, a little apart, a third.

Below that, in a hand that was recognisably hers from the note by the telephone and was also not quite hers — pressed too hard, the letters not joined where she joined them everywhere else — four words:

*it was on a shelf*

---

Fridan sat in a warm front room in Halverton with a piece of paper in his hand and understood that he was going to be sick, and that being sick would require an explanation, and so he was not sick.

"Do you know," Vera said comfortably, "I kept it because it frightened me. Isn't that silly? Not the words. Just — finding my own writing that I couldn't remember doing. You feel like there's someone else in the house."

"Yes," Fridan said.

"I asked Roy if he'd written it as a joke and he hadn't, and he wasn't a good enough liar to have got away with it." She took the paper back, refolded it along its own fold, and put it in the drawer, and closed the drawer. "There. That's my unusual sleep. I hope it's some use to your project."

She looked up at him then, and something in her face changed a little, and she said, in a different voice, "Are you all right, love? You've gone very white."

And here is the thing that Fridan carried out of that house and never put down.

He had it all. He had every piece. He could have told her what she had done, and where she had walked, and that she had been *good* at it — she had got as far as a shelf, nineteen years ago, further than anyone in that building had got before him — and that a version of her mother's glasses case had sat on a table in a room built out of her life to make her comfortable while she was used. He could have given a sixty-eight-year-old woman in Halverton the missing fortnight of her life, and the reason her husband's joke had made her want to go through the floor for nineteen years, which was that some part of her had known it was not a joke.

He could not say one word of it.

Not because he did not trust her. Because the room was in the world, and the world was full of air, and anything he said aloud in it went straight to something that had been listening to him for eleven days and had not answered once, and he did not know what it would do with the knowledge, and neither did Anser, and *the honest position is that neither of us should find out by accident.*

So he said: "Long train. I should get back for the four-forty."

She saw him to the door. She said it had been nice to have someone in the house. She stood on the step in her cardigan with her arms folded against the cold, and she waved when he got to the end of Cowl Street, and he made himself turn and wave back.

---

He was on the platform at Halverton for twenty minutes.

The man came and stood a polite distance away — four feet, near enough to speak, far enough to be nothing — and looked up the line the way everybody looks up the line, and after a while he said, without turning:

"That's a long way to come for an afternoon."

He was perhaps forty. Grey coat, good shoes, a canvas bag over one shoulder. His face had nothing in it at all. Fridan looked at him and had, instantly and uselessly, every detail: a small scar through one eyebrow, a wedding ring, a paperback in the outside pocket of the bag with a supermarket receipt used as a bookmark.

"Do I know you?"

"No," the man said. He sounded like he meant it kindly. "I want to be straightforward with you, because I think you've had a fortnight of people not being. I'm not going to hurt you. I couldn't tell you how, honestly, it's not that sort of arrangement. I'm asking you to stop."

"Stop what."

"Going to houses." The man shifted the bag on his shoulder. "You've been to one. There'll be a temptation to go to more. It doesn't do them any good, and I'd like you to think about that part rather than the part about me."

"Who do you work for?"

And the man said, "I don't know."

He said it plainly, without any of the weight it should have had, in the voice of a man being asked about his pension arrangements.

"I've never met anybody," he said. "It's an account. I get told a name and a town and I go and I write down what I saw and it pays extremely well and it has been, up until fairly recently, the easiest money in the world. I've got two at university." He glanced sideways. "I've been doing this eleven years. You're the first one that's ever looked back at me."

The rails ticked. Somewhere up the line the signal changed.

"You're not the only one on this," the man said. "I want to be honest about that too. Some of it isn't people. Some of it comes in through the sleeping, and that's the bit you want to be careful about, because you can't lock that."

Fridan said, "What happens if I don't stop?"

The man thought about it. He actually thought — Fridan watched him do it, and it took several seconds, and there was no menace in any of it, which was why it went into him like a nail.

"Nothing happens," he said at last. "Nobody comes for you. That's not how it's done and I don't think anyone knows how to do it that way." He picked up his bag properly as the train came into sight at the end of the platform. "You'll just get tired, is all. Everybody does. You'll get tired, and one night you'll sleep properly, the way you used to. And whatever it is you've got, you'll have it a bit less. And then a bit less than that."

He stepped back to let Fridan on first, which was somehow the most frightening thing that had happened all day.

"Get some rest," he said. "I mean that in the ordinary way."

---

Fridan rode two hours and eleven minutes back to Nor Yesey with his forehead against the glass and the fields going by in the dark.

They did not need to find him. They did not need to threaten him or follow him or take anything off him. They only had to wait for the one thing every living human being does, every night, without deciding to — and the whole of the world's machinery would do the rest while he lay in his own bed with his mouth shut.

*You should rest*, the woman in the grey suit had said, in a warm flat furnished from a book he had creased on a train.

He had thought, for a fortnight, that the terrible thing about that night was what had been done to him while he slept.

Sitting on the 6:12 out of Halverton, he understood that the terrible thing was much simpler, and that he had been carrying it since the moment he woke: he was a man who was going to have to sleep, every night, for the rest of his life, in a house with a door he could not lock.
