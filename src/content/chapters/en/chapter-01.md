The room is too warm and nobody has taken off their coat.

That is the first thing I notice, and I notice it the way you notice things here: completely, and without being able to say when I started. There are perhaps thirty people in a room built for more, standing in the loose arrangement of people who have been told to wait somewhere expensive. The ceiling is high and dark. The light comes from low down, from lamps on side tables, so everyone's face is lit from underneath and nobody looks quite like themselves.

They are all wearing coats. Long ones, good ones. It is warm enough that the back of my neck is damp and not one of them has so much as opened a button, and I understand — not by reasoning, just by knowing, the way I know everything in this place — that taking off your coat here would mean you intended to stay, and none of them intend to stay. They intend to win and leave.

I am not wearing a coat.

I did not bring one. I am in the clothes I put on this morning, whatever morning that was, and the difference is the loudest thing in the room. Nobody says anything about it. A woman near the window looks at me the way you look at a chair that has been moved.

Then a man beside me speaks without turning his head.

"First time."

It is not a question, so I don't answer it. He is sixty, maybe, with a heavy ring and a face that has been photographed a lot. He is watching the far end of the room, where a door has not yet opened.

"They usually bring one," he says. "Every run. Somebody who doesn't know the rules." He tips his glass an inch toward me, a toast so small it would be deniable. "It keeps the rest of us honest."

"Brought by who?"

He smiles at the door. He doesn't answer, and I understand that he thinks I already know, and that pretending otherwise would be a kind of rudeness.

---

Here is what I know, and I have no idea how I know it.

I know that everyone in this room is here to find one specific object, and that only one of us will. I know that the object is small. I know it is somewhere in this building and that the building is larger on the inside than the outside allowed for, and that this is not strange, it is simply a property of the building, the way a coastline is long.

I know that there are rules and that these people are not going to explain them to me.

And I know — this is the part I would not be able to explain to anyone, afterwards — that I am going to be the one who finds it.

Not that I might. That I am. It sits in me with the flat certainty of a remembered fact, like the date of my own birthday. I have never in my life been certain of anything the way I am certain of this, and the strangeness of that should frighten me and instead it steadies me, the way the ground steadies you.

I am the only person in this room who does not belong here, and I am the only person in this room who is going to win.

Both are true. Neither cancels the other.

---

The door at the far end opens and the room stops.

Nobody comes through it. It simply stands open onto a corridor with a runner of dark red carpet, and the people in coats begin to move toward it in ones and twos, unhurried, the way people move at a funeral. There is no announcement. Everyone knew, and everyone was waiting to be seen not hurrying.

I go last, because I don't know where I am going, and because I would rather watch which way they turn.

The corridor is long and the carpet swallows the sound. There are doors on both sides. Some of them are open onto rooms with the lights off — I catch the shapes of furniture under sheets, a piano, a wall of books turned spine-in so the shelves are a wall of paper edges. The building has the smell of a place that is cleaned but not lived in. Wax and cold air and, underneath, very faintly, incense.

That is when I understand what this reminds me of.

It is not an auction, which is what the coats and the low voices first suggested. It is a service. These people are not shopping. They are observing something, and the room upstairs was a narthex, and every one of them knows the responses and I do not.

Somewhere ahead of me, someone laughs, once, and stops.

---

I search the second floor for what feels like an hour and might be four minutes.

The rooms give onto each other in ways that don't reconcile. I go through a study into a bedroom into a bathroom into the same study from a different door, and I accept this, because arguing with it would waste time I don't have. I open drawers. I put my hand into the gap behind a radiator and find dust and a dead moth. I lift the lid of a window seat and find a stack of folded curtains, and I put my hand all the way down the side of them, and there is nothing.

The thing I am looking for is small. I know that. I do not know what it looks like, and this ought to be a problem and is not, because I also know that I will recognise it. That is simply part of the arrangement.

Twice, people pass the doorway while I am working. They slow down. One of them — a younger woman with her hair pinned up, coat open at last — stands in the doorway for a moment and watches me go through the curtains, and when I look up she doesn't pretend she wasn't looking.

"You're very thorough," she says.

"Is that against the rules?"

She considers this seriously, which is worse than if she had laughed. "No," she says. "It's just unusual. Most people look where they've been told to look."

She goes. I stand there holding a curtain.

*Told by whom* is a question I do not ask, because asking it would admit I have not been told anything, and some instinct older than this building says that admitting that would end my run.

---

The clue, when it comes, is not a clue in any way I could defend afterwards.

I am in a room on the third floor with a long table in it, and on the table there is a bowl, and in the bowl there are three apples, and one of them is on its side.

That is all it is. Three apples in a bowl and one of them lying on its side, and the moment I see it, something in my chest goes *there* — not a thought, a physical event, like a hand closing.

I stand in the doorway and look at the apple and feel my pulse in my throat.

It is not the apple. I check the bowl. I check under the bowl. I put my hand into the fruit and feel the cold waxy skins and there is nothing there but fruit. The apple means nothing.

But the *feeling* means something. The feeling is a bearing. The room is right, or the floor is right, or the direction I was facing when I saw it is right — the certainty has narrowed, the way a pain narrows when you press near it, and I turn slowly on the spot in that empty room with a bowl of fruit and try to find the direction that makes it strongest.

North wall. Or through it.

I am reaching for the handle of the door in the north wall when the lights change.

---

They don't go out. They go *down*, all of them, everywhere in the building at once, by perhaps a fifth — enough that the room dims and the window becomes a mirror and I see myself standing there with my hand out.

Then a voice, from nowhere in particular, pleasant and unhurried, the way a train station tells you a platform has changed.

"The run at this location is now concluded. Thank you."

That is all. No name. No apology.

I stand with my hand on the door.

*Now?* I want to say. *Now, when I have it, when it's through this wall, when I am four feet away?* — and even as the thought forms I know how it would sound, and I know that the people in coats are already walking back toward the stairs without breaking stride, because the announcement has told them something it has not told me, and they have heard this voice before.

I open the door anyway.

There is a landing behind it, and a window, and a stairwell going down, and no object, and no feeling. The bearing is gone. It went out with the lights, cleanly, like a switch.

Behind me someone says, kindly, "You'll want to come down. They don't wait."

---

They gather us in the entrance hall.

The doors stand open onto the night and the cold comes in over the threshold in a slow sheet, and the people in coats are grateful for the coats at last. Cars are arriving outside. Not taxis. Long, dark, patient cars, arriving in an order that nobody is directing.

The man with the ring finds me again. He looks almost sorry.

"You were close," he says.

"How would you know that?"

"Because they stopped it." He says it the way you'd explain rain. "They don't stop a run for the ones who are lost."

He steps down onto the gravel and a door is opened for him and he is gone, and I am standing in the doorway of a building I could not draw, at an hour I could not name, in a city I have not once looked at properly, and for the first time all night the certainty is not quite silent — it has moved, it has gone *ahead* of me, the way a hand pulls you when you're still deciding to walk.

There is another building. I don't know where.

I know I am going.

I go down the steps into the cold, and I do not get into any of the cars, and nobody stops me.
