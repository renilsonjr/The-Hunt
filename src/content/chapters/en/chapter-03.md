There are five of them and they all stand up.

Not at once — that would be a thing you'd notice and object to. They come up out of the lobby chairs in a ragged sequence over about four seconds, the way people rise when someone they've been waiting for finally comes through a door and they'd rather not make a performance of it.

Nobody is behind the reception desk. The desk is lit and staffed by no one and has been, I think, for some hours.

"You made good time," says the woman nearest me.

She is somewhere in her forties, in a grey suit that fits, with a lanyard she isn't wearing but is holding, folded, in one hand. Her manner is the manner of a very good hotel: attentive without pressure, a half-step back from where you'd expect. She does not offer her name and I do not think to ask for it, which afterwards will strike me as the single most efficient thing anyone does to me all night.

"Good time from where," I say.

She smiles like I've made a joke that's a little old between us. "You'll be tired," she says. "We've put you on nine."

---

The lift is mirrored on three sides and I watch five reflections of myself watching five reflections, and none of them is wearing a coat.

Two of the others have come up with us. They stand behind me, which I do not like, and they don't speak, which I like less. In the mirror I can see one of them looking at the back of my head with the flat, unembarrassed attention of a man watching a screen.

"What's the event," I say.

"Tomorrow evening," the woman says. "You're early. Most people are."

"Early for what."

The floor numbers climb. She lets three of them go by.

"You'll want to eat something," she says. "There's a kitchen."

---

The apartment is on the ninth floor and the door has no number.

Inside it is warm and low-lit and entirely ordinary: a short hall, a kitchen off it, a sitting room with two sofas at right angles, a bedroom through a door left ajar so I can see the corner of a made bed. There are lamps on. Somebody turned lamps on in here before I arrived.

"Bathroom's through there," she says from the doorway. "Anything you need, the phone goes to the desk."

"There's nobody at the desk."

"The phone goes to the desk," she says, pleasantly, and it is not a correction, it is the same sentence again, and the fact that she does not adjust it by even one word is the first thing tonight that actually raises the hair on my arms.

Then she says: "You should rest."

"I'm not tired."

"You should rest," she says, and smiles, and closes the door.

The door does not lock. I check, immediately, ashamed of myself and checking anyway: the handle turns, the door opens onto the corridor, the corridor is empty and carpeted and silent. Nothing stops me. Nothing has stopped me all night. I keep noticing that nothing stops me, and I keep failing to draw the obvious conclusion, which is that nobody needs to stop a man who is already going where you want him.

I close the door and stand in the little hall.

The flat is furnished the way a show home is furnished — everything chosen, nothing owned. Two sofas nobody has sat on. A bowl on the counter with nothing in it.

On the low table by the window there is a book.

It is face-down, splayed open to keep the page, spine up. I know the cover. I know the exact whiteness of the crease down the spine, because I made that crease, on a train, four years ago, forcing the book flat with one hand because I had a coffee in the other.

I stand over it for a long time without touching it.

Then I pick it up and look at the page it is open to, and it is the page I stopped at, and I put it back down face-down exactly as it was, and I go and stand in the kitchen with both hands flat on the counter until my breathing is my own again.

*They have been somewhere they should not have been.* That's what I get. That's the whole of what I can build from it, standing in that kitchen — that somebody has been in my life, close enough to know a crease in a book.

The correct conclusion is available. It is right there. It is that they did not come and look at my things, they *derived* them; that a book with a crease in it is not surveillance but arithmetic; that everything in this flat is a prediction with a very small error bar.

I don't reach it. Not for months.

---

I don't rest.

I go out into the corridor at what must be four in the morning and I start on nine and I work down.

It is a strange kind of searching, and I get better at it as the night goes on. I am not looking for a small blue object in a building with two hundred rooms; that would be hopeless and I would have given up on the third floor. I am looking for the feeling. The apple in the bowl, the *there* in my chest — I am hunting for that, and the rooms are only the medium it might arrive through.

So I walk. I open doors that open. I stand in the middle of empty function rooms with my eyes shut, waiting to be pulled.

Twice, she says something.

**"Not this floor."** And, later, so quiet it is almost not there: **"Slower."**

That's all. She has gone thin in here. On the rail she was a warm room; in this building she is a voice through a wall, and I find myself talking out loud in empty corridors to keep hold of her, saying things like *still here?* and *anything?*, and getting, most of the time, nothing at all.

---

I start seeing the others on the sixth floor.

The first is a man at the end of a corridor with his hand flat against the wall, sliding it slowly along the paper as he walks, the way you'd feel for a hidden door. When he sees me he takes his hand off the wall and puts it in his pocket and says, "Can't sleep either," and walks past me toward the stairs.

The second is on five, standing on a chair with the ceiling tile pushed up, arm in the void to the shoulder. She looks at me over her shoulder without hurrying and says, "Bulb's gone."

There is no light fitting anywhere near her.

By the fourth floor I have stopped pretending I don't see them and they have stopped pretending they aren't looking, and what settles between us is not hostility. It is much worse than hostility. It is the mutual, professional courtesy of people at the same job on different contracts.

They are searching. They have been searching a great deal longer than me, in a building they clearly know better than me — and they are searching *badly*. That's the thing I keep coming back to, on the stairs between three and four, my legs going. They open cupboards at random. They feel along walls. They put an arm up into a ceiling void because a ceiling void is a place a small thing could be.

They are guessing.

They are guessing, and I am being *pulled*, and the difference is not a difference of degree.

---

The woman in the grey suit finds me on two, at some hour with no name, sitting on the bottom step of a stairwell with my elbows on my knees.

She sits down beside me. It is companionable. It is the least threatening thing anybody has done all night, which is precisely why my whole body goes still.

"You've been up all night," she says.

"So has everyone."

"Mm." She looks at her hands. "Can I ask you something? It's not a test."

"That's what someone would say before a test."

She laughs, and the laugh is real, I'd swear to it. Then: "What are you looking for?"

The corridor light hums. Somewhere above us a door closes.

And I understand it, sitting on that step, in one piece, complete, the way the certainty came in the first building — except this comes from me, and I know the difference now, I have learned the difference tonight, and that is how I know this one is mine:

*She does not know.*

Not *she's testing whether I'll admit it.* She does not know what it looks like. None of them do. Five people waiting in a lobby at three in the morning, a whole building of them feeling along walls and putting their arms into ceilings and asking the man who arrived last, at four in the morning, on a bottom step, *what are you looking for* — because they need him to say it out loud.

They cannot find it.

I can. That is what I am. That is what the coat was, and the certainty was, and the flat that was furnished from a book I creased on a train — I am not a competitor who was generously admitted. I am the instrument they could not build, walked into the building, and pointed at the floor.

I sit with it. It takes perhaps four seconds.

Then I say, "I'll know it when I see it," which is true, and is hers, not mine, and is the first thing I have said all night that I chose for what it would do to somebody else.

She nods slowly, like that's fair. She pats the step twice as she gets up, the way you'd say *well, anyway*.

"Third floor's nice," she says. "Quiet."

She goes up. I sit on the step.

And here is the part I would give a great deal to be able to explain, afterwards, to anyone who asks me how it happened — because everyone will ask, in the end, and they will all ask it the same way, with that particular tilt of the head:

I knew. On that step, at that hour, with my legs shaking from nine floors of stairs, I knew that I was being used, by people who had been in my life without entering it, to find a thing they wanted and could not reach.

And I got up and went to the third floor.

Because I wanted it. Not what they wanted, not what she wanted — *it*. The thing at the end of the pull. Whatever it was, it was the only thing that had ever been meant for me specifically, and I had been walking toward it all night, and I was closer than anyone had ever been.

That is the whole of my defence and I know exactly how it sounds.

The third floor is quiet.
