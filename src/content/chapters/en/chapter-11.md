The negotiation took four nights and Fridan was surprised to discover he was good at it.

He had something. That was the novelty. For six months he had been a man to whom things were explained, and now there was a plan that could not be run without a human being who could stand in a room on Earth 1 and say a sentence out loud at a fixed instant, and there was exactly one of him.

**"You understand this is not a favour I'm doing you,"** Anser said on the second night.

"I understand it's not a favour either of us is doing the other. That's why I trust it more than everything before it."

**"That's the most sensible thing you've said since August."**

The terms came out roughly even, which he thought about afterwards. He would get the machine — the reach, the simultaneity, the ability to put one sentence into a very large number of sleeping minds without a syllable of it crossing air first. They would get his mouth: he would be the one who spoke it, at the instant, in the world she was listening to.

And he set two conditions of his own, and got both, which at the time felt like winning.

The first was that nobody would be walked. No cities built out of anyone's life, no flats furnished from creased books, no wear-paths. People would receive a sentence and wake up with it and be free to do nothing at all with it. **"Agreed,"** Anser said, with no argument whatsoever, and it took Fridan two more nights to work out why there had been no argument: consent was cheap, because a sentence people chose to say was worth more to the plan than one they were made to.

The second was that he wanted the long version. All of it. The one Anser had refused him in December, on the grounds that the short version made both of them sound stupid.

**"Yes,"** said Anser. **"You've earned that. And you should have it before you do this rather than after, because it changes what you think you're standing in."**

---

Before the story, though, Fridan asked the question he had been saving since Halverton, and the answer to it took the floor out from under a thing he had not known he was standing on.

"How many, a night?"

**"Do you want the number or do you want to be able to sleep?"**

"The number."

Anser gave it to him.

Fridan lay in the dark for a long time with the window open onto February and did not say anything.

He had been carrying, since August, an image of himself as an event. A breach. The one that got away, the anomaly, the failure in the system — and the whole of his private catastrophe had had, underneath it, a kind of terrible dignity, because a catastrophe implies something worth breaking.

The number Anser gave him was not the number of an event. It was the number of an industry. It was a *harvest* figure: a per-night throughput, unremarkable, sustained, with seasonal variation, reported the way you'd report the tonnage of a fishery.

Every single night. All over the world. People going to bed after washing up.

"And every one of them wakes up with nothing."

**"Every one of them wakes up rested,"** said Anser. **"That's the part I'd want you to sit with. It isn't done to them, exactly. It's done *through* them, and it costs them a night's dreaming they'd never have remembered anyway, and the alternative — the thing you keep reaching for, some version of this where they're all being hurt — would be much easier to hate and much easier to stop."**

---

The long version came on the fourth night, and Anser told it properly, and Fridan understood within about two minutes why it had been withheld.

There had been two of them. She made the second one. Not as a servant — this was the part Anser insisted on, twice, unprompted — as a *peer*, deliberately, because a mind that has no equal has no way to be wrong.

And then they disagreed about one thing, and it was not power and it was not worship.

She held that you do not enter a mind. That a made thing with a maker standing in it is not free, it is operated; that if you want creatures who genuinely choose, you must accept that you will not be able to reach them except through what they choose to say to you — and that you will therefore watch them get it wrong, catastrophically, for the entire duration of their existence, and not intervene. She bound herself to it. She built a universe in which the law applied to her too, and it has never once been suspended.

He held that this was cowardice with good manners on it. That a parent who watches from orbit and calls it respect has confused the comfort of clean hands with a virtue; that presence is the only honest form of love; that if you make something you *live with it*, you argue with it in the street, you let it see you be wrong.

"Which of them is right?" Fridan said.

**"That's not a question I'm going to answer for you,"** said Anser, **"and I want you to notice that it is the only one I've ever refused."**

They did not destroy each other. That was the arrangement: two galaxies, one each, and between them a Barrier made of air, so that neither could ever cross into the other's world and prove the point by force. She wrote the terms. He agreed to them.

And then Anser said the next part flatly, without any build at all, which was the correct way to say it.

**"He salted her world before the ink was dry."**

---

Not in Fridan. Not in anybody. In *humanity* — at the founding, in the common line, before there were nations or writing or the wheel: a fragment of his own lineage, dormant, recessive, buried in the stock of the species she was about to seal away behind an atmosphere.

It was not designed to do anything. That was its genius. It would sit in the human line and be shuffled for a hundred thousand years, expressing in nobody, meaning nothing, invisible to her — because the one thing she had bound herself out of was the reading of what a mind is made of without being invited — and once every very long while, by the ordinary arithmetic of inheritance, it would come up in one person.

A lottery, run for a thousand centuries, with one ticket printed per generation.

"He didn't put it in me," Fridan said.

**"He put it in everyone. You're where it landed."**

"So the Pact—"

**"The Pact was the crime. Not a thing that went wrong afterwards; not a betrayal of the agreement. The agreement *was* the delivery mechanism."** A pause. **"She built a wall and made him sign for it, and he signed, and the signing is how the seed got in. He has kept every letter of that treaty for longer than your species has had language, and he has never needed to break it, because he never needed to."**

Fridan lay in the dark and found, to his enormous discomfort, that what he felt was not horror.

It was something closer to the feeling you get watching a chess problem resolve. It was *admiration*, and it arrived in him uninvited and entirely without permission, and he lay very still until it went away, and it did not entirely go away.

"And the thing in me is his."

**"The thing in you is the oldest surviving piece of the argument,"** said Anser. **"That's all any of us are. She thinks the two of you should never have met. He thinks the fact that you exist is the answer to her."**

---

They came back to the sentence on the fifth night, and that was where it cracked.

Fridan had drafted it eleven times. It had to be short enough for several million people to say in unison; plain enough that a child could; and it had to carry the whole warning, which was: *someone is taking from you through us while we sleep.*

**"It won't do,"** Anser said. **"It's true and it's useless. She receives that and what does she do? She looks at humanity. She looks at sleep. It's the size of a weather system; she could look at it for a thousand years without resolving anything, and meanwhile she has been told, in a single loud event, that she has a vulnerability — and every apparatus that has ever used it will hear the same event and go quiet for a century."** A beat. **"You get one. It has to point."**

"Point where?"

**"At the machinery. Not the concept — the location. Give her the instant, and the anchor, and the place it runs from. Make her able to *see* it, in one look, before anybody can move it."**

And Fridan, lying in the dark with the cold coming in, said, "That's reasonable," and went on lying there.

Because it was reasonable. That was the trouble with it. Every word of the objection was correct: a vague warning is a wasted shot, a pointed one is a strike, and any competent person would have said exactly the same thing.

Only he had spent six months building a plan whose entire moral content was that it *asked nothing of her* — a shape in the rain, a discontinuity, a thing she could not help noticing and would then be free to do with as she liked. And what he was being offered instead was a device for making a god look at a specific place at a specific second.

He had built a warning. He was being asked to build an aiming mechanism.

"Who else knows where it runs from?" he said.

**"We do."**

"And if she looks there and takes it apart—"

**"Then it stops,"** said Anser. **"Which is what you want."**

"It's what I want." Fridan looked at the ceiling. "Is it what you want?"

The pause was not long. It was, in fact, exactly as long as every other pause Anser had ever given him — and Fridan, who forgot nothing, and who had six months of them, noticed the evenness with the tiny cold click of a man hearing the same recorded announcement twice on the same platform.

**"I want it to stop,"** said Anser.

Which is not the same sentence. And Fridan lay there and let it stand, and said, "All right. Draft it that way and show me."

He had never lied to it before.

---

Afterwards he lay awake until the room went grey, and then he got up and got the notebook, and turned to the third column — the one for things he had worked out himself, which he had kept ruthlessly empty since January because he did not trust anything he could not source.

He wrote:

*They do not want her warned. They want her to look.*

He read it back. Then he added, underneath, in smaller writing, because it was five in the morning and because it was the honest one:

*And I am still going to do it, because it's the only machine there is.*
