For nine days after that he did not do very much at all.

He went to work and produced a passable imitation of a man doing his job. He ate when it occurred to him. He sat in the evenings in the chair by the open window with a blanket over him and the cold coming in, and turned the same fact over and over the way you turn a stone in your pocket until it has no surface left.

She had known.

Not *found out.* Known — for the whole of him, presumably; for the boy of nine on a bicycle and the man of twenty-six with his first flat and the whole ordinary catalogue of a life she had apparently been able to look at any time she liked. There had been a piece of her brother sitting in the middle of a man in Nor Yesey, and she had seen it, and she had done nothing about it, and then one night in August something had walked into that man's sleep and used the fragment as a doorknob.

**"You keep saying she let it happen,"** Anser observed, on the sixth evening. **"I want to be careful with you here, because it would be easy for me to agree and it wouldn't be honest. I don't think she knew what was being done. I think she knew what you were and thought it was nothing — a fragment, inert, a smudge left over from an old quarrel. I think it never occurred to her that anybody could build a use for it, because she has never once in her existence had to think like something that is locked out."**

"That's worse."

**"It might be. It's certainly worse for her."**

Fridan sat with the blanket and the cold.

He noticed — dimly, at the very back, the way you notice a draught — that every road Anser walked him down ended in the same place, and that the place was always *she is not what you thought and I am here.* He noticed it and set it down, because it was the ninth day and he had run out of the strength required to suspect anybody.

---

On the tenth day he decided to speak to her.

He was clear with himself about the reasons, and he made himself write them down, in the notebook, in two columns, because he had learned that much:

*Because she needs to know. Something is using her, every night, on an industrial scale, through people who wake up with nothing, and she does not know, and I am the only person alive who can tell her.*

That was the left-hand column and every word of it was true.

The right-hand column said: *Because I want her to answer me.*

He looked at the two columns for a long time. Then he closed the notebook and went to find the keys to the roof, because he had discovered by then that a true reason and a real reason can sit side by side in a man perfectly comfortably, and that the second one is the one that moves his legs.

Anser had told him not to. That was the whole of it, plainly stated on the first night and twice since: *say nothing, not aloud, not to anyone, she hears the words.*

He was going to say the words.

---

The roof of his building was flat, with gravel on it, and a low parapet, and the hum of the extract fans in a housing at the north end. He went up at ten past five in the morning with a coat on and no plan except the order of the sentences.

The city was doing its early business. The parking structure had four cars in it. There was one lit window in the block opposite and a man in it moving about a kitchen.

Fridan stood at the parapet in the wind and put his hands on it, the way he had put them on a handrail over four tracks in a cutting in a city with no name, and he said, out loud, in a clear voice:

"I don't know your name. I've never known it. I'm going to say this anyway because you can hear it, and I've been told you can, and I've spent four months finding out that's true."

The wind took it. Nothing came back.

"In August something used me. It came in while I was asleep and it built a place for me and walked me through it, and it did that because there's a piece of something in me that isn't from you. And the thing I picked up at the end of it wasn't a prize. It was a machine and it was pointed at *you*." He was steady. He was surprised at how steady. "They aren't after me. They've never been after me. They're getting something out of you, a night at a time, out of people who wake up with nothing — and you can't see it, because the only place you look properly is the one place they've worked out how to stand."

The fans hummed.

"There've been others. Hundreds, maybe. There's a woman in Halverton with a note in her own handwriting she's kept for nineteen years and she doesn't know why—"

And she was there.

---

There was no arrival. He would say that afterwards to the only person who ever asked him properly, and he would say it badly, because the language available is all built for things that come from somewhere: she was not present and then she was present, at the same size as everything, the way weight is present in a bridge.

Warm. That was the first thing, still, after four months and everything he had learned. Not kind. *Warm*, the way a room is warm, not doing it at him, simply its condition — and something in Fridan's chest broke open at it with an eagerness so total and so undignified that he was ashamed of it before it had finished happening.

He had one second of it.

Then she looked at him.

It was not the same as being spoken to and he had no preparation for it whatsoever. It was not attention in the human sense, an eye turned, a face lifted — it was more like being a page in good light. He was open. Not violated, not searched, nothing rummaged: simply *legible*, all at once, all of him, the nine-year-old on the bicycle and the fortnight in November and the flat grey shortage that food did not touch and the four stitches on a cuff and the two columns in a notebook, the honest one and the real one, both of them, read together without emphasis.

He stood on a roof in the wind with his hands on the parapet and let it happen, because there was no mechanism in a human body for doing anything else, and because — this is the part he never told anybody — for about two seconds it was the best thing that had ever happened to him. To be entirely known. To have every single thing about him held in something's regard at once, including the worst of it, and not to have to *say* any of it.

Then the reading arrived at the place in him that was small, and old, and quiet.

And she stopped.

---

He felt the stop. It had a texture. It was the sensation of a very large thing arriving at data it has no compartment for — not shock, nothing so animal; a halt, the way a wheel halts on a stone.

And then something else, moving through and past him: the nearest thing to an emotion he ever detected in her, and he spent years failing to name it and settled in the end for the closest available word, which was *grief*, and which he knew was wrong the whole time.

The wind came across the roof.

She said: **"You are not the one I was speaking to."**

That was all.

He got out one syllable — not a word, a sound, the beginning of *wait* — and she was gone, and the going was not like the silence.

The silence had been absence: a room with nobody in it, which is a thing that can end. This was a door being closed. He felt it close. He felt the specific, deliberate, unhurried *shutting* of something that had been open his entire life without his ever knowing it was there, and the roof he was standing on afterwards was a different roof, in a world he had never actually been in before, and would now be in permanently.

---

He stayed up there until it got light.

At some point he shouted. He was aware of doing it and made no attempt to stop, and what he shouted was not dignified and was mostly her, or the absence where a name for her should have been, and one whole ugly minute of *I was trying to tell you* — and the wind took all of it, and it went nowhere, and he understood that it went nowhere in a completely new way, because for four months his words had been going somewhere and being ignored, and now there was simply no channel for them to travel in. He was shouting into physics.

He sat down eventually with his back against the fan housing.

The city came on: lights, the first buses, the man in the kitchen opposite gone now, a plane going over very high with the sun on it before there was any sun on the ground.

And Fridan, who forgot nothing, went back over the last ten minutes with the flat total availability that had been installed in him in August, and found the thing at the bottom of it.

She had come at the first sentence.

Not the third, where the substance was. Not at *they're getting something out of you.* At the first — at *I don't know your name* — she had already been there; the arriving had taken no time at all, less than a breath, faster than a phone is answered by someone sitting beside it.

She had been right there.

For four months he had talked to kitchens and ceilings and the inside of a shower and up onto roofs at five in the morning, and every single word had landed, and she had been near enough the whole time to answer inside a second, and had not.

He put his head back against the housing and laughed once, and it came out wrong.

---

Anser came that night, and Fridan let it come.

**"You spoke."**

"Yes."

**"On a roof, I imagine."**

"Yes."

A pause. Not one of hers.

**"Then that's done,"** Anser said, and there was nothing at all in it — no reproach, no I-told-you, none of the small satisfactions a person is entitled to when they turn out to have been right. **"You'll want to sleep. Not tonight; you won't manage it. In a few days. Tell me when you're ready and we'll start on what's actually in front of you."**

Fridan lay in the dark with his mouth shut and waited.

He waited about eleven seconds, and then he understood what he was waiting for, and that it was not going to come.

Anser had not asked what she said.

Four months of a thing that answered every question the instant it was asked, that had never once been incurious, that had opened its first conversation with *ask in whatever order you like* — and a god had appeared to a man on a roof and spoken to him for the only time in his life, and it had not asked what she said.

*File it,* Fridan thought, very carefully, in the part of him that could not be heard by anything. *Don't turn it over. Don't look at it. File it and go on.*

"Give me a week," he said aloud.

**"A week,"** Anser agreed.

---

He lay awake until the room went grey, working the problem, because working the problem was the only structure left standing.

She could hear what was spoken. That had been true on a footbridge in August and it had been true on a roof that morning, and it was the only law of the universe he had ever verified personally.

She could not hear him now. Not the words, not the shouting, not anything: he had felt the mechanism close and he did not need it explained to him.

So there was a message that had to reach her — the only message that mattered, the one that would let her see the hole in the floor of her own creation — and it had to be *said*, out loud, because that is the only kind of thing she gets.

And the one man alive who knew what it was had just had his mouth taken away.

Fridan lay in his bed in Nor Yesey with the window open onto the cold and put it to himself in the plainest words he could find, which is how he put everything to himself from that morning onward:

*How do you say something to a god who has stopped being able to hear you?*
